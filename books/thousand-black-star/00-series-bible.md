# THOUSAND BLACK STAR — Story Bible

*A novel in sixty chapters. One thousand souls. Sixty love stories.*

---

## 1. The Premise

Astronomers speak of a **black star**: a sun whose light has gone out, yet whose pull still bends everything near it. You cannot see it. You can only see what *moves* because of it.

In this novel, a black star is **the thing a person never said aloud** — the love, the apology, the confession kept behind the teeth. Everyone carries one.

For forty-five years an astronomer named **Seraphine Wyndham-Rao** (born in Colombo, died in Cornwall) kept a private ledger called **The Register of Black Stars**. Anyone in the world could write her one letter, confessing the thing they had never said. She promised to keep each letter, never to name the writer, and to number them in the order they arrived. She stopped at one thousand.

On the night she dies, her grand-niece **Nadeesha Fernando** opens the Register to the public online. For the first time the thousand letters are readable, searchable, and answerable. Strangers read each other's unsaid words. Some answer. Some are found. Some run.

Over one year — from one new-moon night in November to the next — **sixty love stories** play out across forty-odd countries. Some end in marriage. Some in graves. Some break off mid-sentence, because the paper was torn. Some stay open, because love can be patient across an ocean.

## 2. Title Meaning

*Thousand Black Star* — the thousand unseen stars of the heart. Seraphine's own letter is **Entry 1000**, the last, never posted, and it is the last page of the book.

## 3. The Frame Device (Shakespearean)

- **The Chorus.** Each Act opens with a fourteen-line sonnet spoken by the Chorus (the voice of the Keeper), and the book closes with an epilogue in rhymed couplets.
- **Margin Notes.** After certain chapters the Keeper adds a short note in the margin of the Register ("Entry 0713: …"). These carry dramatic irony and foreshadowing.
- **The Torn Page.** When a love story is *unfinished*, the narration stops mid-sentence, mid-scene, inside the chapter, followed by one italic line: *Here the page is torn.* No explanation. The reader must live with it.
- **Register Numbers.** Every one of the thousand characters has a number (0001–1000). Numbers are never reused.

| Act | Chapters | Season | Shakespearean Engine |

## 6. The Central Open-Ending Story — Fayaz & Ermis

**Fayaz Rahman**, 26, Dhaka, Bangladesh — **Register No. 0713**.
**Ermis Larasati**, 24, Yogyakarta, Indonesia — **Register No. 0714**.

Their entries were posted on the same night, so they sit side by side in the Register. She answers his.

- They like each other. They never say the word *love* outright, until the very end, and then only as a hint.
- **Ermis** has a design fellowship in Rotterdam. She must focus on her career. She chooses the work, and it costs her.
- **Fayaz** is stuck: his father's debts, his mother's health, his sister's schooling, a failed startup, two refused visas. He cannot rise yet.
- They meet **only online**. Dhaka is UTC+6, Yogyakarta UTC+7: **she lives one hour ahead of him**. Her line, in Chapter 30: *"I am an hour in your future. I have already seen it. You were kind."* This is the book's central hint that they will be together.
- Their story recurs in Ch. 1, 6, 14, 22, **30 (full chapter)**, 44, 47, 58, and the Epilogue.
- It stays **open**: no wedding, no goodbye. Last image: *the typing dots*.

## 7. Content Rules (from the author)

1. Exactly **1,000 characters** with Register numbers; exactly **60 love stories**; **60 chapters**.
2. **No LGBTQ content.** All romances are between a man and a woman.
3. Some love stories are **unfinished mid-chapter** (11, listed).
4. Romance stays tender and chaste enough for a general KDP audience; no explicit scenes. Faith, family, and cultural customs are shown with respect.
5. Every character, place name pairing, and event is fictional. Any resemblance to real persons is coincidental.

## 8. Style Guide — Writing in the Manner of Shakespeare

This is *in the manner of*, not imitation of Elizabethan spelling. A modern reader on Kindle must be able to read it fast and feel the music.

**Do**
- Open each Act with a **sonnet** (iambic pentameter, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG). The Prologue is already written.
- Use **soliloquy**: a character alone, speaking the truth to the night, in heightened prose or blank verse.
- Use **paired scenes**: one lofty, one low (lovers in the garden, then the fool, the cook, the taxi driver, commenting on them in plain speech).
- Use **wordplay and puns**, especially on *light/dark*, *star/stare*, *post/post*, *hour/our*.
- Use **extended metaphor** drawn from each character's trade (a baker's love is bread; a pianist's is rest between notes).
- Use **dramatic irony**: the reader knows what the letter says before the lover does.
- Include **fools**: every Act has two comic characters who say the wise thing by accident.
- Use **letters, oaths, and overheard speech** as plot engines.
- Use direct address occasionally: *"Mark this, reader."*

**Do not**
- Do not quote or paraphrase actual lines of Shakespeare's plays.
- Do not use "thee/thou" constantly. Reserve it for the Chorus, for oaths, and for the Keeper.
- Do not let dialogue become a pastiche; characters must sound like their own trade and country.

**Verse vs. prose**
- Verse (blank or rhymed) for: Chorus, prologues, vows, curses, lovers at their most naked.
- Prose for: everyday scenes, comedy, technology, messages.
- Texts and chats appear in set-off blocks. The medium is part of the poetry.

## 9. Themes

- What we do not say shapes the world more than what we do.
- Distance is a kind of lens.
- Ambition and love are not enemies, but they keep different hours.
- Time zones as fate; an hour is a long way.
- Grief and courage often share a doorway.
