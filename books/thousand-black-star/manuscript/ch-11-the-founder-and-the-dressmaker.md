## Chapter 11
### The Founder and the Dressmaker

> *"I only design clothes for the woman I am afraid to become."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0954

---

### SCENE I
*Yaba, Lagos, Nigeria. Adeniran Court, a five-storey building that was once a bank, then a church, and is now a cluster of ambitions with a shared generator. Six in the morning. The generator has begun.*

In Lagos, the sun does not rise. It is *switched on*, by a large and unseen hand, at an hour when everybody has already been awake for three hours, and the first sound of the day is not a bird but a generator.

It is a sound of enormous character. It is a cough, a clearing of the throat, a long grinding grievance, and then a steady and defiant drone, as of a man determined to have the last word in an argument that began in nineteen-eighty. Under the drone, in a city of twenty million, the danfo minibuses begin to hoot, the *okada* motorbikes weave like needles through the go-slow, a woman at the corner begins to fry *puff-puff* in a pan the size of a cartwheel, and from the Third Mainland Bridge there rises the roar of a metropolis that has never in its history been asked to wait.

On the fourth floor of Adeniran Court, in an office with a whiteboard, nine desks, and a very handsome neon sign that said **AJO**, a young man sat alone before a laptop, and read the same bank notification for the eleventh time.

*Your balance is insufficient for the scheduled transfer.*

"It is not *insufficient*," said Emeka Okafor, aloud, to nobody. "It is *shy.*"

He was twenty-eight, the founder of a company named for the *ajo*, the old Yoruba savings circle in which a dozen neighbours each put a little money in a pot every week and take the whole pot in turn, so that a market woman can buy a freezer and a tailor can buy a machine, with no bank and no interest and nothing at all but trust. He had built a phone application to do the same thing on a grand scale. For two years it had been the most promising thing in the building. It had been featured in a magazine. It had won a prize in Nairobi, and a lunch in London, and the heart of an investor who had said, with tears in his eyes, that Emeka was *going to change Africa.*

And then, in March, the investor had changed *his mind.*

It had gone quickly, as a good building goes: first the funding, then the clients, then the app store ranking, and finally, in the quiet of a Thursday, the last of the sense that anyone would ever want to talk to him about it again. The company was dead. It was simply that nobody had told its heart.

He had nine employees. He paid them, on the twenty-fifth of each month, from his own account: from his savings, from the sale of a good watch, from a loan against a flat in Surulere, from the last of his father's gift. He had not told them. He had not told his sister. He had not told the neon sign, which went on every morning with its hopeful buzz, and which he had begun to hate.

*My company is dead,* he had written to an old woman in England, on a night in which the generator had failed, *and I keep paying salaries from my own pocket.*

He had meant it as a confession. He had not realised it was also a budget.

### SCENE II
*Mama Put Joint, behind the building. Noon. A tin roof, long benches, and enormous steaming pots. A woman of sixty in a sailor's cap is eating* amala *with the manner of a captain taking a ship.*

"You are looking," said Ifeoma Bello, "like a man who has swallowed an anchor."

She was sixty, a tiny, wiry woman from Ibadan, who had gone to sea at nineteen as the only woman on a Lagos-registered freighter, and had spent thirty-one years on the oceans of the world, rising to first mate and acquiring a vocabulary that could blister a bulkhead. She had retired with a pension, a parrot-handled cane, and a very large number of opinions, and she now invested small sums in the young, on the condition that they take her to lunch.

"I am well, Auntie."

"You are lying. It is the first thing a young man learns, and the last he learns to do *well*." She tore a morsel of *amala*, dipped it in *ewedu* and gbegiri, and chewed with great deliberation. "What is the number?"

"Which number?"

"The number you are not saying."

He looked at his plate. "Four million, one hundred thousand naira. Outstanding. After the payroll."

"And the company?"

"It has—" He stopped. "Auntie, it has three customers. They are all my cousins."

"Hm." Ifeoma Bello put down her morsel, and wiped her fingers on a napkin with an air of solemn purpose. "I will tell you a thing from the sea. There is a story of the captain who goes down with his ship. It is a very fine story and you will find it in every poem. In thirty-one years, young man, I met three captains who went down with their ships. They were all *very* dead." She raised an eyebrow. "The captain who lives is the one who writes the lifeboat list on the *first* night of the leak, and lets his crew read it."

"They will blame me."

"Naturally. They will blame you for a week. And then they will remember that you paid them for nine months from your own pocket, and they will blame you for being a fool, which is a different thing, and keeps better."

"How do you know I—"

"Emeka," said Ifeoma gently, "you have sold your watch. You have a pale line on your wrist like a very sad bracelet."

He looked at his wrist. He put his hand under the table.

"Pride," said the old sailor, "is a heavy ballast. A ship needs some. Not so much that it cannot rise." She pushed the pot of *ewedu* towards him. "Eat. And go and look at the third floor. I am told that there is a young woman there who is making something you could learn from."

"A woman?"

"A *dress*," said Ifeoma Bello, with great dignity, "is also a company. It also dies if nobody pays. Go."

---

### SCENE III
*The third floor. A workroom, gold with afternoon. Bolts of cloth in every colour of the earth: adire in indigo, aso-oke in rust and wheat, ankara in a hundred voices. A long table. A mannequin in the corner.*

Now we must make the acquaintance of a young woman who lives in her own opera.

Chidinma Eze was twenty-seven, a fashion designer from Enugu, who had come to Lagos at twenty with a suitcase, a sewing machine, and a conviction that the whole city was a stage on which she had been unaccountably cast as the maid. She was small, quick, and brilliant, with a short crop of black hair, a mouth that could convey eleven emotions per sentence, and a way of entering a room as though she had been announced. She wore her own designs always: that day a gold-and-indigo wrap with sleeves like a pair of wings, and a headscarf that made her look like the figurehead of a very expensive ship.

She was at that moment kneeling at the feet of a mannequin, with a mouth full of pins.

"No," she was saying, round them. "*No.* The shoulder must be sharper. Sharper, my dear. You are a woman who walks into a boardroom and *causes it to be silent.* You do not have a *gentle* shoulder."

The mannequin did not argue.

It stood in the corner, headless, in a jacket of heavy indigo adire with hand-pleated sleeves, and beside it, on a hook, hung eight garments in different states of completion. It was the strangest thing in the studio, and the most beautiful, and Chidinma, who confided in it as other women confide in their mothers, called it *Her.*

"Excuse me," said a voice from the door. "I am told that the sea requires me to go and look at a dress."

She did not turn. "Who is there?"

"Emeka Okafor. Fourth floor. The, ah, *Ajo* people."

"The neon people."

"The neon people."

"You are the ones who have been stealing my diesel."

"We pay our levy."

"You have *not* paid your levy for two months. The generator committee has a list. You are number three. I am number one, because I am saintly." She took the pins from her mouth and turned, at last, on her knees, and looked up at the tall, tired, handsome, creased young man in the doorway, with a lock of hair falling into his eyes and a pale line on his wrist. And, because she was a designer, she saw the whole of him in a glance, as a tailor does: the slope of the shoulder, the tension in the jaw, the shirt that had been ironed with great care and a failing hope.

*That man,* she thought, *has not slept in a month.*

"I will pay the levy," said Emeka.

"You will not," said Chidinma. "I paid it. For both of us."

There was a short and terrible silence.

"I did not ask you to."

"No. You did not ask anyone for anything. It is a *remarkable* condition." She stood and brushed off her knees with a theatrical flourish. "Do not worry. It is four thousand naira. I charged it to the ministry of nobody."

"Why?"

"Because the generator is a *commons,*" said Chidinma, "and the whole of the building is a *tragedy* of it, and I am an actress at heart. If I see a man with a grave face and a fine shirt and a company that is making the sound of a deflating tyre, I do what any decent woman does. I pay his diesel and say nothing, and then I wait for the opening to say it *very* loudly." She smiled. "Welcome to the third floor."

He looked at the mannequin.

"Who is she?"

"Nobody." The smile went out. "It is a dress form."

"It has a name."

"Everything has a name." She went rather pink. "Do not mind it."

He came into the room and stood before the jacket, looking at it in silence for a long time, with his hands in his pockets, as one looks at a diagram one does not understand but cannot bear to leave. It was the most extraordinary garment he had ever seen. It was powerful. It had a collar like a drawn bow. It looked like the coat of a woman who had decided, at last, to be *looked at.*

"It is not a *design,*" said Emeka. "It is an *argument.*"

Chidinma looked at him for a moment in a manner she had not looked at anyone for a long time.

"Yes," she said, very quietly. "That is exactly what it is."

---

*In her workroom, that night, with the machines asleep, she speaks to the empty room, and to the headless guest, in the only manner a designer can trust; which is to say, in the language of the thing she cannot afford to say in daylight.*

> *I have been sewing thee these seven years,
> My silent lady of the sharp-cut sleeve.
> I never gave thee eyes, for fear of finding
> Thou lookedst somehow like myself. Thou art
> The woman I am frightened of: unbowed,
> Unasking, tall, and not apologetic;
> The woman who has never said, "I'm sorry
> To take up room." I dress thee every season.
> I say, "She is a fiction. She is cloth."
> And then I stand before thee, in the dark,
> And measure: here the shoulder; there the waist;
> And find that every inch of thee is mine.
> It is a mortal thing to make a woman
> Whom thou wouldst love, and tremble to become.*

She stood in the dim, with a pin in her hand, until the generator coughed and went out. Then she laughed, once, at herself, and said, "You see, *Her*, this is the problem with being theatrical. The cure is worse than the complaint."

---

### SCENE IV
*The next evening. The lobby of Adeniran Court. The light has not merely gone: it has been taken by the State, with a smile, and without notice, and forty people stand in the dark with their phones lit, a congregation in a cathedral of small blue candles.*

It was the night of the show.

It had been meant to take place in a great ballroom on Victoria Island, with a runway, a sponsor, and a DJ. Eight weeks ago the venue had been booked. Three weeks ago the sponsor had withdrawn. A week ago the venue had demanded a deposit of two million, three hundred thousand naira, which Chidinma had not got and could not borrow. And yesterday she had called her father in Enugu, a retired tailor, a devout and silver-haired man with a stern face and a very tender conscience, who had said, over a faint line, in the voice of a man who has said the thing many times: "Nnem, a coat is a coat, whether it is hung in a palace or a lobby. The woman who wears it will not ask."

So the show was in the lobby.

The tenants of Adeniran Court had made themselves its audience: the lawyers from the second floor, the three young men who ran an app for hailing motorcycles, the woman with the dental practice, a pack of children from the compound, Mama Ngozi from the stairwell. There were plastic chairs and a runway of ankara cloth laid down the length of the tile. There were eight models, all friends, a single working speaker, and a cousin of Chidinma's in a bow tie with a laptop; and in the middle of an enormous silence, with great ceremony, the power failed.

"*Abeg,*" said somebody in the audience. "Not again."

"It is a *design feature,*" said somebody else.

Chidinma, in the wings, in gold, closed her eyes.

"Chineke," she whispered. "It is a joke. It is a very bad joke, and God is the comedian."

"Madam?"

She opened them. On the stair, with the lights of eleven phones behind him like a very small constellation, stood Emeka Okafor in his ironed shirt, flanked by a short, powerful young woman in coveralls with a spanner in her belt and a face of divine patience.

"This is Ngozi," said Emeka. "She is a mechanic."

"I am her *best friend,*" said Ngozi Afolabi, who was thirty-three and had been, for six years, the sole person on earth to whom Chidinma Eze did not play. "And I have been downstairs for twenty minutes, and your generator has the soul of a man who has been told it is Monday. It needs a *belt.*"

"We do not have a belt!"

"*I* have a belt," said Emeka, "and it is in the car, and I will go and fetch it, and I will also bring the cables from the office, and the nine laptops, which will be the light." He paused. "It is a thing we know about. We are a company that has been running on very little for some time."

Chidinma stared at him.

"You are a founder," said Ngozi Afolabi, wiping a spanner on her coverall, "and a man of *infrastructure.* This is a rare thing. Do not waste him."

"I am not wasting—" said Chidinma. "I am only—"

"Go on," said Emeka, quietly. "Get ready. It will be lit."

*It was.*

For in fifteen minutes the nine desks of AJO had been emptied, and nine staff members, whom their employer had supposed to be ignorant of the entire state of his finances, filed down the stair in a solemn procession, each carrying a laptop, an extension cable, and an expression of great composure. Tobi, the youngest, a twenty-four-year-old with spectacles and a ferocious talent for code, set down his machine on the lobby floor and opened it with a click. It lit a small circle of tile in a pale electric blue. Beside it, another. And another.

They arranged the nine screens in a long line along the sides of the ankara runway, like the lamps along a harbour. Each of them, on a white background, burned a single word, which Tobi had typed in thirty seconds, with the dry humour of the very young.

*WELCOME.*

*ENTER.*

*PAY.*

*(Support available.)*

"Oga," said Tobi, to his employer, as the crowd laughed and the light settled over the tile like warm water. "We have been reading your bank alerts on the office printer for two months. We thought you would tell us when you were ready."

Emeka did not speak.

"We are not stupid, oga," said Tobi, very gently. "We are *nine.* We have been quietly agreeing, in the toilet, that we shall keep coming to work until the building falls."

---

The show began in the glow of nine screens, and it was the best thing that had ever happened in Adeniran Court.

The first model came down the cloth in a skirt of golden aso-oke that moved like a lit bell. The second, in an adire wrap the colour of the deep Atlantic. The third, the fourth, the fifth: coats and gowns and caftans cut from the old loom-cloths of three peoples, remade as armour, as laughter, as a woman who knows her price. The audience went quiet; then, as the sleeves went by, it began to murmur; and by the sixth garment they were on their feet.

And at the back, by the stair, a very old woman in a purple wrapper and white head-tie, who had been standing in the stairwell of the building for eleven years, offering to any who would listen the word of the Lord, said, in a voice that carried: "*I have seen it!*"

"Mama Ngozi?" said Emeka, beside her.

"I saw it in a dream, my son." She was fifty-nine, the leader of a small chapel in Yaba, a woman of true and unwavering faith and no sense of direction whatsoever; and she gripped his sleeve with a hand like a bird's. "I saw that a man in this building would lose everything on a Thursday and gain it on a Friday. I did not know what it meant. I thought it was the stock market."

"It is *Wednesday,* Mama."

"Then," said Mama Ngozi, with perfect serenity, "I am early." She looked at the runway, and the lit faces, and the nine screens glimmering, and the girl in gold at the end of the cloth with her hands clasped before her in a posture that no one had ever seen on her. "But I do not think the Lord *minds.*"

And there was a hush, because the last model had not come out.

---

She had not come.

There was a gap. The whole room felt it. There was a silent beat at the end of the runway, where the eighth garment should have come: the jacket, the indigo, the sharp-shouldered one, the armoured one that cost her eleven weeks. Beneath it in the dark, with a hand on her mouth, stood a young woman in gold who had expected to see it lifted down from its hook by a friend who had that morning been called away to a funeral in Anambra.

"Where is *Her?*" whispered Chidinma. "Ngozi, where is the eighth?"

"The model—her train—"

"She is not coming?"

"She will not come."

She looked at the jacket on its hook. She looked at the dark. She looked, for the first time in seven years, directly at the woman she had been making.

*It is a jacket. It is cloth.*

*It is not cloth.*

She took it down.

*Mark this, reader.* A person does not walk into the thing she fears with a flourish. She walks into it with one foot and a bad heartbeat, in the wrong shoes. Chidinma Eze put on the coat in the dark, and it fitted as if it had been cut against her bones, and she stepped onto the ankara cloth between two lines of glowing screens, with forty people in the dark before her, and the whole tall frightening woman she had stitched, shoulder by shoulder, for seven years, walked into the light.

No one spoke.

At the end of the runway, in the nine small blue lights, a young man in an ironed shirt stood up out of his chair, without knowing that he had, with his hands at his sides and a pale line on his wrist, and thought, with absolute clarity, in the middle of the end of his company:

*I know that woman. I will do anything not to lose her.*

And then, from somewhere at the back, the generator, with a long shuddering gasp, caught.

The lights came on.

The people of Lagos will applaud a thing for any reason at all, but a thing that is *true* they applaud as though it were a goal.
