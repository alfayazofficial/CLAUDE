## Chapter 36
### Cycling Through Cloud

> *"I crashed because I was thinking of you."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0586

---

**Antioquia, Colombia. Stage Six of the Vuelta. A quarter to eight in the morning, on the climb of Las Palmas, inside a cloud.**

In the Colombian Andes the cloud does not drift; it sits down on the mountain like a hen on an egg, and stays until it has been sufficiently admired.

On this particular morning it had been sitting for six hours, and the road up from Medellín, which climbs a thousand metres in eleven kilometres by way of hairpins and prayer, had been swallowed to the knee. Along its edges the people of the mountain stood in their ponchos with transistor radios held to their ears. They sold empanadas and sweet *aguapanela* from steaming pots. They shouted the names of men they could not see. And over every radio, from a motorbike somewhere in the white, boomed the voice of the most beloved commentator in the south-west of the Republic, a gentle, nervous, cardiganed man from Cali named Esteban Ortiz, who became, the moment a microphone came near, a thunderstorm.

*"Amigos del ciclismo, GOOD MORNING! Nobody can see anything, which is the finest thing that can happen to a radio man! I am inside a cloud, I am inside a legend! Here come the leaders, I think. I hear them. It sounds like a swarm of very expensive bees! And behind them, forty seconds behind, comes the story of the day, ladies and gentlemen: Sebastián Torres of Bogotá! Eleven months ago, a crash on the descent at Letras. A collarbone, a hip, ten months of hospital and rehabilitation. And today, the comeback! I have lost a bet on this man four times, and I would lose it again for the pleasure!"*

The man in question was at that moment climbing at the speed of a determined snail, in a group of six, with the white wall closing before his front wheel and his own breath loud in his ears. Sebastián Torres, twenty-nine, professional cyclist, stubborn by trade and humorous by reflex, could not see his handlebar. He could see a foot of grey tarmac, and a rival's rear tyre, and now and then a yellow leg like a drowned man's.

*It is a strange thing,* he thought, *to ride for a year toward a day, and have it arrive in a cloud.*

He knew the road. He had ridden it a hundred times in training. And he knew what lay a kilometre ahead, behind a green gate on the left, where a hillside of glass had been hung out of the cloud like washing.

A year before, he had punctured at that gate, on a Tuesday, cursing in two registers; and a young woman in rubber boots had come out of a greenhouse with a roll of tape, a spoon, and a face like an indignant sunrise. *You climb,* she had said, kneeling to his wheel, *like a man running away from something.* *I climb,* he had said, *like a man who is late.* She had mended it with a teaspoon and a temper. She had given him a rose for his mother. It was a Tuesday, and nothing had occurred.

Two weeks later, descending a mountain in the Tour of the Andes at eighty kilometres an hour, he had seen, at the corner of his eye, a flower stall, a bucket of red roses, and thought of a teaspoon; and the road had turned, and he had not.

He thought it now, in the white, in the only prose the lungs allow at that gradient, which is verse:

> *White, and a white beyond the white, and breath.
> I cannot see the road. I know the road.
> The legs are maps that need no light to read,
> And I have ridden blind since one small spoon
> Mended one wheel. So let me climb in cloud
> And trust the grade, as some men trust in prayer
> Who never saw the One they pray to. Let
> There be, somewhere on the left, a gate. A boot.*

The cloud thinned.

---

**The same morning, at a green gate on the left.**

The farm was called La Nube: The Cloud. That season it had been living up to its name.

Daniela Ruiz, twenty-eight, flower farmer, bright and proud and out of money, had stood in her greenhouses before dawn, among forty thousand roses that nobody would now buy. A Miami buyer had cancelled by e-mail on Thursday, with a pleasant apology, and the apology had arrived at the precise moment that the letter from the bank had arrived with an unpleasant one. A rose, once cut, keeps for nine days. These had been cut on Wednesday. By Friday they would be a very expensive compost.

"I could *give* them away," she had said at four in the morning, to her foreman, to the radio, to the stripped benches. "Or I could put them on the road."

It was not a plan. A plan has a bottom line. It was the thing that proud people do when they cannot afford to be seen losing, which is to lose magnificently.

Now, with sixty of her workers and every cousin in the *vereda* and a ten-year-old named Paola Ramírez who was supervising with a hand-lettered sign, she laid them out, stem to stem, in a long red ribbon along the grass on the left verge of the road, for eight hundred metres, from her gate to the top of the rise. Forty thousand roses. They lay in the white like a thread of blood on a bandage, and nobody who came up that road, if he looked to his left, could fail to follow it.

She did not know if he would see it. She did not know if he was even riding. She had read **ENTRY 0586** in a cloud of her own, in November, in her kitchen, at three in the morning, and had said nothing to anyone, and had put the phone in the flour jar.

*"I can hear them!"* howled the radio, in the hands of eleven people. *"Here they come! A blur of colour! A tremble in the air! And—wait. Wait. Friends of Colombia, I do not believe it. There is a road of roses on the left. Forty thousand red roses, the whole verge, a carpet! The leaders are riding on the right of the road, they are going past in a kind of astonishment—"*

On the right of the road the leaders went by like a gust. They did not look to the left. Cyclists do not. They are busy, and in pain. Then the second group, and the third.

And then, out of the white, with his head down and his jersey the grey of old ash, came a single rider on the left-hand edge of the tarmac, so close to the verge that his tyres hissed in the petals.

*"It is Torres!"* cried Esteban Ortiz, and his voice cracked in half. *"He has seen it! He is riding the roses! He is following them as a blind man follows a rail! Señoras y señores, a man who crashed because of a flower is climbing through a cloud on a red road. I am a professional. I am not supposed to cry. I am crying."*

She saw him pass. It was a second, no more. His face was streaked and grey and grimly absurd. His eyes went sideways, once, to a girl in rubber boots standing at a gate with her hands clasped under her chin, and held there, as you hold a hot plate you have no intention of putting down. And then he was gone into the white, and the sound of his freewheel faded up the hill.

He did not win. It should be said at once, since the reader is an honest person and will want to know. He came over the summit in eleventh, with a time that Esteban Ortiz described to the Republic, in a voice quite gone, as *the best eleventh place in the entire history of cycling, and I have the figures.*

**Kraków, Poland. The old hall of the Collegium Maius. The same week, as the evening bells began.**

There is a hall in Kraków, up a stair where Copernicus is said to have set his boot, and in it, on this night, three hundred people held their breath for a girl who could not hear them.

Zofia Kowalska, twenty-three, violinist, stood alone in a pool of light, in a blue dress, with her instrument under her chin. She had not heard a note for nine weeks. She had not told her teacher until the day the sound went; she had not told her mother until the day after; and she had told only one other person the whole of it, in a stairwell, in March, in the voice of one reading a will. There was a name for what lay on her nerves, a Latin name, which Jakub Nowak, clockmaker's apprentice, had written on his palm that day in biro and spent three weeks learning to pronounce.

She was to play the great *Chaconne*, the long dark mountain at the end of Bach's second partita, fourteen minutes without a note of accompaniment. Nobody could play it without hearing it. This was a well-known fact. It was why the hall was full.

She lifted the bow.

Nobody saw that the floor of the stage had a trapdoor. Nobody knew that beneath it, on his back among old cables and the dust of two centuries, in his one black suit, lay a thin young man with a clockmaker's mallet in his right hand, tapping, on a seasoned oak beam, with the precision of the finest escapement in Poland, the pulse of the *Chaconne*.

It was a thing he had worked out over six weeks, with a stopwatch, a stethoscope borrowed from a cousin, and a length of rubber hose. The boards of the stage were old and resonant. A tap on the beam went up through the joists, through the planks, through the soles of a violinist's shoes, and into her bones; and she, who had stood and played with her whole body since she was five, could take the pulse of the music in her feet.

She began. And the thing she could not hear came out of her instrument, and out of her, and went into three hundred listeners as a river goes into a sea.

He could not see her. He could only hear it, from below, through the planks, as the people in a cellar hear the church above them: the great chords like beams falling, the long ascents, the slow descent into the minor, the dark opened and opened again. He tapped. His arm went numb, and he went on. Somewhere in the ninth minute he found that he was weeping, flat on his back in the dust, quite soundlessly, and that he had not missed a beat.

At the end she stood in the silence with the bow lifted, as the old players do, to hold the last chord in the air. She did not know whether it had ended. She felt, through the soles of her shoes, three taps, soft, final, like a clock saying *there*. And so she lowered the bow.

The hall rose. She felt it as one feels a ship's deck begin to move: three hundred pairs of feet, a shudder in the boards, a roar in the floor, a gladness that she could not hear and did not need to.

Afterwards, in the arcaded courtyard, in the half-dark, with the old clock above them preparing to strike, Jakub came out with dust in his hair, and she saw it, and began to laugh.

"You have been under the stage."

"Fourteen minutes. I was very comfortable."

"Liar." She put out her hand and brushed the grey from his fringe with great tenderness. She read his lips by the lantern. "Jakub. I have to tell you the date."

"I know the date."

"It is on the ninth. They say that if all is well, I may hear again, a little. They say that if all is not well—" She did not finish. The Latin name lay between them like a coin. She took from her bag a small silver pocket watch with a faded engraving on its case: *Z.K.*, her grandfather's, which had stopped three summers ago, and which had, in January, been left on her doorstep, going. She had never asked who had mended it. "Keep it wound," said Zofia, "will you? At nine every night. Do not let it stop."

"I will."

"I want to ask you one thing. The last bar." Her eyes did not leave his lips. "Did I get the last bar?"

"Every note," said Jakub.

"Liar," said Zofia, tenderly.

"Yes," said Jakub.

And she smiled, and put her forehead against his shoulder, and they stood in the arcade of the oldest university in Poland while above them the little mechanical figures of the great clock began to turn, and the hour was struck, and she did not hear it, and he did, and neither of them said so. It was the kindest transaction in Kraków that night.

*Reader, you were told this was a tragedy, and it is. The Keeper's pencil is not a cruel one, and so here is the fact, plainly: the operation was on the ninth of June. She did not wake. The surgeon wrote that it was quiet. And Jakub Nowak wound the silver watch at nine every night for the rest of his life.*

**Bucharest, Romania. The Circul Aurora, on the night of the new programme.**

Elena Stanciu, seventy-seven, retired hotelier, had sat in the third row of every circus performance in Bucharest since 1962, and was therefore in a position to inform the young woman beside her that nothing, in the entire history of the circus, had ever been as dangerous as it looked, or as safe.

"My husband was a trapeze man," said old Elena, sucking a lemon sweet, "and he fell eleven times, and he was never so hurt as when he did not fall."

"That is not comforting, doamnă," said Ioana Dumitrescu.

She was twenty-eight, a nurse, and she was sitting bolt upright, with her hands knotted in her lap and her coat still on. She had read the scans. She could have drawn the crooked seam between the first and second lumbar vertebrae of Andrei Popescu with her eyes shut, and had told him that she would not tell him to stop, and would not pretend to like it. She had brought her bag, and it sat on her knees like a small black dog. Here she was, eyes open, as the lights died on the ring and the owner of the circus, Ionuț Radu, forty-three, a dentist who had inherited the big top and pulled teeth on Mondays, made his announcement in the voice of a man who has bitten on something and decided to enjoy it.

"Ladies and gentlemen! Without nets, without harness, without insurance, and with my *deepest regrets*—the man who has walked the wire for eleven years! *Andrei Popescu!*"

The drums began.

There had been a low wire, six feet from the ground, with a net beneath it, built for him by Mihai for the gala. Andrei had walked it twice in rehearsal, thanked the owner with great courtesy, and asked for the high one.

He came out in white. He was thirty-one, lean, quiet-faced, fearless in the air and timid on the ground, a man who in the ordinary world had needed a conspiracy of six people to ask a woman to dinner, and who now climbed a rope ladder as though it were a staircase in his own house. At the top he stood on the little platform, twelve metres above the sawdust. He lifted his pole. He looked down, once, over the rows, until he found the third seat in the third row, and a woman with her hands in her lap.

He smiled. And he set his foot on the wire.

It was nothing. It was eighteen metres of steel cable, thin as a pencil, and he walked it as a man walks a pavement, with the pole sinking and rising, sinking and rising, like the bar of a scale. The drums hushed. The trumpets held a single sustained note. In the third row Ioana counted his steps, as she counted a pulse, one, two, three, four, and found that her own had slowed to match.

Halfway across, the wire sang a note she had never heard it sing. It was high, and short, like a kettle just before the lid. **Antioquia. Six in the evening.**

At six, in the first clear light of the day, when the cloud had gone up the mountain like a congregation going home, a figure on a racing bicycle came down the Las Palmas road without his team, without his jersey zipped, and with a lamp clipped crooked to his handlebar.

The roses were still there. The road had been opened, the vehicles had passed, the whole circus of the Vuelta had flowed on to Rionegro in a river of horns. But on the left verge, in the dusk, a long red ribbon still lay along the grass, half trampled, half untouched, an eight-hundred-metre testimony.

Daniela was at her gate, in her boots, with the end of a broom. She had sent her workers home. She had told Paola to go and tell her mother. She stood alone at the entrance of a farm, with a letter from a bank folded in her back pocket, and watched a man ride toward her through a field of her own pride.

"You are supposed to be in a hotel with a physiotherapist," said Daniela.

"I am supposed to be where I crashed."

"You did not crash *here.*"

"I did, a little," said Sebastián, and put his foot down.

He was shaking. It was not the effort. He leaned the bicycle against her gate, with the care that he gave nothing else in the world, and he stood with his helmet in his hand and his hair plastered and his knees going, a man of twenty-nine who could not remember the first line of what he had rehearsed up a mountain.

"I came eleventh," he said.

"I know. I heard. It was on every radio in the valley. They are going to name a *sandwich*."

"I crashed because I was thinking of you."

"I know. It is on the Register." She did not smile. She held the broom as a bishop holds a crook. "Sebastián. They were going to rot. It was not a gesture. It was a Tuesday decision. I could not sell them. I have a bank that wants ninety million pesos by the end of the harvest, or it will take the farm, and I will not stand in my own greenhouse and tell a man who has just climbed a mountain on a carpet of my roses that I need to be rescued. I am *not* a rose in a bucket."

"I did not say that you were."

"No. You were too kind."

"I have a prize of four hundred thousand pesos," said Sebastián, "for the eleventh place."

She laughed. It was a startled, offended laugh, the laugh of a person who has been caught in the middle of being proud, and it went on a good deal longer than she meant, and left her eyes bright and wet. He, who had made a career of timing, did not say a word.

"If I asked you now," he said, after a while.

"Then I would say, *not now.*" She looked at him, straight and clear and tired. "It is not the same as *no.* I have found that the two are very different animals. One of them is a door shut. The other is a door that wants to be a better door."

"How long does it take to build a better door?"

"I think," said Daniela, "until the end of the harvest."

He nodded, slowly, in a ragged dusk, like a man who has been told the length of a mountain and will take the hairpins as they come. From the edge of the verge she took, out of a bucket, one single rose, the last one, stem and thorns and all, and held it out to him, across the gate.

"For your mother," said Daniela Ruiz.

"She will be insufferable," said Sebastián.

"I am counting on it."
