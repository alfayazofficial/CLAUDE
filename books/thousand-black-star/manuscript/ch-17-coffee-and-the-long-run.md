## Chapter 17
### Coffee and the Long Run

> *"This is my last race. After it my knee is finished."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0243

---

### SCENE I
*Addis Ababa. The slopes of Entoto, a little before six, nearly three thousand metres above the sea. Eucalyptus smoke, cold sunrise, and two runners going up a hill that has no intention of ending.*

At that altitude the air is thin as a rumour, and a runner who wishes to breathe must first make friends with it.

"Again," said Selam Tesfaye.

"I cannot."

"You can. You are twelve. At twelve one can do anything but one's homework." She did not slow. She was thirty-three, hard and narrow as a blade, with a black brace strapped around her right knee that she wore under her tights so that nobody might see, and had worn so long that she no longer remembered running without it. "Again. To the tree with the red cloth."

"That is *two kilometres.*"

"It is *nineteen hundred metres.* I measured it myself, in 2011, with a rope."

Bethlehem Girma, twelve, schoolchild, who had been running behind Selam's heels every dawn since the previous spring for no reason she would give, said something unrepeatable in Amharic and kept going.

Down the slope past them came the women of Entoto, in a long bent line, each with a bundle of eucalyptus branches strapped to her back that towered above her head like a green and walking house. They went down in the cold light, under their loads, as their mothers had, to sell the wood in the market at the foot of the hill; and one of them, an old woman with a face like a folded map, lifted her chin as the runners passed and said, with perfect indifference:

"*Selam. Your leg.*"

"It is fine, Emaye."

"It is *not* fine. It makes a sound."

"It is a *private* sound."

"Then tell it to be quieter," said the old woman, and went on down the hill with the wood.

They ran to the tree with the red cloth. Selam touched it, and turned, and for a moment stood quite still with her hand against the trunk, and the whole bright city lay below her in the haze, with its cranes and its tin roofs and its thousand blue plastic water tanks like a sea of small dull stars. In the spring there was a race, on the roads of Addis itself: twenty-six miles and three hundred and eighty-five yards. It would be the last time she ever ran so far, and she had told no one, because to say it aloud would be to start the counting.

"Selam?" said Bethlehem, who had come up behind her, wheezing. "Why do you only buy your coffee on Saturdays?"

"Because on Saturdays I have earned it."

"But you go to the same place. The man with the big shoulders who never smiles."

"He smiles."

"He does not. I have watched." The girl bent double, hands on her knees. "He looks at your leg when you are not looking at him. And you look at his hands when he is not looking at you. It is very strange. It is like two people at a bus stop who both pretend the bus has come."

"Run," said Selam. "*Down.* And I am not your coach."

### SCENE II
*Samuel Bekele's roastery, in a lane off Churchill Avenue. Saturday, midday. A drum, a pan, a brazier. Sacks of green beans, stencilled with the names of the high country: Yirgacheffe, Sidamo, Guji. A smell that could convert an atheist.*

Samuel Bekele, thirty-six, steady, proud, and as visibly unsmiling as a very large door, was roasting coffee with the intensity of a man who has a debt.

He had six. That was the part nobody saw.

It had been a good business once. *Bekele & Brother, Green and Roasted, Since 1998.* It had been a place with a name on a board, two trucks, a ledger in Yared's careless left-handed hand, and a laugh that came up the lane before its owner. Then, in October, Yared had died on the Awash road, and what he left was the laugh's absence, a warehouse with a mortgage and eleven thousand sacks of green Yirgacheffe, and a list. Samuel had found it in a cigarette tin: *a baker, two cousins, a bank, a priest, a man in Dire Dawa who sold goats.* A brother who owed everyone, and had smiled about it, and had never said.

Samuel, who was not a man who smiled, had said: *Then I owe everyone.* It was a sentence of a kind that, once said, will not go away.

At the front of the roastery, on a low stool beside a brazier of coals, sat Selam Tesfaye with her coat open and her bare hands round a clay cup, watching him like a judge watching a defendant who is not quite guilty.

"The pan," she said.

"I have a perfectly good drum."

"I want the pan." She said it without heat, the way one says a plain truth about the weather. "I am a woman of Addis. I will have my coffee made in my grandmother's way, on the coals, in the flat iron, with the beans turned by a man who will not talk to me."

"I have not said a word."

"I know," said Selam. "That is what I am paying for."

He washed the green beans in a bowl, as they had done it in his mother's house, and set them on the pan over the coals, and began to move them with a long iron spoon. There was a sound like rain beginning on a tin roof. Then a smell rose, and it was green first, grassy, bright, and then, as the colour deepened, sweet, and then, abruptly, nutty and brown, and over the whole room the blue smoke rolled and hung in the sunlight like incense in a church, and Selam bent and fanned it toward her face with her cupped hand, as one does, and closed her eyes.

> *The bean is green, and thinks itself a stone.
> The fire says: Open. And it will not hear.
> The fire says: Open. And the hull goes brown.
> The fire says: Open. And it cracks, and sings,
> And what it was is lost, and what it is
> Is all the house can smell for half a mile.
> Pray, child, that when the heat comes for your hull,
> You crack, and sing, and do not call it grief.*

It was an old song, said by women over pans. Samuel did not know that he had said it aloud until he saw her face.

"Where did you learn that?"

"From my mother," said Samuel Bekele, and for the first time in four months, did not look away.

The bell on the street door rang.

It was a tall man in a black suit that had once been good, and a short woman in a brown one that had never been, and between them a third, a stout and mournful figure in a woollen hat, carrying under one arm a very large book and under the other a very small umbrella.

"Ato Samuel," said the tall one. "A moment."

"It is Saturday," said Samuel.

"Debts do not observe the Sabbath."

"This one does. It is Saturday, and I am roasting."

The short woman in brown, who was Rahel Girma, fifty-five, landlady of the warehouse and scribe of the legal paper for half the lanes of Addis, and who did not so much lack manners as use them as a lawyer uses a cough, set a folder on the sack of Guji beans. "Samuel. There are six. Three are patient. Three are not. The man in Dire Dawa has sent his nephew, who is large."

"The goat man."

"The goat man's nephew. He is outside, and he has brought a goat, as a *warning.*"

At the end of the room, Selam did not move. She sat as still as a runner in the starting-blocks, her cup halfway to her mouth, with a face from which every expression had been carefully removed, as one removes the pits from olives. She was not looking at the creditors. She was not looking at him. She was looking at the pan.

And Samuel, who could feel her not-looking, felt something in his chest that he had no word for in any of his languages: not shame, nor quite gratitude, but the sweet and awful sensation of being seen and spared.

The man in the woollen hat cleared his throat.

"I am not a creditor," said Dawit Tadesse, fifty-nine, librarian, of Bahir Dar, with an air of enormous apology. "I wish it understood. I am only a *lender.* Your brother Yared, may he rest in peace, borrowed from me three thousand birr and a book."

"The book?" said Samuel.

"*Fikir Eske Mekabir.* A first edition, with *annotations.* It is of immense sentimental value, and also one thousand birr." He held up the very large volume under his arm, which was not that book, and then, aghast, put it down. "I do not need the money, Ato Samuel, though I would welcome it. But I have another request, which is a *great* impertinence."

"What?"

"I am fifty-nine years old and I have never seen the sea." He said it with the stricken look of a man confessing to a crime. "I am a librarian. I know the sea in nine languages. I have read about it for forty years. I understand that your trucks go to Djibouti. When the next container goes, I should like—it would settle the whole matter for me—to ride in the lorry. And look."

There was a long pause. The beans cracked on the pan like a small burst of applause.

"A debt," said Dawit Tadesse, in the voice of a man who has said a thing so true that he is unaware of it, "is only a letter that has not yet been answered. I should like to be answered with a view of the water."

Rahel Girma, scribe of debts, who had written ten thousand promissory notes and read in every one the same fear, looked at the old librarian with an expression that, in a less professional person, would have been called *tenderness.*

"Ato Samuel," she said at last. "I will tell the goat man's nephew that you are *in negotiation.* I will give you until the lunar month's end. For the others—" She picked up her folder. "They will wait. They are more patient than they appear. They have all been to a funeral."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me. I charge for it." She nodded to the room, and to Selam, with an ironical little bow, and withdrew, followed by the tall man, and the goat man's nephew, who was audibly arguing with a goat.

Dawit Tadesse lingered at the door.

"I was very sorry about Yared," he said. "He was the only man in Addis who ever returned a book *late* and still made me glad of it."

When he had gone, there was only the coffee: the brown beans heaped in the pan, glossy as river stones, shedding their thin smoke into the light.

Selam put down her cup.

"Samuel."

"Yes."

"The beans are burning."

He started, and swept them off the pan with a long scrape of the iron spoon, and stood with his hands on the edge of the table and his back to her. It was a very broad back. It had carried a coffin.

"I did not hear anything," said Selam Tesfaye. "I want you to know that. I heard the beans, and I heard the street, and I have heard nothing else at all."

"I know."

"And you did not see me limp on the stairs, and you did not see the brace, and you did not see me put my hand on the wall."

"I saw nothing," said Samuel.

"Good." She stood. She was shorter than he, by a head, and as upright as a post, and as she rose she did something, a very small thing, with her knee, which clicked, in the quiet, like a latch. Neither of them remarked on it. "Then we are in agreement. I will take two hundred and fifty grams of the Yirgacheffe. And I would like it ground."

"In the mortar?"

"In the mortar, by you. With the long pestle."

He ground it. It took some minutes, and the room filled with the smell of a crushed morning, dark and floral and bright. When he handed her the little brown bag she held it a moment against her chest before putting it into her pocket.

"There is a race in the spring," she said abruptly. "Here, on the Gulele Road. It is my last."

"I did not—"

"I did not say you did. I am *saying.*" Her jaw was set. "So you will understand that I do not want anyone standing at the thirty-fifth kilometre, in the dust, with something hot. I shall not look for you."

"Coffee," said Samuel, to the pan.

"*Obviously* not," said Selam.

She did not say *come.* He did not say *I will.* Between two proud people, reader, nobody has ever said a plainer thing.

### SCENE III
*St Petersburg. The fourth-floor studio above the Nevsky Chamber Theatre, at five in the evening, which in January is midnight with the lamps lit. Plaster angels on the ceiling. A barre, a battered piano, an iron stove. A grey sky pressing its face to the windows.*

A pianist's love is made, if he is honest, of rests.

This is a secret of the trade, and Dmitri Volkov, forty, accompanist, who had sat at the same instrument for sixteen years and never once been asked what he thought of the ballet, kept it better than most. A note, he knew, is only half the music. The other half is the silence that follows it. It is the held breath, the *fermata,* the pause before the downbeat in which a whole house of eight hundred people forgets to cough; and in a long and orderly life he had learned to love that silence more than any chord, because it was the only thing in the world that was his alone.

At a quarter to five, with the children's class dismissed and the corridor emptied of small shrieking dancers in woollen leg-warmers, he sat at the piano in the half-dark, with the stove ticking and the snow in the window turning blue, and said, to the instrument, to the plaster angels, to nobody:

> *I wrote it in three-quarter time, because
> A waltz is three: a step, a step, a breath.
> The first is for the leaving; and the second,
> For the return; the third is for the rest
> In which the dancer, poised above her future,
> Does not yet know which way her foot will fall.
> I wrote it for a woman who has taught
> Two hundred children how to stand on air,
> And never has been asked to stand on it
> Herself, since that last night, the knee, the curtain.
> I have not asked. I have been courteous.
> I have been courteous for two years,
> Which is to say a coward, with good manners.
> Tonight there is the snow. The room is empty.
> If I should ask, and she should say it plain—
> Then let her say it plain.*

"You are talking to the angels," said a dry voice from the door, "again."

Dmitri did not leap, though a lesser man would have. He rose, with the grave slow formality that was his only armour, and turned, and there she stood, with her black bag over her shoulder and a cane she denied was a cane. She was thirty-eight. She had a long throat and a proud flat mouth and a way of standing that was less a posture than a ruling, and the left knee, under her trousers, was stiff as a hinge in winter.

Anya Petrova, retired ballerina, who in her day had been called *the Swan of the Fontanka* and, in her present state, had been called by the youngest of her pupils *Madame Hurricane,* looked at him down the length of the rehearsal room as at a man who has been caught with the silver.

"I was warming the instrument," said Dmitri.

"For whom?"

"For the... morning."

"It is five in the evening."

"Then for the *next* morning."

"You are the most courteous liar in the Russian Federation," said Anya. She came in out of the doorway, and he could hear the knee on each step, the little dry click like a clock being wound, and she dropped her bag on the bench by the barre. "I forgot my glasses. Do not look at me like that. I am thirty-eight. I am entitled."

"I did not look."

"You always look. You look and you pretend you did not. You have done so for six years, and since the gala you have done it up four flights of stairs." She turned, with the glasses in her hand, and did not put them on.

There was a silence. The stove ticked. It was, he noted with the instinct of his trade, an excellent silence: round, full, unhurried.

"Madame," said Dmitri.

"Do not call me *Madame.*"

"Anya."

"Better."

"I have something to ask you."

"If it is about the piano tuner, he is a thief. If it is about the children's concert, I will not wear the sequins."

"It is not about the sequins."

"Then?"

He crossed the floor. It was twelve paces; he counted them, from habit, in three. At the end of them he stopped, and bowed, with a deep old-fashioned bow, the kind that has not been seen in a Petersburg rehearsal room since the days of Imperial teachers, and came up again with a face as white as a fresh page.

"Anya Petrova," he said. "I wrote a waltz. You know that. You heard it in the wings, on the night of the gala. I did not know; I learned it from four lines left under a lamp. I have never told you whom it was for. I have never asked you for anything. I am asking you now." He drew breath. "Would you do me the honour of one dance?"

There was no sound in the room but the stove.

Anya Petrova stood quite still. A long moment went by in which a great many things happened in her face and none of them stayed: a flush, and a flash of anger, and something so sore and bright and young that it seemed to belong to another person, a girl of seventeen on a stage lit like the inside of a pearl.

"I have a knee," she said.

"I am aware."

"It has a longer memory than I have, and a shorter temper. It has not forgiven the third act of *Giselle.*"

"It need not forgive. I do not ask it to *leap.*"

"What do you ask it to do?"

"Walk," said Dmitri. "In three. A step, a step, a breath. I will play it for you in the head. You need not move your arms. You need not do anything but be in the room while I am in it."

"That is not dancing."

"That is the *oldest* dancing," said Dmitri Volkov, with the sudden, startling shyness of a man saying the one true thing he has rehearsed for two years, "and I am told I have a gift for the rest."

She looked at him. She looked at the rest, the silence, the held breath in the dark, the hour on the clock, the angels on the ceiling, who had looked down for a hundred years on every sort of fool.

"No," said Anya.

He did not move. It is a mark of a courtly man that he can be struck in the face and keep it at the same angle.

"Not now," she said. "Not tonight. I will not do it in a room with a stove and a cough. I will not do it badly. If I am to dance with a man for the first time in six years, I intend to do it *well.* Ask me again."

"When?"

"When the Neva thaws."

"That is April."

"It is *usually* April."

"Then I will ask in April."

"You will ask all winter," said Anya. "I know you. You will ask me in your head with every bar. And I will say *no,* and *not yet,* and *you are impossible,* and in April—" She stopped. She took up her bag, and her glasses, and put the glasses at last upon her nose, which gave her the look of an affronted owl. "In April, we will see."

She went to the door. With her hand on it she paused, and did not turn.

"Dmitri."

"Yes."

"Dmitri Andreyevich."

"Yes."

"The seventh bar. The third beat. You are not holding it *long* enough."

"I know."

"Play it again. With the rest."

He sat. He put his hands to the keys. Behind him the door shut with a click, and her uneven footsteps went away down the stairs, step, step, and the dry small click, and the step. And he played the waltz, slowly, the whole of it, in the dark, with the rests as long as they wanted to be, and at the seventh bar, on the third beat, where a great deal of his life was kept, he held the silence so long that the plaster angels leaned from the ceiling to find out what would come next.

In the corridor, among a rack of tutus, with a copy of the *Evening Petersburg* held before his face like a mask, Sergei Orlov, costume-master, fifty-seven, devout, who had read a good half of the theatre's private correspondence by the simple expedient of standing very still, murmured to a feathered skirt:

"*Well, my dear,*" he said. "*She did not say never.*"

Sergei, who read other people's post and other people's lives, and was wrong about the one and right about the other, crossed himself, and went away to mend a swan.
