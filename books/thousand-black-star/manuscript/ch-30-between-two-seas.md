## Chapter 30
### Between Two Seas

> *"I am an hour ahead of you, and I keep looking back."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0714

---

*Enter the Chorus, alone, in the dark between two cities.*

> Between two seas a single night is strung,
> One hour long, as thin as spider's thread,
> On which two hearts, both silent, and both young,
> Hang out their lamps and fear to be misread.
> Behold: not here the clash of rival lines,
> Nor poisoned cup, nor ferry set aflame;
> But two lit screens, a parrot, and the signs
> Of two shy souls too proud to speak a name.
> Be gentle, then. For this is how it goes
> When ambition and duty keep their hours:
> No sword is drawn; the only thing that glows
> Is one small glass, a candle, and some flowers.
> Sit close. We tell the night they almost said it.
> The Keeper noted it. The ledger read it.

---

### SCENE I
*Mirpur, Dhaka. A tea stall at the corner of a lane. Friday, 9:20 p.m. Rain, then no rain, then rain again, as though the sky were undecided.*

Bunny, who was thirteen, owned a kettle, a bench, and an opinion on every subject, and had never once been asked for any of them. His uncle owned the stall; Bunny owned the customers.

"Bhaiya," he said, as he poured the tea (two spoons of sugar, one of condensed milk, as prescribed), "you are wearing your *good* shirt."

"It's the same shirt."

"It is the same shirt," Bunny agreed, "but today it has been *ironed*. A shirt that is ironed on a Friday night is a shirt with a plan."

Fayaz Rahman, twenty-six, freelance developer, founder of a company that no longer existed, and sole proprietor of a worry that did, took the glass. "I have a call at ten."

"With the Java girl."

"She is from Yogyakarta."

"That is where Java is."

"*Yogyakarta is in Java*," said Fayaz, "but Java is not in Yogyakarta."

Bunny considered this with the gravity of a judge. "That is very clever," he said, "and I am not sure it is true." He leaned on the counter. "Bhaiya. Let me tell you a thing about this girl."

"I have not asked."

"No. But I have been pouring your tea for four months, and in four months a man has told me three things. One: she laughs like a tin roof in rain. Two: she is always awake before you. Three—" he held up three tea-stained fingers "—she is *an hour ahead*." He shook his head. "A girl who is an hour ahead, and a man who runs an hour behind? Bhaiya. That is not a love story. That is a *timetable*."

Fayaz opened his mouth to say that nobody had said the word *love*, which was not a thing a sensible man said to a boy with a kettle.

Then the light bulb above the stall went out. The whole lane went out. The humming refrigerator at the pharmacy next door sighed and died, like a very large beast surrendering to sleep.

"Load-shedding," said Bunny serenely. "Twenty minutes. Maybe an hour."

"An *hour*?"

"You see," said Bunny. "Everything in your life is an hour." He took the candle stub from under the counter, the last of the Eid supply, and set it in Fayaz's hand as a priest sets a blessing. "Go. Tell the timetable I said it will be on time."

---

### SCENE II
*The same night. Fayaz's flat, third floor. A candle. A phone. One laptop, at eleven percent.*

At 9:58 by his clock, which meant 10:58 by hers, Fayaz propped the laptop on two textbooks and a copy of *Thinking in Systems*, lit a candle, and pressed the green button.

For a moment there was nothing but a spinning ring.

Then the room across the sea came into being.

It was a workshop: low beams, hanging cloths in rust and indigo, a copper-tipped pen in a brass dish, a small stove where wax lay melted to gold. In the foreground sat a young woman with her hair pinned up with a pencil, a smudge of blue on her cheekbone, and the particular quiet that belongs to people who are about to say something difficult and have spent the afternoon rehearsing something else.

"You are in the dark," said Ermis.

"The power is out."

"You look like a man in a play."

"I am a man in a *flat*," said Fayaz. "But I accept the compliment."

"I did not say it was a compliment." Her mouth moved. "It was an observation. I make observations. It is a professional habit." She leaned closer to the screen. "Is that a candle?"

"It was cheaper than a generator."

"You *do* have a generator! You said so in March."

"I said it was *on the wrong circuit.*"

She laughed, and he heard the tin roof, the rain, the particular music that had been the best part of every Friday for four months. Then something small and sharp in the room behind her said, quite clearly:

"*TOO LATE! TOO LATE! BEAUTIFUL!*"

"That is Ayu," said Ermis, without turning. "My grandmother's parrot. She is not allowed in the workshop, but she is not allowed anywhere, and she has taken the principle personally."

"She sounds wise."

"She is *vile.* Ayu! *Diam!* She is Eyang's parrot, not mine. My grandmother is eighty-one and has never lost an argument, and she has taught the bird to do it for her."

"*BEAUTIFUL!*"

"*BEAUTIFUL!*"

"She says that to everyone," said Ermis, a little too quickly.

"I am sure she does," said Fayaz, a little too gravely.

There was a silence. It was a comfortable silence, as the best ones are, with two separate rains in it.

"Fayaz," said Ermis.

"Yes."

"I have to tell you something, and I would like you to not say anything until I have finished."

"I can do that. I am the most patient man in Mirpur. It is my only qualification."

She took a breath, carefully, like someone who has learned in her profession how long wax takes to cool.

"I have been offered a fellowship," she said. "In Rotterdam. At the Maashaven Textile Lab, which works with *tulis* masters from four continents. Two years. They pay for everything. I will have my own studio. I will learn what I cannot learn here. I will…" She stopped. She looked down at her hands. "I have known since January."

"Since *January*?"

"You said not to speak."

"I withdraw the question."

"I said yes in January," said Ermis. "At seven minutes past midnight, an hour after we had said goodnight. I sat with my finger on the button, and then I pressed it, and then I sat there until my tea went cold. That is the part I am ashamed of. Not that I accepted. That I did it after you had said goodnight, so that I would not have to see your face. And I did not tell you, because I did not want you to look at me the way you are looking at me now."

"How am I looking at you?"

"Like a man who is happy for me and wishes he were not."

Fayaz said nothing. In his flat, the candle burned very straight, there being no wind to trouble it.

"I leave on the twenty-second," said Ermis.

"Of this month?"

"Of this *month.*"

"That is three weeks."

"I can count, Fayaz." Her voice cracked on his name, once, like a thread taking a strain. "That is why I am telling you tonight. I wanted to tell you in January. And then in February. And then in March. And every week I thought: next Friday. And every Friday you made a joke about a generator and I thought: how can I say that to a man who makes jokes like that?"

"That is what jokes are *for.*"

"I know." She wiped her cheekbone with the back of her wrist; the smudge of indigo went with it, leaving a long blue comet. "I know exactly what they are for."

And then, because he was kind, and because he was twenty-six, and because the whole dark city seemed to forbid anyone to be selfish for very long, Fayaz Rahman did the thing that cost him the most.

He smiled. A real smile, not a courtesy.

"Ermis," he said. "Go."

"That is not what I asked you."

"You did not ask me anything. You said not to speak. I have been speaking out of turn for two minutes now, and so I will finish with this. *Go.* Go to Rotterdam. Go and learn everything those four continents know and then come home and teach it to the world." He drew a breath. "I will not be the thing that makes you stay."

"I did not say you would be."

"No. You were too kind to say it. That is why I am saying it."

For a long moment she looked at him, and he could not read her face, which was a thing he had never before had to say. The wax on her stove had started to smoke a little; she reached out without looking and turned the flame down.

"You are a very stubborn man," said Ermis.

"It is the only thing I have inherited that I could not sell."

### SCENE III
*The call continues. The power does not return. The battery, at six percent, begins to fail.*

"Now you," said Ermis. "Your turn."

"My turn for what?"

"For telling me the thing you are not telling me." She said it lightly, the way one walks across ice. "I said you could not speak until I finished. I have finished. So: speak. What is it this week? The company? Your father? Tithi's exams? Do not say 'nothing'. You say 'nothing' like a man sitting on the lid of a pot that is boiling over."

Fayaz laughed once, ruefully, and rubbed the back of his neck.

"It is all of them," he said. "On the same day, in a good week."

"Tell me."

He told her. It took longer than he intended, and shorter than it felt. He told her of the debt that had come, like a stray dog, with the death of the company; of a supplier his father had trusted who had not been trustworthy; of fourteen lakh taka, which was not a great sum in the great arithmetic of the world, and was a mountain in a flat in Mirpur. He told her of his mother's breathing, and how at night he could hear it through the wall like a clock that has been wound too tight. He told her of Tithi, who was seventeen and sharp as a nail and would be the first in the family to go to university if the fees were paid. He told her of two embassy letters, each beginning *We regret,* and of a third he had not opened because he knew what it would say.

"I have a contract with a firm in Singapore," he said. "Night work. It pays in a currency that is kind to me. In eleven months it will clear the debt. In twenty it will clear my mother's treatment. In thirty it will let me think about something else." He gave a half-laugh. "I have a very good schedule. It is only that I am somewhere in the middle of it, and the middle is a long place."

"You never said."

"I never *say*," said Fayaz. "It is a practice. I am very well-mannered about my own misfortune."

"That is not manners. That is a wall."

"Then it is a wall with *excellent* manners."

"Eyang says a wall is only a door that has been told to wait," said Ermis.

"Your grandmother says a great many things."

"She is eighty-one. She has earned the rest." She did not laugh. She leaned her chin on her hand, and her eyes, which were large and dark and the color of strong tea, moved slowly over his face, as though reading a pattern she had known for months and was only now seeing in full.

"You wrote," she said, "*I would tell you everything, if I were free to.*"

"I did."

"Two winters ago."

"I was twenty-four. I had a company. I was a very confident man."

"And now? Are you free to?"

He considered it. The candle shivered; the battery icon blinked an amber warning.

"No," said Fayaz, slowly, honestly. "Not yet. But I am more free than I was. And I am telling you *this.* Which, if you knew me, is a great deal."

"I know you," said Ermis. "That is why I know it is."

---

*The battery, at two percent, begins to blink.*

"I will lose you in a moment," said Fayaz. "Let me say something before the machine decides for me."

"Say it."

He did not say what he had intended. That was the trouble with having been honest for twenty minutes: it had worn the cover off the thing.

"I am not going to ask you to wait," he said. "That would be a cruelty. You will go to Rotterdam. You will be wonderful. You will meet people who are not tired and not in debt. And I would think no less of you, at the end, for anything."

"Fayaz—"

"But if I may ask one thing."

"Yes."

"Every Friday. This hour. *Ten, mine; eleven, yours.* If you can. If you are willing. For as long as we are able." He swallowed. "I am not asking for a promise. I am only asking for an appointment."

There was a long silence. In it, he heard a copper pen touching a brass dish, once. In it, the parrot said, softly, as though even she had been moved: "*Beautiful.*"

Ermis took a very deep breath.

"I will tell you," she said, "what it is to be an hour ahead."

"I would like to hear it."

"When I wrote that line—two winters ago, at the end of my last year, at a table with no light—I meant a friend. A boy who went home. I kept looking back at a window that had gone dark." She looked at the screen. "I did not know it was a prophecy."

"It was a line of poetry."

"It was a *forecast.*" Her mouth trembled and then, with great resolution, did not. "It means this. I am an hour in your future, Fayaz Rahman. I have already seen it. And I want you to know that you were kind. All of it. The debt, and the dark, and the long middle. You were kind."

His throat closed. He did not trust himself to speak. Outside, the rain began again, as if to give him cover.

"So that is my appointment," said Ermis. "Fridays. Ten, yours; eleven, mine. And I will tell you what I will do, between Fridays. I will draw a pattern, on indigo, with no name. It will be a field of dots—*bintang,* stars—and every dot will be black. And when it is finished I will call it *Bintang Hitam,* because someone must, and because that is what it is. Black star. A thing that cannot be seen and that bends everything near it." She breathed out. "And I will send it to you when it is done."

"A field of black stars," said Fayaz, "and nobody to see them."

"You will see them." She said it so simply that it did not sound like a promise. It sounded like weather.

"I have no way to repay a thing like that."

"Do not repay. Be on time."

"I have never been late in my life."

"That," said Ermis, "is not true. Last Friday you were four minutes late, and you pretended the clock was wrong."

"It *was* wrong."

"By four minutes."

"By *four* minutes," said Fayaz, with great dignity. "A very small error, very consistently made."

She laughed. She laughed until the pen rattled in the dish and the wax on the stove shivered and, in the room behind her, a large green bird screamed "*TOO LATE! TOO LATE!*" with every appearance of joy.

---

### SCENE IV
*The laptop shuts down. Fayaz is in the dark, with a candle. He picks up his phone.*

The screen died on a half-laugh, which is, of all the ways to die, perhaps the kindest.

For a time Fayaz sat quite still in his flat, with a candle for a sun and the rain going on and off like a clock wound by a clumsy hand. Below in the lane a boy was whistling. Through the wall, his mother coughed twice and was quiet. In the next room Tithi was asleep with a physics textbook on her face.

He picked up his phone, which still had nine percent, and opened the chat window, which had a little pale dot of green beside her name.

He typed:

> **Fayaz:** *Same time Friday?*

He looked at it. He deleted *Friday* and typed *next Friday* and deleted that and typed *every.* He deleted *every.* He put back *Friday.* He had written three thousand lines of code a day for years and had never, once, been so slow.

He sent it.

The little word *delivered* appeared beneath it.

Then a moment. Then a lighter, nervous moment, in which the whole world seemed to wait for the weather.

> *Ermis is typing…*

He watched the three small dots pulse, and sink, and pulse again, like a heart pretending to be calm.

> *Ermis is typing…*

They stopped.

They began.

They stopped.

*What* she was writing, in her lit workshop an hour ahead of him in time, with indigo on her cheek and a stove at her elbow and a parrot at the window, the page does not say. It is not for want of curiosity. It is that some sentences are not given to the reader. They are given only to the one for whom they were written, and then, if the stars are kind, to a ledger, in due time.

> *Ermis is typing…*

The dots pulsed once more.

Outside, the rain stopped. Somewhere, a generator coughed and started. A lamp in the lane came on, and then another, and then the whole street, like a thousand small decisions made at once.

From the lane below, a thirteen-year-old voice shouted, "*Bhaiya! The light has come! Is the girl still there?*" and was answered by nobody.

And in a third-floor flat in Mirpur, a young man in an ironed shirt, with no fortune to speak of, looked at the little green dot on a screen, and thought, for the first time in two years, with a surprise that he would have been embarrassed to admit:

*It is possible that I am happy.*
