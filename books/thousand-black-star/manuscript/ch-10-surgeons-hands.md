## Chapter 10
### Surgeon's Hands

> *"I lost the patient you taught to walk."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0818

---

### SCENE I
*São Paulo, Brazil. The rehabilitation wing of the Hospital Santa Aurora. Seven in the morning. The city outside is already honking, in a dozen million keys.*

São Paulo does not sleep; it merely lowers its voice from a roar to a mutter, and by seven in the morning it has remembered its mistake.

Carlos Mendes, thirty-three, sports physiotherapist, stood at the end of a set of parallel bars and said, in the voice of a man coaxing a nervous horse over a stream: "Dona Cida. One more. It is only a step. A step is the smallest unit of heroism."

"I am seventy-one," said Dona Cida, "and it is a *very large* step."

She took it. The little ward, which contained a dozen patients, six physiotherapists, and one television tuned to a soap opera in which somebody's twin had just returned from the dead, burst into applause. Carlos bowed. He had a warm, crooked, handsome face, brown eyes that crinkled at the outer corners, and the easy manner of a man who has made it his life's work to be useful to people in pain, and has found that if one is sufficiently useful, no one inquires after one's own.

On the fourth finger of his left hand, a plain gold ring caught the light of the fluorescent tube.

He had worn it for three years, as one wears a thing that has become part of the body. His patients assumed that he had a wife. His colleagues, who knew that she had died of a long illness two Marches ago, did not mention it, in the delicate Brazilian way, by mentioning everything else at great length. His friend Paulo Souza, the pharmacist across the street, who never answered telephones but sent voice messages of eleven minutes' duration, had once said: "Carlos, one does not have to take it off. But one might *polish* it less."

He polished it, in fact, on the hem of his white coat, whenever he thought himself unwatched.

*I am a widower,* he had written to an old woman in England, one night, with a bottle of cachaça and a bad conscience, *pretending to be a free man.*

It was a good sentence. It was a true sentence. It had never been spoken.

"Seu Carlos," said a young voice. "Excuse me."

---

He turned.

A boy stood at the entrance to the ward, sixteen, in school uniform, a thin, serious, wide-eyed boy holding a paper bag against his chest, and in his face, at once, Carlos knew the family.

"Leandro," he said, very quietly. "Raimundo's grandson."

"Yes, senhor."

"You came all the way from Santos?"

"The bus is two hours, senhor. My grandmother sends this." The boy held out the bag, which was warm and smelled of cheese. "It is *pão de queijo.* She says, thank you for the fourteen months. She says that you made him a man who could curse you on his own two feet."

It was the exact and only epitaph that Raimundo Teles would have wished.

He had been fifty-eight, a bus driver, a vast and furious man who had fallen from a scaffold while painting his daughter's house and had woken in a hospital with a verdict that he would never again walk to his own front door. Carlos had spent fourteen months proving the verdict wrong. Raimundo had insulted his mother, his methods, and the football team he supported, and on the last day, panting between the bars, three hundred steps and not one of them pretty, he had said: "Doutor, I will walk to my own funeral."

Which, three weeks ago, in the narrow and ironic geometry of life, he had very nearly done.

"My grandmother also wants to thank the doctor," said Leandro.

"Which doctor?"

"The one who tried." The boy swallowed. "The lady surgeon. She says the lady cried."

The ward, which was full of noise, became for Carlos Mendes a very long and quiet corridor.

### SCENE II
*The same day. The staff cafeteria, 9 p.m. Rain, in great loud sheets, as it comes in São Paulo: not falling so much as arriving.*

Beatriz Lima had been awake for thirty-one hours, and was eating a bowl of beans with the expression of a woman reading a final demand.

She was thirty-one, the youngest vascular surgeon in the hospital's history, and she looked it only in that she had not yet learned to stop proving it. She was tall and straight-backed, in green scrubs, with black hair pulled back so tightly it seemed an act of discipline, and large dark eyes rimmed with the grey of a person who has forgotten the use of sleep. Among the nurses she was called *a Doutora*, in a tone that contained admiration, fear, and a pity they never let her hear.

Her mother's name was Lima. She had kept it, after the funeral, as one keeps the key to a house that no longer exists.

"Eat," said her father, from across the table.

"I *am* eating."

"You are pushing a bean from the left side of the bowl to the right. It is a sad little pilgrimage."

Pedro Rocha was sixty, a retired chef, a huge, bald, talkative man with the forearms of a blacksmith and the soul of a tabloid. He had arrived at eight-thirty with a thermos of feijoada the size of a fire extinguisher, in defiance of every regulation concerning outside food, and had distributed it among the nursing staff like sacraments. He was so proud of his daughter that it had never occurred to him to say so; it would have been a vulgar attempt to describe the sun.

"Papai, I lost a patient."

"You lose patients. It is your profession. You are a surgeon."

"I *lose* them very rarely."

"Then it is the more astonishing that it should have happened to you." He leaned forward. "Who was he?"

"A bus driver."

"A *bus driver!* A man of the road! Salt of the earth! What was his name?"

"Raimundo Teles. He was fifty-eight, and he had been paralysed from the waist, and a physiotherapist taught him to walk. And then on the morning of the surgery, Papai, he *walked in.* Three hundred metres, from the front doors. He refused the stretcher. He told the porters, 'A man who has walked for Carlos Mendes can walk into any hospital in Brazil.' And I stood at the end of the corridor and watched him come." She put down the spoon. "And the aneurysm gave way at four minutes past ten, on the table, and there was nothing, *nothing*, not a thing I could do."

Pedro Rocha said nothing. For a talkative man, it was a feat of love.

"I have never met the physiotherapist," said Beatriz. "I have only seen his name on charts. But I think about it every night. I think: a man spent fourteen months, and made a man whole, and then I—" Her voice caught. "I could not hold him a *morning.*"

"Daughter." Her father reached across the table and put a hand like a spade over hers. "It is not your fault. Do you think the Lord, who is not a fool, would send a man a physiotherapist for fourteen months and a surgeon for ten minutes if He did not know how to count?"

She laughed. It was a small, startled, wet laugh, and she covered her face with her free hand.

"Ah," said Pedro. "There she is. Now let me show you something that will cheer you up." He took out his phone with the pride of one displaying a grandchild. "You know, of course, that I have a *blog.*"

"Papai, nobody reads your—"

"A hundred and forty thousand people have read *this.*"

---

The photograph showed an empty counter: a long counter of pale wood, in a very small shop; eight stools, a red-and-white curtain, a steam-clouded window. In the foreground a bowl of ramen stood at the eighth stool, the last one, by the pipe: perfect, golden, steaming, with a single sheet of seaweed and an egg cut in half, and no one before it.

Under it, in Portuguese and in a clumsy English translation, Pedro had written:

*Osaka, spring. The chef told me, through the interpreter, that he makes one bowl every night for a woman who stopped coming. He sets it at her stool. He does not eat it. At closing he pours it away, and in the morning, he begins again. I asked, "How long?" The chef did not answer. He only wiped the counter.*

Her father wiped his eyes with a napkin. "A man in Curitiba proposed to his girlfriend over it," he said.

Beatriz looked at the photograph for a long time.

A single bowl, steaming, before an empty stool. A thing made every night for one who is not there. She did not know why her throat had closed; she was a doctor, and wished to blame the beans.

*I know that bowl,* she thought, and could not have said why.

### SCENE III
*A lift, in the old wing, at a quarter past ten. The storm has taken the lights. The elevator, one of those great brass antiques that the Hospital Santa Aurora refuses to retire on grounds of sentiment, has stopped between the second and third floors.*

It was, of course, a conspiracy.

The reader should not be surprised. A hospital contains choirs, locksmiths, and matchmakers in roughly equal proportions, and when all three are present, the result is inevitable.

The choir was that of Fábio Santos, a thirty-year-old director from Campinas, who conducted the hospital's Christmas ensemble every Thursday in the ground-floor atrium. The locksmith was Thiago Oliveira, forty-one, vain and glittering, with a waxed moustache, who had been summoned by the porters to rescue the elevator, and who, being a matchmaker by vocation and a locksmith by trade, regarded the entire building as a lock with a very obvious solution.

"Who is in the lift?" he asked the porter.

"The Doutora Beatriz. And the physiotherapist. Seu Carlos."

"Both?"

"Both, Seu Thiago."

"Hm." Thiago Oliveira put down his bag, and looked at the ceiling with an expression of great professional delicacy. "It will take," he said, "approximately forty minutes."

"Forty? It is a simple fault. A child could—"

"A *good* lock," said Thiago, "is a slow lock."

And he sat down, and began, with a flourish, to lay out his tools in order of size.

*Mark this, reader. In forty minutes of a stuck lift, more things have been said than in forty years of dinner.*

---

It was dark. It was warm. There was a crack of emergency light along the floor, thin and amber, and by it they could see each other's shoes.

"I am sorry," said Carlos. "I was on my way to the pharmacy."

"I was on my way to a bed." She was standing with her back to the wall, arms crossed, in the gloom. "You are Mendes."

"I am."

"Lima. Beatriz Lima." There was a pause. "I was Raimundo Teles's surgeon."

"I know."

"I should have come to find you."

"I should have come to find *you*," said Carlos.

The elevator creaked. Somewhere beneath them, down in the atrium, a thin sound began, a choir with its hymn-book open, and the notes crept up the shaft like a faint, sweet smoke.

"He walked in," said Beatriz. "You must know that. He walked into my hospital, three hundred metres, in front of the whole ward, and he said your name. He said it to the porters, to the nurse, to the lift. I stood there, and I thought, *This is a man who has been given his life back. And I am about to—*" She stopped. "I have not been able to put it down."

"Doutora."

"Do not tell me it was not my fault. Everyone says so, and it is a kindness, and I cannot *use* it."

"I was not going to say that," said Carlos.

There was a silence, thick as velvet.

"I was going to say," he said, "that I have thought about that morning every day. Not about the aneurysm. About the three hundred metres. Do you know what I realised? Fourteen months. And in all that time, he never once walked for *me.* He walked to spite me, to prove me wrong, to see my face. But that morning he walked for *himself.* To a hospital. Into the hands of a woman he had never met. It was the whole point." His voice roughened. "I taught him to walk, Doutora. You gave him a place to walk *to.* There are not many surgeons who could say that."

She was silent. In the thin light he saw her put a hand to her mouth.

"That," said Beatriz, at last, unsteadily, "is the first thing anyone has said to me in three weeks that I can bear."

"I will say more if you want. I am a physiotherapist. I can say things for hours in a very reassuring voice."

She laughed. It broke out of her like water out of a cracked jug, and she slid down the wall of the lift to sit upon the floor, laughing and weeping at once, in her crumpled scrubs. Carlos, after a moment, sat down on the other side, at a safe and gentlemanly distance of about a metre, and found a clean handkerchief in his pocket, and extended it across the gap.

She took it. Their hands touched.

And in the amber glimmer, she saw it: a plain gold band on the fourth finger of a warm brown hand.

It is a curious thing, the speed of the heart. It can fall four floors in a quarter of a second.

"Your wife," said Beatriz, drawing her hand back with great composure, "must be very patient, to have a husband who sits on floors with strangers."

Carlos looked at the ring.

For a moment he opened his mouth. *Not what you think,* he thought. *It is exactly what you think. It is neither.* Words stood in his throat like men in a doorway, each waving the others past.

"She—" he said. "It is—a difficult matter."

"I am sure it is," said Beatriz, with the clipped kindness of a surgeon closing a wound. "Thank you for the handkerchief. I am very sorry for keeping you from the pharmacy."

*Ah,* says the Keeper, quietly, from the dark between the floors. *There it is. He might have said: I am a widower. A word of three syllables, and a world of difference. And she might have said: I should like to know. And neither did, and the lift did not move.*

> When I cannot. You are my grammar. You
> Stand where a verb should stand. I never said,
> "She's gone." I said, "My wife." The tense is wrong,
> And no one corrects me, for they are kind.
> I wear you as a man might wear a bandage,

---

The lights came on at ten fifty-two.

They came on with a great shudder, like a house waking. The lift gave a sob, and a lurch, and began to rise; the doors opened on the third floor, and a golden wedge of light fell in upon two people sitting very straight on opposite sides of the floor, each studying the ceiling.

And from the stair below, in four-part harmony, with great conviction, came Fábio Santos's choir, singing a carol which had been chosen, apparently, in the belief that it was Christmas in November.

"*Thiago!*" shouted a nurse. "What took so long?"

"A very old lock," said Thiago Oliveira, rising from his tool-box and brushing his moustache. He glanced at the elevator, in which a surgeon was standing up with great dignity and a physiotherapist was handing her a lost shoe, and in his face there was something that in any other man would have been tenderness and in Thiago was merely vanity. "They do not make them like that any more. I should say they were *made* for each other."

"Which lock?" said Fábio, a little confused.

"*Any* of them," said Thiago, and sailed off in search of his fee.

Beatriz stepped out first. She turned, in the doorway, and said, with perfect composure: "Seu Carlos. Thank you. I think we shall not need to speak of this again."

"No?"

"No." And then, a little more softly: "But I shall be at the rehab wing on Thursday, for a follow-up. A patient I am concerned about. If that is... not an imposition."

"It will be," said Carlos, "an honour."

"It is a *follow-up*," said Beatriz Lima, very sternly, and walked away down the corridor with her chin up, and her hand, which she did not know she was doing, pressed to the pocket where she had put his handkerchief.

### SCENE IV
*Osaka, Japan. Twelve hours ahead. It is eleven in the morning of the next day, and has been, here, all along. The ward of Fukushima, where the ramen shops put out their curtains like flags.*

There is an old joke, among those who live between time zones, that it is always tomorrow somewhere. In Osaka it was already the next morning, and in a narrow apartment above a stationer's a woman of thirty-three stood in her kitchen with a cup of tea she could not taste, looking at a phone.

Mei Sato had been a food critic for eleven years. She had written, for the *Naniwa Shimbun*, reviews in a cadence of salt and smoke, sweetness and acid, that readers called *a hymn in a bowl.* And last September, when a fever that should have been forgotten by Friday had gone, she had sat down to a plate of *tamagoyaki* and tasted nothing at all. It had been, she thought, like hearing the world's last piano go quiet.

She had told no one. She wrote now from memory. She was good at it. She could construct a review of any bowl in Osaka from the vocabulary of her past, and no one, so far, had known the difference. But she had stopped going out. And she had stopped, in particular, going to a very small ramen shop in an alley in Fukushima ward, eight stools, run by a taciturn man called Tanabe, where she had eaten, on the eighth stool, at the end, near the pipe where the steam rose, every Thursday for two years.

She had not been back since the new-moon night, when she had sat at his counter, said the one true thing, been fed, and gone home to the quiet of her own mouth. *One night of being fed is a kindness,* she thought. *Every night would be charity. And he would see it.*

On her phone, a friend had sent a link, with a single line: *Is this your Tanabe?*

She opened it.

A photograph. A counter. A bowl. An empty stool, the eighth, at the end, near the pipe.

The caption was in Portuguese, which she did not read. But beneath it ran an English translation, and she read that, very slowly, with her hand on the cold kitchen counter.

*He makes one bowl every night for a woman who stopped coming.*

She put the phone down.

Across the city, she knew, a man of thirty-four sat alone behind a counter in the clean quiet of a closed shop, skimming a pot he had begun at three in the morning. And between that man and a stranger's photograph, in a kitchen, with a cup of tea that tasted of nothing, stood the one woman for whom the bowl was made.

"Daichi," said a voice, in a shop across the city, to a historian who had arrived with a mobile phone and the news. "You are telling me that I am famous in *Brazil.*"

"A hundred and forty thousand hearts, Tanabe-san! A woman in Recife says you have ruined her mother's soup!"

Hiroshi Tanabe did not look at the phone. He did not look at the empty stool. He lifted the ladle, tasted the broth, adjusted nothing, and said, with the economy of a man who had spent one sentence a day for six months:

"Eat."

At the kitchen window of a flat above a stationer's, Mei Sato put on her coat.

She stood in the hall. She took it off.

She put it on again.

*Thursday,* she thought. *It is only Thursday. I will go on Thursday, and either I will tell him the rest of the truth, or I will tell him a lie, and either way I shall have been there.*

Far away, a boy called Leandro slept in Santos with the smell of cheese still on his sleeves, a surgeon sat in her car in a hospital garage with a man's handkerchief in her pocket, and a physiotherapist polished a ring on the hem of his coat.

And at a small shop on a narrow street, a bowl would be set that night at the eighth stool, by the pipe, and steam, as though it knew.
