## Chapter 43
### The Widow's Classroom

> *"I sell maize and I sell nothing of what I feel."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0662

---

### SCENE I
*Lusaka, Zambia. Soweto Market, seven in the morning, in the cold dry season, when the city wears a blanket of woodsmoke and the sun comes up the colour of a ripe mango.*

*Enter the Chorus, in a chitenge wrap, with a basket on its head.*

> Two cities in this chapter: one that drowns,
> One that is dry and dusty and awake.
> In one a woman sells her golden grain;
> In one a girl is told to stop, and stops.
> We shall not tell you which was kinder. Both
> Were hard, and both were holy. Both are ours.

The cold season in Lusaka has a sharpness at dawn that sends the whole market to the charcoal braziers, and at seven in the morning the great Soweto Market, bigger than some towns and a good deal louder, was a smoking maze of tarpaulin, tomatoes, secondhand shoes and shouting.

Grace Banda's stall stood in the third row from the east gate, between a man who sold blankets and a woman who sold buns.

It was a good stall. It had a painted sign, in blue letters going a little pale: **BANDA & WIFE — MAIZE, GROUNDNUTS, KAPENTA.** Three years ago the *Banda* had died, of a short sickness and a long hospital, and the *Wife* had kept the sign exactly as it was, for the reason that nobody could have said what else to put. She was thirty-five. She had a laugh like a drawer of coins being gently shut, a daughter named Chilufya, aged eight, who did her homework on a crate under the table, and a way of weighing out white maize in a battered tin cup so exactly that the whole row said she could weigh a man's soul in it.

She could sell anything to anyone. What she could not do was add.

That is, she could add to ten. She could count a tin of maize, and a coin, and a coin back. But the *books*, the figures of what came in and went out, who owed and who had paid, which her husband had kept in a green school exercise book, had gone into a drawer on the day of the funeral and never been opened. The stall had run three years on memory and goodwill, which are fine currencies until somebody asks for a receipt.

"*Muli bwanji, Mama Chilufya?*" said a man's voice.

"*Ndili bwino. Kaya inu, Ba Teacher?*"

"*Bwino, zikomo.*"

He came every Saturday. He was tall and narrow, in a pressed white shirt and a grey cardigan with a darned elbow, and he held the hand of a girl of ten and the hand of a boy of seven, one in each of his, as a man holds a pair of kites. This was Joseph Tembo, thirty-eight, schoolmaster of the Chilenje Community School, widower these four years. He was solemn, kind and courteous, and his pupils said he could silence a classroom by lifting an eyebrow and then make every child feel it had been their own idea.

"Three cobs, Mama," he said, in the Nyanja of the capital, which is the Nyanja of people who mean to be polite to everyone. "Roasted. And one for yourself."

"I do not need one for myself."

"It is the custom of the stall. The vendor must eat."

"That is not the custom of any stall, Ba Teacher."

"It is the custom," said Joseph gravely, "of *this one,* on Saturdays, by my own regulation."

She roasted the cobs on the brazier, turning them with long tongs, until the kernels split and blistered brown and the whole row smelled of summer. She gave him the sweetest, chosen by her own thumb, and charged him the right price; and he paid, as he always did, with a note that was a little too large, and she gave him his change, as she always did, which was exactly right. And they both stood a moment with the small silent trouble of two honest people who have each spent a year being scrupulous for the other's benefit.

"Mutale," said Grace to the girl, "you are tall."

"I am ten, Mama Chilufya."

"It is a very good age for being tall."

"Kondwani lost a tooth."

"He lost it *on purpose,*" said the boy, very grandly, and opened his mouth to show a gap like a small dark doorway.

Grace laughed, and Joseph looked at the cob in his hand as though it were a very difficult piece of arithmetic.

"Oh, ho, ho!" came a voice from the next stall, enormous, rich, and well-upholstered. "Look at the pair of them! A schoolmaster and a maize-seller. It is a very *Biblical* match. Man of letters and woman of the field. I have said it from the first."

"Loveness," said Grace.

"I am *merely* observing."

Loveness Zulu, thirty-eight, baker and proprietor of *Loveness's Heavenly Buns,* had such dazzling vanity and such tremendous warmth that the market had ceased to tell them apart. Her wig that week was a startling auburn; the week before it had been platinum, and the week before that a short severe black bob, "in mourning for my last hairdresser." She could not say goodbye, which was the market's chief entertainment. A conversation with Loveness began at seven and might be concluded, by the grace of God and the intervention of customers, at nine.

"I must go," she told Joseph now, and did not go. "Ba Teacher, a word from a woman who has seen more weddings than the Bishop. A man who buys three cobs and says he is buying them for his children is buying *four.* I know the sums. Goodbye. I am going. Mama Chilufya, he is a good man, I have *checked.* *Goodbye*—"

"Loveness," said Grace.

"I am gone."

"You are *still* here."

"That," said Loveness, with immense pride, "is because I have a very big heart, and it does not fit through the door." She swept off, in a wave of perfume and flour and auburn nylon, bellowing farewells at the next three stalls.

Joseph Tembo looked at the cob again. He did not say what he had meant to say. He had said nothing for a whole year, with the same grave good manners, and the list of things unsaid had grown very long.

"Mama Chilufya," he said at last.

"Yes, Ba Teacher."

"I teach a class, on Tuesday evenings. For adults. Reading, writing, and—" He paused. "Bookkeeping. The simple kind. For market people. There is no charge."

She looked at him. Grace wondered, for a moment, whether she was being offered a kindness or a pity. She decided, because he had said it as a *fact* and not a *favour,* that it was a fact.

"Who told you," said Grace, slowly, "that I could not add?"

"No one," said Joseph. "I saw your sign. It says *Banda and Wife.* It does not say *and Ledger.*"

"That," said Grace Banda, after a moment, "is the cruellest sentence I have ever heard, and it is also correct."

"Tuesday," said Joseph. "Six o'clock. Bring a pencil."

### SCENE II
*Manila. A narrow lane in Tondo, five nights after a typhoon. The water has gone down, but it has left a brown line on every wall at the height of a child's chin. A wake.*

Now across the world, to a house where nobody was speaking of arithmetic.

In the Philippines a death is not an event but a *season.* The body is not taken quickly. It is kept at home, in a white coffin with a glass lid, for five nights, seven, nine, while neighbours and cousins and strangers come and sit, and drink coffee out of paper cups, and play cards at folding tables in the lane all night, loudly, so that the family will not be alone with the silence. It is called the *lamay.*

The house was in a lane too narrow for a car. And yet there stood, in the lane, in the dark, with its lights off and a white sheet drawn over its nose, a jeepney.

Gabriel Santos had bought it at twenty-two, with a bank loan and his mother's earrings, and painted it from bumper to bumper in the colours of a carnival: red, yellow, a green parrot on the hood, a silver horse on each fender, a hundred small mirrors, and, in a sacred row above the windscreen, a number of pious slogans, as is the custom. *GOD IS GOOD. BLESS THIS RIDE. SAFE TRAVELS.*

Gabriel had been the cheerfullest driver in Tondo, and had never been seen without a hat, a joke, and a coin of change for the child who had lost hers. In the night of the typhoon, when the water came up the lanes like a brown tide and the whole neighbourhood stood on its roofs, he had driven his jeepney into the flood and made seven trips: forty-one neighbours, two goats, an old woman and her sewing machine. On the eighth, at the bridge, the current had taken the jeepney by the nose and turned it, gently, like a hand turning a page.

They had found the jeepney on the second day, in the creek. They had found Gabriel on the third.

*Nakikiramay po,* the neighbours said at the door, one after another, the old phrase: *We share your grief.* They said it to his mother, a small, silent woman with a white scarf; to his father, who sat in a corner with his hat in his lap; to his cousins, his friends, a hundred drivers who had come in white shirts, with their jeepneys, from every route in the city.

And they said it, softly, to a girl of twenty-six at the back of the room, who had not cried, who had come in the night straight from work with her headset still hanging around her neck, who stood with her back against the wall as though it were the only thing holding up the roof.

Maricel Dela Cruz was a call-centre agent. She spoke for six hours a night, in a bright clear American-adjacent voice, to strangers in Ohio and Perth and Leeds, about their phones and their insurance and their lost parcels. For those six hours she was **Maggie,** a name the company gave her, and she was never tired or afraid or sad. Every night at four in the morning she took off her headset and went home in a jeepney, and the jeepney was always *his.*

He had never charged her. She had never said that she knew.

"You must eat something," said a voice.

It was Joy Aquino, fifty-six, retired librarian, who had run the Tondo branch of the public library for thirty years and had taken on, for the duration of the wake, the management of all the grief in the lane. She had a clipboard and a rota, and a list of those to be told firmly to stop singing "My Way."

"I am not hungry, Ma'am."

"Of course you aren't. Eat anyway." She put a cup of coffee in Maricel's hands and a *pandesal* in her pocket. "I have a rule at the library, child. In a library you are quiet, because people are reading, and their reading is sacred. But in a *wake* you are not quiet. The dead like noise. They have the rest of time for silence."

"I do not know what to say, Ma'am."

"Then say nothing, and say it where people can see you. That is all a wake is." And she swept off toward the card table, where a very old woman was cheating at *tong-its* with an honesty so complete that nobody had the heart to stop her.

At midnight, Gabriel's best friend, a big shambling farmer from Cebu named Ariel Reyes, who had come up on the morning boat in his only suit, stood in the doorway and cleared his throat.

"Maricel," he said. "There is a thing."

"What thing?"

"He did not want you to see it until it was finished." Ariel's voice cracked. "He painted it last month. He said it was the best thing he had ever done. He said he would show you in the spring. He kept it under the sheet."

They went out into the lane. The whole wake came with them, a slow silent crowd, until the little street was full from wall to wall. Ariel took hold of the white sheet over the jeepney's nose, and pulled.

Above the windscreen of the jeepney, in a row of small gold letters, in a hand that was clearly Gabriel's, not a professional painter's, a little crooked and a little too large, were the words:

**PARA KAY MARICEL.**

In Tagalog, *para* has two meanings. It means *for.* And it means *stop.* A jeepney driver hears it a thousand times a day, when some passenger raps on the roof with a coin and cries *Para po!*, "Stop, please," and the driver brakes and lets the stranger down at the corner of her life.

*For Maricel. Stop, Maricel.*

Nobody said a word.

Then Maricel, who had not cried in five days, put her hand over her mouth, and made a sound that was not any sound she had ever used in her six hours of calls, and went down on her knees in the wet lane; and a hundred drivers in white shirts, who knew exactly what that word meant, took off their caps.

They buried him on the ninth day, in the afternoon, under an enormous grey sky. The hearse went first. Behind it, in a line a mile long, honking one long note, forty jeepneys followed in their carnival colours. And in the front seat of the last, with a farmer from Cebu at the wheel, a girl in a white blouse sat with her hands in her lap, in the seat that had been kept.

At the cemetery they passed the flower down the line, hand to hand to hand, a white chrysanthemum, in the old jeepney fashion: *Pakiabot po,* each one said to the next. *Kindly pass this forward.* It went from the farthest to the nearest, from stranger to stranger to friend, until it reached the coffin, and Maricel laid it there.

"*Pakiabot po,*" she said, to nobody. Her voice broke on the second word. "Kindly—pass it—"

"It has been passed," said Joy Aquino, softly, behind her. "It has reached him, child. That is the whole of the law."

### SCENE III
*Lusaka. The Chilenje Community School. A Tuesday evening, six o'clock, the sky already dark. Load-shedding: the power gone, the lake behind Kariba Dam being low. A paraffin lamp, six candles, a blackboard glimmering with chalk.*

There were nine pupils in the Tuesday class: a bus conductor, two market women, a retired railway clerk who wished to write his memoirs, a youth who wished to sell phones, and **Grace Banda,** at the back, in a school desk made for a child of twelve, with her knees nearly to her chin and a stub of pencil gripped like a trowel.

"Debit," said Joseph Tembo, in the candle's glow, "is what comes in. Credit is what goes out. Or the other way round. It does not matter, so long as you are *consistent.* At the bottom of the page is a line, and under the line a number, and the two columns must *agree.*"

"And if they do not?" said the bus conductor.

"Then somebody has been telling a lie," said Joseph. "Usually the person holding the pencil."

Grace did not laugh. She was looking at the chalked columns with terrible concentration. She had been afraid of this thing for three years, and tonight she had decided that it was only a *shape.*

"They must always agree?" she said.

"In bookkeeping, yes."

"In my life," said Grace Banda, "they have never once agreed."

Half the class thought it a joke. Joseph did not. He stood a moment with the chalk, and then said, very gently, "That is why we draw the line at the bottom, Mama Chilufya. Not to *make* them agree. To show how much they differ. So that you may see exactly what is owed, and by whom, and what is left."

"And what is left," said the old railway clerk, "is called the *balance.*"

"No," said Joseph. "It is called the *remainder.* The balance is only a word we use when we are being polite."

It was an hour before they finished. At the door, in the dark, among the departing chitenge and torches, he put a small exercise book into her hands.

"What is this, Ba Teacher?"

"Your ledger," said Joseph. "It is blank. I have ruled the columns. Those are things a stranger might do." He looked at his own shoes. "The first line is for you."

She opened it. In the glow of a borrowed torch, on the first line, in his small clear schoolmaster's hand, he had written one word: *Grace.* And beside it, in the debit column, nothing. And in the credit column, nothing.

"Both columns are empty," said Grace.

"They are *yours,*" said Joseph. "You will fill them. I did not like to presume what is owed."

She did not answer. She held the ledger against her chest, as one holds a thing that might otherwise blow away, and went home through the dark, past the shuttered shops and the glowing braziers, saying to herself, quite silently, all the way: *Debit. Credit. Balance. Remainder.*

And at a kitchen table four streets away, by one stub of a candle and the great noise of the Lusaka night, a widowed schoolmaster marked forty exercise books and, without noticing, began to think in verse.

> *I marked a hundred pages, and my hand
> Went stubborn at the margin of the ninety-first.
> A woman wrote, in letters like a child's,
> The sum of two plus two, and made it five,
> And I could not correct it. Let it stand.
> Four years I've kept a house of silences,
> A tidy house, with two small kites to fly.
> I thought the ledger of my heart was closed:
> That what was owed had long ago been paid.
> But here a woman, awkward at a desk,
> Has opened me by adding up a column.
> I do not know what I would say to her.
> I know that in the morning I shall go
> And buy three cobs, and not know what to do
> With my own hands, which hold three cobs, and four.*

### SCENE IV
*A night, a week later. An office in Manila where six hundred headsets glow in rows. Four in the morning, local time. A girl at a desk, on her break, with a cold coffee and a phone.*

It was Joy Aquino who had told her. "There is an answer," she had said, on the telephone, in her library voice. "Someone has replied to your Register letter, child. They have posted it publicly. I thought you ought to see it, and not alone."

So Maricel sat, in a break room that smelled of microwave noodles and carpet cleaner, in the small hour of the morning in which she had always been strangest, and read.

> **To Entry 0045 — from Lusaka, Zambia.**
>
> *Dear Maricel,*
>
> *I do not know you. I am a woman who sells maize in a market. I have read what you wrote, that you speak six hours a night to strangers and never to the one you wished to. I do not know how it ended. A friend told me it ended with water.*
>
> *I am a widow. Three years now. I sell maize, and I sell nothing of what I feel, and I cannot tell you that it gets better, because I do not know that this is true. What I can tell you is what I have learned this month, from a man who teaches me to add. It is that there is a line at the bottom of every page, and under it, a number, which is called the remainder.*
>
> *You are the remainder, Maricel. That is not a small thing. It is the only thing the page is for.*
>
> *Your six hours are not wasted. Every stranger who rang you was held, for those few minutes, by somebody kind.*
>
> *Say what you cannot say. Say it to the page. Say it to the water.*
>
> *— Grace Banda, Soweto Market, third row from the east gate.*

She read it twice. She did not cry; she had cried herself dry at the cemetery. She sat for a long while, with her coffee going colder in her hand, and then the little light on her desk, which meant a call, began to blink.

She put on her headset. She drew a breath, and found her bright, clear, practised voice, the voice of a woman called Maggie, and then, somewhat to her own surprise, set it down, as one sets down a heavy bag in a hallway, and spoke instead in her own.

"Thank you for calling," said Maricel Dela Cruz. "My name is Maricel. I am sorry. I am a little—I have had a hard month. How may I help you?"

There was a silence on the line, from the other side of the world: a man in a kitchen in Ohio, where it was a quiet afternoon, with a broken phone and a bad week. Then the man, gently and a little uncertainly, said:

"Take your time, miss. I've got all afternoon."

*Para,* thought Maricel, with her whole body, and for the first time in a week it was a word that meant *rest.*
