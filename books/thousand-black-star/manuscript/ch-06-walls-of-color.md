## Chapter 6
### Walls of Color

> *"Your face is on a hundred walls and you do not know."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0299

---

Long after the dead have been fed, Mexico City still smells of marigolds.

They are everywhere: heaped in the Jamaica market in orange mountains, crushed in the gutters of the Roma, caught in the grilles of bus shelters and the hair of small girls, and weeks after the last candle has guttered on the last altar, their bitter gold perfume goes on hanging in the thin high air, as if the city were not yet quite sure that the visitors had gone home.

It was in this season, upon a scaffold of rusted pipe on the Calle Zacatecas, in the blue hour before dawn, that a young man named Rafael Ortega stood with a bucket of cobalt blue in his left hand, a radio blasting a ranchera into the whole sleeping neighbourhood, and the end of a very large nose upon his right.

It was the nose of a woman. It was thirty feet high. It was one of a hundred and three.

"She has the *worst* nose," said Marisol García, holding the ladder, from the street. "It's too long, Rafa. She looks like a heron who has read a lot."

"It is the nose of a *lawyer.* A woman who reads like that is going to be a lawyer or a saint. She had a book as thick as a loaf. She was frowning at it as though it had insulted her mother." He leaned back, with one foot on a plank and the other on empty air. "And she was *right.* Whatever the book had said."

"You saw her once, at the Parque México, in the rain, two Novembers ago. And once in a dream."

"The dream," said Rafael, "was more *detailed.*"

He was twenty-six, loud, broad in the shoulder and long in the leg, with a mop of black hair stiff with a decade of paint and a pair of eyes in which every emotion arrived at once and nobody was ever in charge. He painted as other men argue: with his whole body, at the top of his lungs. In two years he had covered, without a permit, without a sponsor, and without, as far as anyone could tell, any plan, a hundred and three walls of the capital with a single face.

It was always the same. A young woman on a bench, reading. Her hair fell forward over one shoulder; her mouth was set in a line of ferocious concentration; her head was bowed, and her eyes, upon every one of the hundred and three, were *closed.* The critics called it *La Lectora.* The children called it *La Señorita Que No Se Cansa.* The city had begun to leave small offerings at the foot of her bigger walls: a marigold; a pencil; once, on the Calle Chihuahua, a copy of the Civil Code.

"And why," said Marisol, who was forty-five, and a painter, and had known him since he was a boy with chalk on his knees, "are the *eyes* shut?"

Rafael did not answer for a moment. A truck went past. On the radio, a man sang of a girl who had broken his heart in Guadalajara.

"Because I do not know," he said at last, "what colour they are."

---

At the end of the block, in the thin first light, there was a policeman.

He was a very fine policeman. He had a moustache of formidable architecture, a belt that creaked with importance, and a profile that he turned, wherever he stood, slightly to the left, so that the world might benefit from its better side. His name was Alejandro Hernández. He was forty-five and had been making Rafael Ortega's life a misery for two years, with that peculiar tenderness that exists between a man and the thing he is obliged to fine.

"*Ortega!*" called the officer. "Article forty-one of the Civic Culture Law! Unauthorised alteration of a building!"

"It is a *wall,* Commandante!"

"It is the property of a private citizen!"

"The private citizen has asked me to do it. He is eighty. He is weeping with joy. He has given me a plate of *tamales.*"

"The tamales will be taken into evidence."

"They are in my stomach."

"Then your stomach will be taken into evidence." But the policeman's eye had strayed up to the wall, as it always did, as it had done on the hundred and two previous walls; and as always he found himself regarding the great painted face with an expression in which severity and something else contended. He cocked his head. He turned his profile. He regarded the line of the painted jaw.

"That *jaw,*" said Alejandro Hernández, slowly.

"What of it?"

"It is a very strong jaw."

"It is a woman's jaw, Commandante."

"I have been told," said the policeman, "I have a similar jaw. I have been told that by a number of people, including the *barber.*" He folded his arms. "I have the impression, Ortega, over two years, that you have been painting a *portrait.* And I do not wish to say that I believe it is *me.* I only wish to observe that the resemblance is *close.*"

"Commandante. It is a *girl.*"

"A girl with my jaw."

"She is twenty-five."

"A *young* girl," said Alejandro Hernández, with a shiver of indignant satisfaction, "with *my* jaw." He took out his citation book. He wrote, for a long time. Then he tore the sheet out and handed it up on the end of his baton, which was long enough, and said gruffly, like one who has had a lot to bear and very little to say: "That is a fine for the scaffold. For the *painting,* I make no comment."

"Thank you."

"I am saving money to disappear, you know," said the Commandante, staring into the middle distance. "To a small village in Oaxaca. To keep goats. I tell this to no one."

"You have just told it to a street."

"It is a *private* street," said the policeman, and walked away down it, with a tread that was faintly unsteady, and his profile turned to the left.

Three kilometres to the south, in an apartment in the Colonia Narvarte with a map of the capital pinned to the kitchen wall, a young woman was losing an argument with her best friend, and enjoying it.

"It is *circumstantial,*" said Camila Reyes.

"It is a hundred and three walls," said Lupita Hernández, who was a chef at a restaurant in Xochimilco, twenty-five, round-faced, unalarmable, and had arrived at six in the morning with a pan of *chilaquiles* and a phone. "You have been to ninety-one. You keep the list in a *drawer.*"

"I keep the list *for research.*"

"On what?"

"On the phenomenon of *public portraiture.*"

"Cami. The woman has your nose."

"A nose is not evidence of identity."

"Show me a heron," said Lupita, "who reads the Constitution, in a coat, with *that* chin."

Camila Reyes, twenty-five, fourth-year student of law at the National University, and the most disciplined human being in the south of the city, shut her eyes. She was a small, plain, intense young woman with her hair twisted up in a pencil, a flat voice that she used on judges, and a heart that she kept, by long habit, in the same drawer as the list. She had lived all her life by the principle that a fact is a fact and a feeling is an *allegation.* She had noticed the first wall two Decembers ago, on the Avenida Álvaro Obregón, when she was walking home with her books, and had stopped dead in the middle of the pavement, and had stood there for twenty minutes with a bag of oranges, because a thirty-foot woman with her own mouth was reading her own case-book, in the rain, with her eyes shut.

She had been very *angry.* It had taken her a month to understand why: it is uncomfortable to be seen by a stranger, and seen *accurately.*

"Read me his," said Lupita softly.

Camila took the phone.

*"Your face is on a hundred walls and you do not know."*

"He's wrong," said Camila.

"Is he?"

"I *do* know."

"I know you know. *He* doesn't."

"And mine?" said Camila, very low. "Show me mine."

*Entry 0121 — Mexico City, Mexico.*
*"I have walked to every wall to find the hand that painted me."*

She had written it at three in the morning, after the eighty-ninth wall, and posted it to England in a general despair. It was a very unlawyerly sentence, with no conditionals, no reservations and no precedent: the single honest line in her entire body of written work.

"There is a new wall," said Lupita, scrolling, with the finger of a conspirator. "On Zacatecas, in the Roma. It went up *last night.* The one that was at the top of the page. Everyone says it is the biggest. Everyone says—" she paused "—it has a *tag.*"

"A *what?*"

"A line at the bottom. Hand-lettered. It says, *To the one who reads: I have not finished your eyes.*"

Camila Reyes stood perfectly still, in her kitchen, with a map on the wall behind her and a hundred and three pins in it, and a plate of chilaquiles going cold.

"What time," she said, "is the light?"

"Half past six," said Lupita, who was a chef and knew about mornings. "He paints at first light."

"It is ten to six."

"I know."

"I am going to need a coat."

"I have brought you," said Lupita, who had been right in advance, "*two.*"

**Dhaka, 22:41. Yogyakarta, 23:41.**

*(Between the two cities and the two walls: a small thing, a pulse of light. Let us listen.)*

In the corner of a lane in Mirpur, under a tin awning thick with the smoke of evening stoves, a tea-stall boy named Bunny, thirteen, was explaining the art of the *cha* to a customer who had not asked.

"The secret," said Bunny, "is not the leaf. The secret is the *wait.* You must let it sit exactly as long as it takes a man to decide whether he is going to be a coward. And then you pour. Here, Bhaiya."

Fayaz Rahman, twenty-six, took the glass with the weary courtesy of someone just home from a fourteen-hour day on a contract paid in a currency not his own. He had walked here from his flat with a laptop that smelled of hot dust, and he sat on a bench with his sleeves down and his shoes grey with dust, looking at his phone without looking at it, as men do who have been waiting for a week without admitting it.

For eight nights he had read the Register on his phone. He had read the line beside his own, and then he had read it again. He had not written to the girl who had written it. He had begun, perhaps forty times. He had typed *Dear* and deleted it; he had typed *Hello* and deleted it; he had once typed *I don't wish to intrude* and had deleted that so hard the whole screen went white. Politeness, in an hour of need, offers so many ways of not saying it.

And then the phone, in his hand, in the lane, under the tin and the smoke, gave a small pale shiver.

It was not a ring. It was only the tiniest of lights, an envelope, a name.

> **Ermis Larasati:** Hello, Dhaka. You are one hour behind. I am not sorry about it.

He read it.

He read it a second time, which is to say, he went on reading it, though the sentence had long since ended, as a man goes on holding a door after the guest has come in.

Behind his shoulder, in the steam of the stall, Bunny, who was a boy of the most spotless discretion and no manners whatsoever, had planted his chin.

"*Bhaiya,*" said Bunny, in a hushed and terrible voice.

"Do not read it."

"I was not reading it. I was *visiting.*"

"She is a *stranger.*"

"She is a stranger," said Bunny, "who has an hour to *spare* and has *sent* it to you. That is not an ordinary thing. Say *hello* with *dignity.*"

"I am a person," said Fayaz, with a faint despairing smile, "who apologises to a vending machine."

He did not say that. He typed, in the end, a thing that took him three minutes and cost him a pound of nerves and read, when it was done, exactly like the work of a man who had spent three minutes on it, which is to say, honestly:

> **Fayaz Rahman:** Sorry. (Reflex. It was not about the hour.) Hello, Yogyakarta. I would tell you everything, if I were free to. May I tell you a little?

He looked at it. His thumb hovered. A bead of steam ran off the awning and down his wrist.

He sent it.

The word *delivered* appeared. The word *seen* appeared beneath it, almost at once, small and grey and merciless.

And now it was her turn. She sat, in her workshop, an hour ahead of him, at the exact minute a week since the old astronomer's margin had pencilled them side by side; at 23:41, by the clock above the door, with the stove burnt down and the wax gone cold and a copper pen lying idle in its dish; and beside her, on the rail of the window, eyeing her with a cold and bottomless suspicion, a large green bird.

"*TOO LATE,*" said Ayu.

"Hush."

"*TOO LATE. BEAUTIFUL.*"

"I said hush!" said Ermis, and laughed, once, with her hand to her mouth, like a girl caught at a keyhole. The phone was shaking in her hand. She looked at what he had written. *Sorry. Reflex.* She looked at it again.

It was not what she had expected. She had expected something cool: a *Thank you for your message.* She had not expected an apology for a thing he had not done, nor that it would feel so *exactly* like the thing she had been listening for.

She typed. She deleted. She typed.

She did not draw a flower on the corner this time. She simply wrote, in the Latin letters she used for clients and for strangers, and sent it before she could think better of her own hand:

> **Ermis Larasati:** A little is how every pattern starts. Begin.

And then, because it was a night in which courage was in the air like a smell of marigolds, and nobody was watching but a parrot, she put down the phone and laid her forehead for a moment against the cold edge of the table, and said, very softly, in Javanese, a word that was only a name.

*Three lines,* the reader will say. *That is all?* It is the whole of the matter: a lamp lit, a hand raised, a small, stiff, honourable reply. The great speeches are seldom long. They fit upon a telephone, and are said at night, across an hour.

**Calle Zacatecas, Colonia Roma. Half past six.**

The light came up over the Roma as it comes up over every Mexican morning: not as a dawn, but as an *announcement,* the whole east flushing from lemon to rose to a gold so fierce it seemed to have been purchased. The pigeons rose off the roofs in a clatter.

And on the pavement, below the longest and newest of the hundred and three walls, in a borrowed coat, with her hair coming out of its pencil and her heart, which she had kept in a drawer, now beating in her ears like a fist, stood a young woman looking up.

It was *her.* It was, without any possibility of doubt, her. There was the nose; there was the dreadful chin. There was the exact way her hair fell forward over her left shoulder; the way her mouth was set; the little frown, between the brows, of a woman who reads as though the page might be taken away. And all thirty feet of it was alive with colour: rust and violet and a blue like the first hour of the night; and the whole face, for the first time, was not looking down, but *up*; the book was lowered; the head was lifted, as if at some sound; and the eyes were still shut, and were *waiting.*

Beneath, in a long ragged line of yellow capitals, a man had written:

**TO THE ONE WHO READS: I HAVE NOT FINISHED YOUR EYES.**

Up on the scaffold, thirty feet above the traffic, with a brush in his hand and a bucket of ochre in the crook of his elbow, a young man with a mop of paint-stiff hair had stopped in the middle of a stroke. He had seen a woman come into the street. He did not know her. He knew only that she was standing exactly where the wall was best seen from; that she had put her hands in her pockets; that she was looking up at his work as though it had said something *libellous* about her mother.

"*Hey,*" said Rafael Ortega, from the sky.

"You've given me a nose like a *heron,*" said Camila.

For a moment nothing moved upon the Calle Zacatecas. The ranchera on the radio had gone on to its second verse. A dog barked.

Then the scaffolding, which had been designed by an optimist, creaked; and Rafael Ortega began, with a haste that put every regulation in the country to shame, to come down it.

He came down the last six feet by a means known only to painters and the fallen. He landed in the street, with paint to the elbows and a smear of ochre across his forehead, and stood before her with his arms at his sides and his mouth open, for once with nothing in it.

"I—" said Rafael.

"Camila Reyes."

"I—"

"Fourth year of law. Narvarte. I have been to ninety-one of your walls." She said it as one reads a charge. "I have eaten bad *tortas* at six in the morning in eleven different colonias. I have been *stared at* by a man in a moustache. I have stood beneath my own face in the rain for two years, and I would like to know, Mr. Ortega, *why you never finished the eyes.*"

He opened his mouth. He closed it. Something went across his face that was too large for it, like a weather.

> *Because I would not guess. A hundred times
> I stood before a wall with that same brush
> And could have made them brown, or black, or grey,
> Or any colour that would make a pleasing
> Picture; and each time I could not lie.
> I saw you once. You looked up once. You were
> Not looking at me, but you looked, and it
> Was gone, a second, a grey-green, a ring
> Of something. I do not know. I would not make
> The thing I did not know. I'd rather paint
> A hundred closed doors than one false window.*

"Brown," said Camila.

"What?"

"They are brown. With a ring of green round the pupil. It is called *hazel,* and it's a very ordinary colour, and I am rather ashamed of it." Her voice was almost steady. "I have never liked them. You will find them very *dull.*"

"Dull?"

"They do not *do* anything."

"Camila Reyes," said Rafael, staring at her eyes with the unashamed attention of someone starved for two years, "I have been looking at them for four seconds, and I would like it to be known that they have *already* done *everything.*"

It is unseemly to burst into tears in the Colonia Roma at six in the morning, in front of a stranger, a pigeon and a hundred and fifty gallons of paint. She did it nonetheless: a short, furious sob, like a flood out of a locked cellar, and then another, and then she laughed, and put both hands over her face.

He held out a brush.

She looked at it through her fingers. It was a thick, soft, fat-bellied brush, wet with ochre-brown and a little green.

"You do it," said Rafael.

"I cannot paint."

"You cannot be *wrong* about your own eyes."

"It is thirty feet up."

"There is a scaffold."

"It was built by an optimist."

"I am an optimist," said Rafael. "I will hold the ladder."

She climbed. It took her eleven minutes, and the ladder swayed, and a bus-driver, passing, sounded his horn in the manner of a Greek chorus; and at the top, in the cold sweet gold of the morning, with the whole slumbering city spread below her like a quilt, a small woman in a borrowed coat stood on a plank at the level of her own face and laid, with a hand that shook only a little, a single brown arc beneath the lid.

And the painted woman opened her eyes.

Far down in the street, a policeman, who had returned for the *tamales,* stopped dead, with a half-written citation in his hand. He looked up at the wall. He looked at the two small figures on the plank, the girl and the boy, with the great closed face blooming between them into sight.

"*Unauthorised alteration,*" said Alejandro Hernández, faintly, to the empty street.

Then he put the citation book in his pocket, took off his cap, and, turning his profile for once away from the light, stood with his hand to his heart like a man at an anthem.

"Article forty-one," said the Commandante, "is *suspended.*"
