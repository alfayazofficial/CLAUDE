## Chapter 12
### Flour and Violets

> *"I still bake my wife's bread every Sunday."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0844

---

### SCENE I
*Lyon, France. The Croix-Rousse, the steep hill of the silk-weavers. Sunday, a quarter to four in the morning. A fog from the two rivers has climbed the hill and is looking in at the windows.*

At the hour when the honest are asleep and the dishonest are, for once, at a loss, a baker was talking to a lump of dough.

"You are too wet," said Pierre Dubois. "It is the weather. Do not take it personally."

He was forty-five, large and stooped and flour-white to the elbow, with a nose that had been broken at rugby and a pair of hands that had never in their lives been idle. He had inherited the shop from his father and the habit of silence from his mother, and had added, by his own labour, a reputation for the best *pain de campagne* on the hill and the worst temper. He kneaded with his whole back, in the yellow light of the oven, in the warm, sweet dark of a bakery before dawn, which is the nearest thing on earth to the inside of a heart.

It was Sunday. On Sundays he made *the other bread.*

It was a round loaf, dark-crusted, with walnuts and a little rye and a pinch of cumin that no one else in Lyon would have dared, and it had been invented in the kitchen of a small flat in the Rue Imbert-Colomès by a woman called Odile, who had died four years ago in the spring of a thing that had taken eleven months to say its name. She had baked it every Sunday of their marriage. He had never in his life made it until the first Sunday after she was gone, when he had woken at four, and found his hands in the flour, and understood that they had decided without him.

He had never sold it. He gave it away. Every Sunday, before the first church bell, a boy of thirteen came on a bicycle, and took twelve loaves in a basket, and delivered them to the old and the lonely of the hill, who had no idea who sent them, and who ate them in silence, with butter, as if they were an answer to a prayer.

*I still bake my wife's bread every Sunday,* he had written, to a stranger, in the dark. *I do not know how to stop. I do not think I want to.*

"Pierre."

He turned. The boy was in the doorway: Baptiste Lefèvre, thirteen, a thin, severe child in an oversized anorak, with a school satchel, a bicycle helmet, and the expression of a very small and uncompromising judge.

"You are early."

"I am never early. *You* are late."

"It is not yet four."

"It is *five to,*" said Baptiste, "and you have flour on your face. It is the look of a man who has been thinking."

"Go and sit down."

"I do not sit. I *witness.*" He looked about the shop with narrowed eyes. "Has it come yet?"

Pierre's hands stopped in the dough.

"Has what come?"

"The thing," said Baptiste, with the impatience of the young explaining the obvious to the old, "that is on your step every Sunday. A little before four. I am a boy who gets up. I have *seen.*"

---

For eleven weeks, on Sunday mornings, a small bunch of violets had been laid at the baker's door.

They were Parma violets, the sweet-scented double kind that Lyon's gardeners coax into bloom under glass in the cold months: a dozen blooms, tight as a clenched fist, tied with a grey ribbon, in a twist of brown paper. They had no card. They had no name. They lay on the worn stone step in the first light, damp with the fog and smelling of a spring that was months away, and Pierre, who found them each week when he opened the shutter, had begun, to his own annoyance, to look forward to the finding.

He did not know who. He had asked. He had put it, in his gruff and wounded manner, to every female customer who had come in that month: "Madame, did you leave some flowers?" and had been given, in return, a series of long stares of the kind that a woman gives a man who has just asked whether she is in love with him.

Now he put down the dough and went to the door.

He opened it a crack, as one opens the door on a stranger, and the fog came in, white and cold, and with it, curling through the cold, faint as a note from a distant room: *violets.*

On the step lay a small twist of brown paper.

He looked up. Down the steep hill, at the corner, in the lamplight, for a single moment, a figure in a long grey coat was walking away, quickly, with its head down, past the shuttered bank and the tobacconist's, toward the river and the flower market. A woman. He saw the line of her shoulder, and a scarf the colour of a pigeon's wing, and then the fog closed round her like a hand.

He did not call out. His throat had forgotten how.

"It is the florist," said Baptiste, at his elbow.

Pierre turned. "*What?*"

"Madame Laurent. From the Rue de la Martinière. The one with the sad shop." The boy shrugged, with magnificent indifference. "I do not know why. She leaves them and she looks at the window and she goes. I did not tell you, because I was not asked, and I am not good at apologising when I am wrong."

"You were not *wrong.*"

"I might have been. It is a risk."

*Mark this, reader.* There is a great deal of wisdom in thirteen-year-olds, and nearly all of it is used to avoid being embarrassed.

Pierre stood a long moment with the violets in his hand. Then he went back inside, and wiped his palms, and took from the cooling rack the last loaf of the morning, the dark round one with the walnuts, and wrapped it in a clean cloth.

"I have always baked twelve," he said gruffly. "Today you will take thirteen."

"To whom?"

"To the Rue de la Martinière. Say nothing."

"I never do," said Baptiste, "unless I am not asked."

---

*At the other end of the hill, in a very small shop, a woman in a long grey coat had just come in out of the fog, and was hanging up her scarf with a hand that was not quite steady.*

Margaux Laurent was forty-two, a florist, a widow of three years' standing, with soft brown hair going silver at the temple, and a way of pausing before she spoke as though she were arranging the sentence in a vase. Her husband Luc had been a violin-maker and had died quickly, in the middle of a Tuesday, and she had been left with a shop called *Les Violettes,* an unpaid lease, and a house that was too quiet by half. She was gentle and humorous and exceedingly shy, and had, eleven weeks ago, done the most daring thing of her life.

She had been walking to the flower market in the dark. She had passed the bakery on the Croix-Rousse, where the lamp was lit; and she had looked, for no reason, through the window, and seen a large man with his arms in a trough of dough, and his shoulders shaking.

It is a thing we are not supposed to see. She had stood there for a full minute in the fog, with violets in her basket meant for the market. And then she had done what a florist does, which is to know when a thing is not a bunch of flowers but a sentence.

She laid them on the step. She walked away.

*I left the flowers at your door,* she had written, afterwards, to an old woman in England, with the kettle going cold. *It was always me.*

Now she stood in her little shop among the zinc buckets and the paper cones and the smell of wet green stalks, and said to her assistant, who happened to be her twenty-year-old brother, another Baptiste (the hill, it must be said, had an inordinate number of them), and who was at that moment arranging chrysanthemums in a manner she found personally offensive: "He saw me."

"Who?"

"The baker."

"Did he *speak* to you?"

"He did not speak. He *stood.*" She sank onto a stool. "He looked at me through a fog. I have not been looked at through a fog since nineteen-ninety-nine."

"What do you intend?"

"To go on," said Margaux, gently and appalled, "and to be a coward at the same time. It is a skill. I have been practising it for three years."

The shop bell rang. A thin boy in an oversized anorak stood in the door with a basket, and in it, wrapped in a clean cloth, one round dark loaf, warm still, and smelling of walnuts and a pinch of something that no one else in Lyon would have dared.

"From a witness," said Baptiste Lefèvre, and set it on the counter, and fled.

Margaux Laurent looked at the loaf. She broke it. The steam rose, and she put her face into it, as one puts one's face into a flower.

### SCENE II
*Kraków, Poland. The workshop of a clockmaker on Floriańska Street, a long dark room that ticks, as a forest breathes, with a hundred small lives. Noon. The great bugle of St. Mary's Church is about to sound from the tower.*

In Kraków, at every hour, a trumpeter climbs to the window of the tallest tower in the square, and plays a short, old, sad tune to the four corners of the earth; and at the exact moment the tune would reach its end, it stops.

It is cut in the middle of a note, by tradition, because on one afternoon long ago the player on the tower was shot in the throat by a Tatar arrow while sounding the alarm, and the city has kept the tune unfinished ever since, in his honour. So that every hour, in the clear air above the square, a stranger may hear a thing that stops mid-sentence. The people of Kraków have been trained for centuries to bear a sentence that stops.

"It is noon," said Jakub Nowak, without lifting his eyes from the movement before him.

"I know," said Zofia Kowalska. "I heard it."

*She had not.*

The reader may keep that, with the rest of the day's secrets, for later.

Jakub was twenty-four, the apprentice of the oldest clockmaker in the street, a precise and gentle young man, fair-haired and long-fingered, with a loupe pushed up into his hair and the habit of holding his breath when he worked, as though the watch under his hands might wake. He had learned in four years that a clock is only a promise to go on, and that everything in the trade follows from the discovery that one must not hurry a promise.

Zofia was twenty-three, a violinist in her last year at the Academy of Music, a slight, luminous girl with a mass of dark hair and a face of such fine and shadowed attention that people, on meeting her, tended to lower their voices. Six weeks ago she had left in the shop a silver pocket-watch wrapped in a handkerchief, with a worn look about the eyes, and said: "It stopped four years ago, at eleven fifty-two. It was my grandfather's." The master had given the job to his apprentice. And Jakub had opened the case, and read, engraved inside in a tiny copperplate hand: *Zofia, for her birth.*

He had mended it. It was a *minute repeater,* a rare thing, the kind of watch that chimes the hour and the quarter and the minute on two tiny silver gongs at the touch of a slide; and he had sat for six weeks of evenings, with a light and a loupe, restoring it, and when it was done, on the last night of October, he had thought, with no reason at all: *Zofia. It is the name of the girl who plays the violin under the tower.* For he had heard her. Every Thursday at dusk, at the foot of the church, in a thin red scarf, she played for coins. He had stood across the square every Thursday for a year with his hands in his pockets, and had never once put a coin in her case, because he could not trust his face.

And now she had come to collect it, at noon, and was holding out his own work as though it were only hers.

"It is ready," said Jakub. "It is a repeater. It will chime. Listen."

He pressed the slide.

The tiny silver chime was bright as a drop of water on glass: high notes for the hour, then the quarters, then the minutes, as precise as a heartbeat. It filled the shop; it was the sweetest sound a clockmaker knows; and he watched her face as it rang, and saw it do a thing he did not understand.

It went very still. It went, for one moment, the stillness of a person listening to a door that is not opening. And then she smiled, a bright, composed, practised smile, and said:

"It is perfect."

He frowned. "You heard all of it?"

"*Of course.*"

"The last four notes? They are very high."

"They are very beautiful," said Zofia, with the smile fixed, and he did not press her; for he was twenty-four, and a gentleman, and a man who had learned that a person who says a thing very quickly is generally not asking to be examined.

*She had heard the first three notes. She had not heard the rest.*

*It is a small matter, the top of a scale, in a young woman's left ear. She had noticed it in September. She had told no one. She had told herself that it was the damp.*

---

"You play," said Jakub, a little later, after he had named a price she could pay and she had pretended to argue.

"How do you know?"

"You hold your hands like a person carrying a bowl."

"I am a violinist."

"I know. I have heard you." He went red from the collar upward. "I mean, I have *heard of* you. At the tower. On Thursdays."

"You have *heard* me?"

"I have a window," said Jakub, desperately.

She laughed. It was a clear, surprised, delighted sound, and for a moment the clocks seemed to quiet to hear it. "You are a very strange man," said Zofia. "You mend a watch that is not yours and you listen to a girl from a window. Is that a profession?"

"It is *horology.*"

"I am sure it is." She stood up, glancing about the long ticking room, and her eyes fell on the back wall. "Is that a piano?"

It was. It stood in the corner, an old upright pianino of walnut with a missing candle-bracket, which had belonged to the master's wife, and on which Jakub, in the long dull afternoons, with the door shut, played the songs his grandmother had sung to him in Nowy Targ.

"It is out of tune."

"Everything is out of tune. Play me something."

"I do not—I am not—"

"Play me *anything,*" said Zofia, "and I will do the rest."

---

*So it was that on the twenty-ninth day of November, at a quarter past noon, in a clockmaker's shop in the most beautiful medieval city in Europe, with a hundred clocks ticking and a very old piano missing its candle, a boy and a girl played the first duet of their lives.*

It was a simple tune: an old Polish lullaby, four bars and a turn. He played the chords, in his careful, even, gentle way, as a man lays bricks. She took the violin from its worn case and tucked it under her chin and closed her eyes, and drew the bow, and the shop was *filled.*

I cannot describe it; I have only words. It was as though a window had been opened in a closed room, and a bird had flown in. She played the way a river runs. And he, bent over the keys, looking for the first time in his life at nothing but the notes, felt his chest do a thing that was not on any list of the diseases of the heart.

She did not once look at him. She played with her eyes shut, her whole body bent toward the piano, her head a little turned.

*Turned. He saw it, in the end, with the unforgiving eye of a man who takes engines apart: she had turned her right ear toward the piano, and her left away.*

*She was following his hands.*

He did not know what it meant. He knew only that when he slowed, a hair, in the second bar, because he had caught his breath, she slowed an instant before him, as though she had *seen* the beat, rather than heard it; and that in the high register, in the turn, when she should have lifted the line, her bow dipped, ever so slightly, and found the note by the width of a hair.

*A clock does not lie,* thought Jakub. *Nor does a person who plays by sight.*

And in the shop behind them a dozen clocks struck the quarter at once, in a bright, scattered chorus, and *she did not hear it.*

### SCENE III
*Lisbon, Portugal. A tram, the 28, climbing the hills of Graça and Alfama in the rain. Evening. The tiles shine like the sides of fishes.*

The old yellow tram ground up the Rua da Graça with a noise like a bad conscience, and in the cab, behind the driver, on a folding seat, an engaged man was telling an engine that he loved it.

"Not *you,*" Tomás Villaverde hastened to explain, patting the great brass controller. "You are a friend. I mean *her.* I have asked her. She has said yes. It is a matter of the postal service."

"You have said so four times," said Nuno Pereira, his friend and fellow mechanic, from the other end of the cab.

"It is a matter of the *postal service,* Nuno. I proposed, as everything between us has happened, by *post.* I wrote it in a very good letter, in the correct format, I sent it to *Senhora Inês Carvalho, Rua das Flores* (a street I have never seen), and the post, in the person of Inês, with her own hands, delivered it to *herself.*"

"And she said?"

"She said she had read it twice. She reads everything twice. And then she took out a pen and signed the back, and *returned* it to the sender." He took out a creased, much-handled sheet. On the bottom, in a small round hand, it said: *Yes. Delivered. — I.C.*

It was the best receipt he had ever been issued.

At the bottom of the hill, in the rain, Pereira the lamp-seller was holding forth to a captive passenger. "I sold her a lamp, you know. A small one, for the table. She said it was for her mother. It was for the *letter.* To read it by. A person must have good light for a thing like that. It is the principal duty of my trade, and no one has ever thanked me."

Inês Carvalho, at that moment, was in her tiny flat above the sorting-office, with a small lamp on the table, and a ring that was not a ring, because he could not afford one yet, but a small loop of copper wire from a tram's motor, that he had bent into a circle with his own pliers and slipped on her finger with the solemnity of a bishop. She sat with it before her in the light. Across the table, her sister Rita, thirty-two, a journalist, was looking at her with an expression of the most complete and unrepentant smugness.

"You *sent* it," said Inês. "The first letter. To the street that did not exist. It was *you.*"

"I was the one who sent the letter," said Rita. "Yes. I am the author of this entire romance. I put a letter in a postbox. I would like some credit." She poured the wine. "I will accept a toast."

"Rita."

"I will accept *two.*"

Inês laughed. She turned the copper ring in the light, as a postwoman turns a letter, looking for the postmark, and found it, in the shine of the wire: the small bright mark of a man who could not rhyme, and had mended it with his hands.

*It is a good ring,* thought Inês, who read everything twice. *I shall read it for a lifetime.*
