## Chapter 58
### Ten Thousand Miles of Wifi

> *"My family is still there. I cannot settle until they are here."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0680

---

*Enter the Chorus, with a cable over one shoulder, like a man about to hang a picture of the sea.*

> Ten thousand miles of cable in the deep
> Now carry, at the speed of light, a word;
> Yet who was ever fed, or helped to sleep,
> By something only sent, and only heard?
> Tonight we ride the wire from shore to shore
> To hear a dozen lovers say, "I'm here,"
> And two who say it slant, and wish for more,
> And do not say it yet. Lean closer, dear.

### SCENE I
*Toronto. Pearson Airport, arrivals hall, 6:40 in the morning. A great many balloons.*

Idris Hakimi, twenty-seven, engineer, had once built a bridge over a river in a country he could not name without his throat doing something complicated, and he had never in his life been so afraid of a set of sliding doors.

"They are late," he said.

"They are on the ground," said Fatima Noori, twenty-five, nursing student, who had checked the board eleven times. "Customs takes an hour. It is the law of customs."

"It has been three years."

"Then an hour," said Fatima, "is a rounding error."

It had been three years, nine offices, two sponsors, a lawyer who had cried once at a hearing and apologised for it, and a file that had at one stage been mislaid on a Tuesday. His mother and his sister were in that building somewhere, in a line, holding two bags and a bundle of bread wrapped in cloth, and he had not slept since the call from Frankfurt.

Behind them, a voice rose, unsteady with joy and helium.

"*Idris bhai! The balloons!*"

Najib Sultani, twenty-five, writer, devout, and the most generous man in Scarborough with other people's money, came through the crowd with a sign in one hand and fourteen balloons in the other. The sign read WELCOME TO CANADA, HAKEEMY FAMILY. The balloons read, in curly silver, CONGRATULATIONS ON YOUR NEW BABY.

"Najib."

"They had a sale."

"Najib, we do not have a new baby."

"*They* do not know that," said Najib, with great logic. "And also they were from the baby shower, and the baby shower was in the next hall, and the supermarket was closed, and it is clearly the will of God that these balloons find a home. I used your card. I will repay you in the next book."

"You have never repaid me in the last book."

"It is a long book."

Fatima, who was shaking, laughed, and put her hand over her mouth; and the doors slid back, and out of them, pushing a trolley with a bundle in a cloth, walked a small woman in a grey headscarf and a long coat, and beside her a girl of fifteen with enormous eyes, who looked at the balloons, and the sign, and the ceiling, and the enormous gleaming country behind the glass, and said, in Dari, "Madar-jan, it is very *clean.*"

Idris did not run. He was an engineer; he walked the whole distance, at a steady load-bearing pace, and when he reached his mother he said, as he had rehearsed on the plane, "*Salaam, Madar-jan,*" and then said nothing else, for there was nothing in the engineering of language that would hold.

She put both hands on his face. She looked at it as one looks at a bridge one has been told has been rebuilt, and has not quite believed.

Then Fatima stepped forward.

She had rehearsed it for two years, on the bus, in the bath, once in a hospital cafeteria at four in the morning with a patient's grandfather correcting her vowels. Her parents had come from Herat, but they had raised her in kitchen Dari, a Dari for salt and bread and *wash your hands,* and she had needed more: a Dari for this.

"*Madar-jan,*" said Fatima, slowly, setting each word down like a cup on a tray. "*Khosh āmadid.*"

*Welcome.* Then, in the same careful hand, a second sentence, longer, in a language that had taken her two winters to learn, and which the page, for once, will not translate, except to say that it was about how a person does not have to be carried alone any more. Idris stood very still beside her; for the first time in his life he did not translate; he could not have.

The old woman said nothing. She took Fatima's face in both hands, as she had taken her son's, and kissed her once on the forehead, and began, without any dignity at all, to weep. Beside her, the girl said, in English, "Is that a *baby* balloon?"

"It is a mistake," said Najib, "that has become a blessing."

*Mark this, reader. A man may be ten thousand miles from his mother and three years from his promise, and then a pair of doors will open, and the arithmetic will be, for once, kind.*

### SCENE II
*Lyon. A bakery and a florist's, side by side, with a new door cut through the wall between them. Sunday, five in the morning.*

Pierre Dubois, forty-five, baker, gruff, stooped, and a widower of eight years' standing, stood at the oven in a floury apron and lifted out two loaves.

The first was his wife's: round, dark, dusted, scored with a cross. He had baked it every Sunday for eight years, and it was the best bread in the arrondissement, and he had never once sold it.

The second was new. It was scored not with a cross but with a spray of small curved cuts, like a handful of violets thrown at a wall.

"It is crooked," said Mathieu Lefèvre, the clockmender, fifty-nine, from the doorway. He had come to oil the shop clock and had stayed, as he always stayed, for the free bread and the pleasure of being slightly wrong. "A baker's loaf should not look like a *mistake.*"

"It looks like violets."

"Which is a mistake in a loaf." Mathieu consulted his watch. "It is ten past five. No. Twenty to six. No— I believe the clock above your door is correct and my watch is a liar. Pierre. Why have you cut a door in your wall?"

"For the draught."

"You have cut a door in a wall for a draught."

"It is a *very* specific draught," said Pierre, and went through the door.

On the other side, in the dark among the buckets, stood Margaux Laurent, forty-two, florist, widow, with her sleeves rolled and a bunch of violets in her hand. She had been leaving flowers at his door for three years. Nobody was supposed to know. All of Lyon knew.

He set the loaf on her counter. He did not say anything. His hands, which were large and white with flour, were shaking, and she looked at them with the gentlest irony in France.

"It was always me," said Pierre at last, hoarsely. "The bread. On your step. Every week, when you thought it was the neighbour's boy. I could not put a note in it. A note is a thing that can be read."

"I know."

"You *know*?"

"The neighbour's boy cannot bake," said Margaux. "He has been to my kitchen. He has burned a salad."

He laughed, a sound like a heavy door on a good hinge. She put the violets in his hand, and took the loaf in hers, and they stood in the dark and held each other's offerings like two people exchanging coats on a cold platform.

Behind them, in the bakery, Mathieu's clock struck, thirteen times, and was never repaired.

### SCENE III
*Mirpur, Dhaka. The tea stall at the corner of the lane, a Friday, nine-fifteen at night. It does not rain. The lane has electricity. Everyone is slightly alarmed.*

"Bhaiya," said Bunny, who was fourteen now, and had been promoted from the kettle to the cash drawer, and from opinion to policy, "you have been looking at your phone for six minutes without drinking your tea."

"It is a thread. From the Register." Fayaz Rahman, twenty-seven, looked up. He had on the good shirt, ironed, and the look of a man who has for twelve months been rather more afraid of good news than of bad. "Nadeesha started it. *One year. Say where you are.* There are four hundred replies."

"Read me one."

"*Youssef, Casablanca,*" read Fayaz. "*I read this whole page myself. It took eleven minutes. Salma timed me. She says the next one may take ten.*"

"Good. Another."

"*Dr. Nusrat, Sylhet. The new autoclave is a marvel. Rahim bhai says that is because it has never met the district.*"

"I like him. He is from Chittagong?"

"He is a foreman."

"Same thing. Another."

"*Dewi, Ubud,*" read Fayaz. "*For my cousin in Rotterdam: your grandmother says Ayu has learned a new word and will not tell me which. I have my suspicions. Putu says I am not to say. I am not saying.*"

Bunny wiped the counter, which did not need it. "Bhaiya. In Rotterdam it is five hours behind us."

"You have looked it up."

"I look up everything. Last year she was one hour in front of you. Now she is five behind. At this rate, by next year she will be in yesterday and you will be in Tuesday and the only place you can meet is in the middle of the sea."

Fayaz opened his mouth, and shut it, with the face of a man who has been handed an important parcel by a child.

"That," he said, "is not very far from geographically accurate."

"I know," said Bunny, with immense dignity. "I read the map."

### SCENE IV
*The flat, third floor. A desk, a lamp, an Instant Power Supply the size of a small coffin, humming. On the wall above the desk, hung with four pins and tremendous care, a long cloth of dark indigo covered in black stars.*

At 9:58 by his clock, which was now correct to the second, since a man with a contract keeps the time he is paid by, Fayaz sat down and pressed the green button.

He had a contract. He wished, he found, to say it often, as one says the name of a new town. Not the night-work for the agency in Singapore, which had been a rope thrown across a river; but a contract in his own name, with a logistics firm, for the building of a system that would track containers across half the world, and a day-time salary in a currency that was, in the language of his old arrangements, *kind.* On the balcony, his father sat with the green ledger on his knee, as he had every night since the spring, and with a steel ruler and a pencil, and on the first of each month he drew a neat line through a number. There were four numbers left. They were large. They were visible, which was a different thing from large.

The Wi-Fi was called BINTANG_HITAM. Tithi had named it, and refused to rename it, on the ground that it was the only thing in the house that never dropped.

The screen woke.

It was a studio. Tall windows, the dark coming down on a wet grey city at five o'clock in the afternoon, a long table, a steaming iron, a copper canting in a brass dish, pinned cloths in rust and indigo and a white so pale it seemed to be thinking. On the wall, over the table, hung a clock whose hands said eleven.

"It is five o'clock in Rotterdam," said Fayaz.

"On Fridays," said Ermis Larasati, twenty-five now, indigo on both cheekbones and a pencil in her hair, "Rotterdam is a province of Java. I have told them the clock is broken. They find me charming and a little ill."

"You have kept it for six months."

"I have kept it for every Friday. You said *ten, yours; eleven, mine.* You did not say whose clock." She smiled. "You have a generator!"

"I have an IPS."

"It is a *generator.*"

"It is, on the right circuit," said Fayaz, "and I would like it recorded that no one was more surprised than me."

They looked at each other. A year of Fridays, he thought, is not a year of looking: it is a year of looking *at.*

"Show me," said Ermis.

He turned the laptop. She leaned toward it, the way one leans toward a small window in a very large wall, and took in the long cloth on the wall of the Dhaka flat: *Bintang Hitam,* a field of black dots on indigo, the pattern she had drawn for him in the spring.

"Tithi counted the dots," said Fayaz, "and gave up at four hundred and some. She says a pattern that wants to be counted is flirting."

"Tithi is a very dangerous girl."

"She has just been admitted to the university."

"I *know.* She told me. She told me *first.*" Ermis laughed, and her eyes were bright. "Fayaz. It is finished. Mine. The big one. It goes on the wall tomorrow."

She turned a second camera, and the screen showed a cloth three metres long, hung from the beams, in two halves. The left half was night: deep indigo, a single white star, small as a pin. The right half was dawn: the palest blue, and in the middle of it, one black star.

"*Selisih,*" said Ermis. "It means the difference. As in, *the difference between two clocks.* The director asked me what it meant and I said *an hour.* She said it was a very expensive hour. I said that is how they come."

"It is beautiful."

"I am going to cut it in half."

"You are *what?*"

"It is made to be two," said Ermis. "I have always known. I have not decided which half will be whose." She looked into the camera. "I will decide when I can see."

Something small rang in the room behind her: a phone, propped against a jar, playing a message. A voice that had been compressed through three networks and a grandmother's thumb shrieked into the studio:

"*TOO LATE! TOO LATE! FAYYAAS! BEAUTIFUL!*"

There was a very long silence.

"She learned my *name*?" said Fayaz.

"She learned it," said Ermis, in a voice of ruined dignity, "from my grandmother. Who learned it from me. Who said it, very *occasionally,* in the kitchen, to a *saucepan.*"

"She has put it in the right order."

"She says it to *everyone.*"

"She said it to me *first.*"

"Fayaz—"

"I have no complaints," said Fayaz, with the gravity of a judge who is also laughing. "I wish only to record it."

She put her face in both hands. When she took them away her cheeks had a third stripe of blue, and her mouth was trembling, not from sorrow.

"All right," said Ermis. "Now. Serious. The thing we have not said in a year. I want us to say it."

"Which thing?"

"*When.*"

He had known it was coming. He had known it for a year, as one knows the date of one's own exam; and he had come to the screen with a plan, three contingencies and a spreadsheet, and found them all, in the face of that small lit window, perfectly useless.

"I cannot say a date," said Fayaz, slowly. "My debt is four lakh from clear, and four lakh is very near, and very far. My mother's treatment has its second phase. My father is better, and I do not wish to leave while he is learning to be." He took a breath. "You have the lab until March. And a second year, if you take it. And after that the world has asked for you."

"I know."

"I would not hold a date over your head like a wet cloth."

"Nor I over yours." She picked up the canting, put it down. "So no date. I do not want a date. A date is a promise with a lid on it. I want something *looser.*"

"When both our skies are clear," said Fayaz.

The pencil in her hair slid and fell. She did not move.

"Say that again."

"When both our skies are clear. Not before. Not by plan. The way a tide comes." He smiled, a little crookedly. "I think it was something I took from my Keeper."

"Where?" said Ermis.

"*Where* is the question." He pulled up a map on the second screen, because he was, after all, a man who built systems. "Dhaka to Yogyakarta, by line, is four thousand kilometres. You cannot come here, with Rotterdam, and I cannot go there, with the embassies as they are, so we need a place *between.* Halfway. I have measured. A few hundred kilometres of unfairness in my favour. I will carry the bags." He turned the screen. "Bunny says we shall meet in the middle of the sea. I have looked for the least wet place."

The map showed the long green leg of the Malay peninsula, and, hanging off it into the blue like a pendant on a thread, an island.

"*Phuket,*" said Ermis.

"It is in the Andaman Sea."

"It is in *Thailand,* Fayaz. Thailand has a flag."

"The beach does not."

"Beaches do not have *visas.*"

"Exactly," said Fayaz. "It is halfway. It is where no flag stands. I will bring a passport that has been renewed, which in Dhaka is a pilgrimage in itself. And I will not be sick on the ferry."

"It is on my time," said Ermis, who had begun to laugh and cry in the same breath, which in a designer is called *registration.* "Phuket is on the Java hour. You will have to put your watch forward."

"I know."

"You will be in *my hour.*"

"For once," said Fayaz, "neither of us will be ahead."

She stopped laughing. In the window, her face had gone very still, and very soft, and she looked just then like the girl in the workshop in a pale first morning, with her sentence on a page, and no one to say it to.

"Then I will tell you what I will do," said Ermis. "In the spring I said that I was an hour ahead of you. Now I am five hours behind you, and it is the first time in my life that I have been behind anyone and not minded." She leaned on one hand. "From where I sit, you are in my future. Tell me what you see."

"Ermis—"

"Tell me. I saw yours, once. It is only fair."

Fayaz looked at the cloth on his wall, and at his father on the balcony, drawing a line through a number with a steel ruler, and at the lit window with the pencil-haired woman in it, five hours back in the day and a year forward in everything else.

"I see a beach," he said slowly. "I see two people who are very bad at beginning a sentence. And one of them says, *I would tell you everything.*" He breathed. "And the other waits for the *if* — and there is no *if.*"

There was a very long quiet. Rain on the Rotterdam windows. A kettle on the Dhaka stove. A parrot, in a recording, three thousand miles away, saying nothing at all, for once, as though she had understood the occasion.

"Be on time," said Ermis, finally, in a voice that was not entirely steady.

"I have never been late."

"Last Friday you were four minutes late."

"It was the *router.*"

"It was four minutes."

"By *four* minutes," said Fayaz. "A very small error, very consistently made."

She laughed, and he laughed, and the Wi-Fi, which never dropped, held them both across five time zones and ten thousand miles of cable on the floor of the sea.

"Good night," said Ermis. "Or good afternoon."

"Whichever of us is right."

She reached to end the call; and did not, quite, and he did not either; and the screen held the two of them, looking.

> *Fayaz is typing…*
>
> *Ermis is typing…*

Two sets of dots rose in two windows, five hours apart, and sank, and rose again, like two hearts in a pretended calm.

Neither of them sent.
