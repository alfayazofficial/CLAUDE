## Chapter 54
### The Tallinn Choir

> *"My voice failed and I do not know who I am without it."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0090

---

### SCENE I
*Jaipur. The old city. A sandstone haveli in a lane that smells of marigolds, hot oil and old arguments. Morning, and for the first time in forty years, a roof.*

For four decades the Rathore haveli had been the proudest ruin in Rajasthan.

It had everything a great house requires except a ceiling: a gateway like a sunrise, carved lattices through which a thousand wives had watched a thousand processions, a courtyard painted in rose and peacock-blue, and above all of it, the sky, a very fine one, which entered by day and left by night without once being asked to wipe its feet.

Vikram Rathore, thirty, heritage guide, heir, ham, and the loudest man in the Pink City, stood in the middle of the courtyard in a turban of saffron silk and watched the last tile go down, and wept openly.

"It is a *roof,*" he told the assembled workmen, whom he had hired and who had worked for him for a year at a price that no accountant should be told. "It is not merely a roof. It is the end of a lament. It is the first stanza of a glorious—"

"Hukum," said the foreman, "the paint is wet."

"—*glorious*—"

"Do not lean on it."

"I would not dream of it," said Vikram, leaning on it.

Behind him, in a plain white *kurta* with her sleeves pushed to the elbow and indigo to the wrist, stood Priya Sharma, twenty-seven, heiress and apostate, who had refused to inherit the family's mills and had instead begun, in a rented corner of the haveli, a small house of her own, in which a dozen women, trained in the old block-printing of Bagru, made cloth with a design that was not their grandmothers'. Her collection was to be shown here tonight, under the new roof, to four hundred people, among them her father, who had not yet forgiven her and who had said he would attend "in a professional capacity."

"You have not slept," said Priya.

"I have slept in *spirit.*"

"The spirit has a bag under its eye."

"A heritage bag," said Vikram, wiping his eye with his sleeve. "Priya. Thank you for the cloth. For the awning. For the whole—" He waved at the courtyard. "I have never been so afraid of a party. Everyone I know is coming. Four hundred people. The man who wrote the guidebook. My mother's cousins, who believe I have sold the house to the Americans."

"You have sold it to nobody."

"I have sold it to *you*," he said, "in spirit."

"I own a spirit and a lease," said Priya, "and I will thank you to stop exaggerating." But her mouth moved.

At the gate, a young woman with a notebook and the manner of a ferret, Pooja Tiwari, twenty-seven, writer, who had been sent to cover the party for a magazine and had been there since six, whispered to an old chef from Agra named Pankaj Singh, seventy-three, who had come to cook the dal for four hundred and was stirring a pot the size of a boat.

"He is going to propose tonight," said Pooja. "I can feel it. I have a nose."

"You have a notebook."

"I have both." She looked at the chef. "Do you think she'll say yes?"

"Madam," said Pankaj Singh, with the gravity of one who has made a million lentils, "I have only ever known one thing about a proposal. The cook is the last to be told and the first to know."

### SCENE II
*Tallinn, Estonia. A late October afternoon, with frost already in the shadows. A wooden house in Kalamaja, grey, tall and old, in a street where all the houses lean together like men at a funeral.*

If you wish to know what silence is, reader, do not go to the mountains. Go into a house where there was always someone, and where now there is no one, at four o'clock on a winter afternoon in the north.

Mikhail Sokolov, forty-two, engineer, stood in the kitchen of the house in which he had been born and listened to the refrigerator.

It was a small noise, a dutiful hum, and the only sound in the building. His mother had died three winters ago, quietly, in the room above; his father had gone before her; the stairs, which had once creaked all day under three pairs of feet, had since become, under one, eloquent in their silence. He owned no radio and no television. He had at first thought it was grief. Then he had realised it was *fear*: of the particular pitch a quiet house has when it is waiting for you to speak.

A letter lay on the table. It had arrived that morning, with a Pärnu stamp, and was addressed in his sister's round, no-nonsense hand. It was the forty-first of its kind. She wrote every month. She did not ask him anything. She answered.

*Dear Mikhail. You will want to know whether to sell the house. No. You will want to know whether to learn the piano. Yes, it is not too late, and you may begin with the one in the church. You will want to know whether anybody could ever bear to live with a man who says "perhaps" nineteen times in a conversation. Yes, Misha. Someone could. Go out. Helena.*

He folded the letter and put it in the drawer with the other forty. Then, for lack of any other plan, he put on his coat and went to work.

> *The house has learned my footsteps and gives back
> No other sound. I wind the clock; it ticks
> As though in apology. I run the tap
> To hear the water. Engineer, they call me:
> One who can make a hundred thousand voices
> Heard on a field, and cannot make a single
> Voice in his own kitchen. I know the physics.
> A silence is a room with nothing in it
> And all the room's resonances intact.
> I do not fear the quiet. I fear it asks
> A question, and I fear I know the answer.*

---

The work was the Song Festival Grounds.

They stand on a green slope above the Gulf, at the edge of the city: a great grassy bowl for a hundred thousand listeners, and above it the arch of the stage, a huge white shell of concrete, curved like the inside of a sea-creature. Once in five years the whole nation fills it, thirty thousand singers from all the choirs of Estonia, and that is as nearly as a country can be said to pray. For twenty years Mikhail Sokolov had been the man who kept it standing. He knew every bolt of the shell, every drain, every cable. He loved it as one loves the only thing that has never asked a question.

That afternoon, as he checked the lamps on the stage, he heard behind him a sound that was entirely out of place. A cornet. A brass cornet, playing, with enormous joy and moderate accuracy, a Lancashire hymn tune.

He turned. At the lip of the stage, in a bright anorak, a young woman with a face like a scone and the shoulders of a bell-ringer was sending her note out across the empty bowl; and the shell, which had been built for the voice, had taken it, and thrown it, and was giving it back, clear as a coin on a plate, from the farthest rim of the grass.

"Nine seconds!" cried a man, running down the slope, a tall, burnt-faced, sentimental sort of man in a wet jacket, carrying a stopwatch. "Nine and a *half,* Poppy! The note hangs for nine and a half seconds! I have timed it!"

"William," said the woman, lowering the cornet, "you are on our honeymoon."

"I am timing a *wall.*"

"You are timing a wall on our honeymoon."

"It is," said William Hartley, with deep reverence, looking up at the arch, "the finest wall in Europe."

"Excuse me," said Mikhail Sokolov, in the precise Estonian of a man who has spoken it carefully all his life and never quite believed he was allowed. "You are not permitted on the stage."

The woman looked at him. The man looked at him. In perfect unison, they said, in the accent of the English north: "*Oh, sorry.*"

It was the beginning of the most unlikely friendship of the Baltic autumn. Within a quarter of an hour Mikhail had shown the groundsman the drainage system under the field, which William fell in love with on sight; and the cornetist had been permitted, as a very great favour, to play one hymn from the centre of the stage while Mikhail stood on the grass at the far rim, a hundred metres away, listening. The note arrived at his ear perfectly, small as a lit match, with no loss at all.

He had known it, of course. He had always known the shell's secret: that at the focal point of the arch, where the curve gathers, a voice no louder than a whisper can be heard from the back of a field. It had never mattered. No choir had needed it. They sang loud.

But he stood a long moment at the rim, with the cold wind in his coat, and a thought began to form in his head, like frost on a window, slowly and with great beauty.

"You have a choir here?" said Poppy Ashworth, coming down the grass with her cornet under her arm. "Mr. Sokolov? We heard singing through the wall, in the rehearsal hall behind the stage, when we came in. It was *lovely.* But there was a woman at the end of the soprano row, with a lot of scarves, who turned the pages and mouthed every word with them. Every word. And she never made a sound."

"That is Madam Mägi's women's choir," said Mikhail. "They rehearse on Thursdays." He frowned. "I do not know the woman."

### SCENE III
*The rehearsal hall behind the stage. A long room of pine floor and tall windows. Forty women of Tallinn, in cardigans, at the end of a Thursday. The light going.*

Katrin Tamm, thirty-nine, had been a soprano for twenty-six years, a good one, and for the last two a woman who held the music for other people.

It was the throat. A small thing: a surgery on the thyroid, two winters ago, and a nerve, in the doing of it, that had been touched and had since declined to explain itself. She could speak, if she spoke softly. She could laugh, in a manner of speaking. She could not sing. Specialists in three cities had said *possibly* and *give it time* and *the nerve is a stubborn creature,* and she had gone on turning the pages at the end of the row with her scarves around her neck and her lips moving, a soprano without a sound, like a bell with the clapper removed.

"From the *top,* ladies," said Liis Mägi, fifty-two, conductor, who farmed rye in the south and drove up on Thursdays in a lorry. "And Toomas, I don't need you to lead the sopranos from the bass line, you are a *tenor.*"

"I was moved," said Toomas Rebane, thirty-five, weaver, the only man in the women's choir by virtue of an administrative accident in nineteen ninety-nine, and a romantic by profession. "The Mozart. It *moved* me. I felt that the line wished to be—"

"Toomas."

"—*carried,* Liis."

"Go and be a tenor."

The women began. The sound went up in the long room like smoke up a chimney, and at the end of the sopranos' row, a woman with a long fair plait and a grey scarf mouthed every syllable of it and pressed the page flat with her thumb.

"You should say something to him," said a voice at her shoulder.

Katrin turned. It was her mother, Triin Saar, fifty-eight, dockworker at Muuga, a square, weather-cured woman who had come straight from her shift in her boots, who did not hold with singing as a rule and had come only because it was the one place her daughter went. "The engineer. He has been standing in the door for twenty minutes. He is a Russian. He looks as though he is going to be sick."

"He is a *Sokolov,*" whispered Katrin. "He keeps the Grounds. He does not speak to people."

"Then that is two of you." Triin nodded to the door. "He is looking at you as I looked at your father, at the dockyard dance. I did not speak either. He came across the floor."

In the doorway, a tall man in an engineer's coat stood with a roll of drawings under his arm and the pale, courteous, hunted face of a person who has just walked into a room with a question in it.

---

"Miss Tamm," said Mikhail Sokolov, ten minutes later, in the corridor, in his careful Estonian. "I apologise. I have no right. I heard that you are not singing."

"I have a nerve." She said it in the thread of voice that was all she had left; it was a soft, husky, unsteady sound, like a cello played in another room. "It is not important. I turn the pages."

"I am an engineer," said Mikhail. "I do not understand voices. I understand rooms." He unrolled the drawing. It was a section of the great stage-shell, with a curve drawn across it, and at the curve's heart a small neat star in pencil. "Here. This is the focal point. A sound made here, however quiet, is given back to the field by the whole arch. I measured it this afternoon. A whisper at the star can be heard at the farthest rim. It does not need a voice. It needs only—" he searched for the word, in a language that had never given him words easily, "—*breath.* The shell does the rest."

She looked at the drawing for a long time.

"You are saying," said Katrin slowly, "that I could—"

"I am saying nothing." His courtesy, which was a wall, wobbled. "I am saying a physical fact about concrete. It is a fact that has no feelings. But I have thought all afternoon, since an English girl played a hymn, that this building was made for people who have a great deal to say and very little air in which to say it. It is a very Estonian building. It has been waiting a long time for somebody to try."

"Now?" said Katrin. "In the dark?"

"There is a light on the stage. And the field is empty." He swallowed. "I will stand at the rim. If it does not carry, I shall say *it was the wind,* and you need never speak of it. I am an excellent liar about the weather."

A silence. In the hall behind them the women's choir broke upon a chord, and held it, and let it go.

At the end of the corridor, twenty women of Tallinn, who had apparently been standing against the wall for some time pretending to examine a noticeboard, scattered like sparrows.

---

The sun had gone, and the great bowl of the Grounds lay black and silver under a rising moon, frost on every blade. Far out at the rim of the field, a hundred metres off, a man in an engineer's coat stood with his back against a lamp-post and his heart in his teeth. On the stage, under a single work-lamp, at the small pencilled star on the boards, a woman in a grey scarf stood with her hands at her sides.

From the dark behind the wings the whole choir, and her mother in her dockworker's boots, and a groundsman with a stopwatch and his bride with a brass cornet, had crept out as softly as they knew how, and were watching from the shadows, hardly breathing.

Katrin Tamm closed her eyes. She thought: *I do not know who I am without it.* She thought: *Then let us find out.* She drew a breath, the first real breath she had drawn for two years, from the very bottom of her, and the whole white arch above her seemed to lean a little closer, as an old house leans to hear its youngest child, and she opened her mouth, and from the far rim of the field Mikhail Sokolov, with his hand on the cold iron of the rail, heard the first note come across the dark grass towards him, small as a lit match, and understood at last that the

*Here the page is torn.*

### SCENE IV
*Jaipur. The courtyard of the Rathore haveli. Night, under the new roof. Four hundred guests, a thousand lamps, and a very long hush.*

The collection had been shown. It had been applauded. A man in a good grey suit, in the third row, who had said he would attend in a professional capacity, had been observed taking off his spectacles, and then, with great dignity, wiping them on a handkerchief that was not wholly dry.

And now the lamps had been lowered, and the tablas had stilled, and across the courtyard, in the saffron turban of an ancestor and a sword that had never been drawn, a tall, vain, magnificent man was walking towards a woman in plain white, with indigo to the wrist.

"Priya Sharma," said Vikram Rathore, in the voice that he used for kings and coaches, "I have been a guide for twelve years. I have shown the Palace of Winds to eleven thousand people. I have told them, every one, that a house is a story told in stone, and that its greatest romance is not its walls but the person who comes to live in it." He sank onto one knee, with a clatter. "I was the heir of a haveli with no roof. You gave it a roof. And I have no more to give you than a ruin that now leaks less. Will you—"

"I will answer," said Priya, "after I have read the contract."

There was a gasp. Somewhere near the chef's pot, Pooja Tiwari dropped her notebook.

"There is a contract?" said Vikram.

"There is always a contract. I will have the north wing for a studio, the courtyard on Thursdays, final say on all colour, and a name on the door." She folded her arms. Her eyes, which were the dark and steady eyes of a woman who has shown her father a whole room of her own, were quite wet. "Is that agreed?"

"Agreed," said Vikram, in a smaller voice. "Agreed, agreed, agreed. All of it. Every word. I will sign in blood."

"Ink will do." And then Priya Sharma, heiress, apostate, designer, put out both hands, and drew him up off his knee, and kissed him on the forehead, once, in front of four hundred people and a fine Rajasthani moon.

"Yes," she said. "Obviously. I was only waiting for you to finish the *speech.*"

The roar went up under the new roof and lasted until the dal was cold.

### SCENE V
*Manchester. The band room. A Tuesday evening, with rain.*

The band room smelled, as it had for ninety years, of valve oil and damp wool, and Mr. Pennywhistle, caretaker, sixty-eight, who had held the key to it for forty-one of them, was reading a postcard to an empty room.

It was an old habit. He read aloud all post, to the chairs, and the chairs, who had heard every confession of the brass section since nineteen-eighty, had never yet corrected him.

*Dear Mr. P. We are on the ferry to Helsinki. The honeymoon has been a triumph. Tell the lads there is a stage here, in Tallinn, shaped like the inside of a shell, and a hymn played on it hangs in the air for nine and a half seconds. Poppy says she is going to make the band come out and play it. I have measured the lawn. It is excellent.*

*I will not tell you what happened on the last night, Mr. P. We were standing in the dark at the side of the stage with some new friends, and the whole place went so quiet, and it was not ours to post. I will tell you in person, or I will not. Poppy says some things you keep. But I wanted you to know there is a place in the world where a person tried a very hard thing, in front of the people who love her, and we were there.*

*The cornet is fine. Love, W. and P.*

Mr. Pennywhistle put the card on the music-stand. He looked out of the rain-streaked window at the car park, where a single streetlamp was holding up the whole dark like a mast.

"He does not say," said Mr. Pennywhistle, to the chairs. "A postcard, I have always held, is a thing that stops where it can." He sighed. "It is the one form of writing that is entitled to leave the best part out."

The chairs, who had heard everything, said nothing at all.

"And I never do find out how anything ends," said Mr. Pennywhistle, "my own novel included," and went to put on the kettle.
