## Chapter 9
### The Bookshop at Kochi's Edge

> *"I hid a poem in a book I knew you would buy."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0699

---

### SCENE I
*Fort Kochi, Kerala. The small hours of the twenty-sixth of November. The north-east monsoon, which the Malayalis call* thulavarsham, *is leaning on the roof like a tired guest who has no intention of leaving.*

At two in the morning, in a room above a bookshop, a man was copying a poem for the seventh time, and had not yet got it right.

His name was Arjun Nair, he was twenty-seven, and he possessed the three gifts of the shy: a neat hand, a good memory, and a talent for being found out by nobody. He had a long, grave, kind face, spectacles mended at the hinge with a thread, and a way of standing in his own shop as though apologising to the shelves for the intrusion. The shop below him was called Karayil Books, which is to say *On the Shore*, and it stood at the very edge of Fort Kochi, where the old Dutch lanes run out of ideas and become the sea.

He wrote with a pencil, on onion-skin paper, in Malayalam script, with its great loops and curls like waves seen from above. He had written it first when he was twenty, on the afternoon a girl in a green churidar had come into the shop, and asked for a copy of a certain children's annual, and said "Thank you" so exactly, with such a plain bright courtesy, that he had gone upstairs afterward and sat on the stairs for an hour.

He had not told her. He had done the other thing, which is cheaper and takes longer. He had folded the poem very small and slipped it between pages forty-one and forty-two of the book she was carrying away, and then stood in his doorway while she walked down the lane with it, saying to himself, with the unreason of the very young, *If she finds it, she will know; and if she does not, I shall be spared.*

She had found it. He knew that, because the following November she had come back and asked for the same annual, and the November after that, and every November since, with the unfailing punctuality of the tides. And every November he had copied the poem out afresh, in new pencil, on new paper, and tucked it between forty-one and forty-two.

Neither had said a word of it. Seven years. It is the longest conversation in the history of Fort Kochi, and not a syllable of it has been spoken.

Here, since it is the thing itself and the reader is owed it, is the poem. It does not sound so well in English. Poems seldom do. They are like fish carried inland.

> *You do not know my name; I do not mind.
> The ink is small, the paper thin as air.
> I put it where I knew that you would find,
> And then I turned away, and was not there.
>
> I am the shelf you pass and do not see;
> The shop-bell that announces who has come;
> I am the hour between your tea and me,
> The word a shy man keeps beneath his tongue.
>
> And if one year the page should come out blank,
> Know that the pencil failed, and not the hand;
> Know that the book, the sea, the year are thanks
> To one who could not say it. Understand.*

"Seven years," said Arjun aloud, to the lamp. "And I still cannot rhyme *hand* with anything."

A voice from the doorway, brittle and anxious, made him jump.

"You are *talking* again," said his father.

---

Hari Panicker, seventy-six, was a retired butcher, a large, anxious, soft-eyed man in a lungi and an undershirt, who had spent forty years in the confident trade of carving and the last seven in the unconfident one of worrying about a son who read. (A Panicker by birth, he had married into a Nair house and been absorbed by it as a stream by a river; the boy had taken the river's name.) He stood at the top of the stairs holding the old brass scale that had hung in his shop for four decades, which he had brought to the bookshop and which now sat on the counter below, where it weighed, with great solemnity, the cheaper kind of paperback.

"Achan," said Arjun. "It is two in the morning."

"I could not sleep. The rain is coming in at the back wall. I have been listening to it. It says something."

"What does it say?"

"It says, *Hari, move the books.* Every year the tide comes up the lane. What do you intend to do about the poetry?"

"Put it on a high shelf."

"And the thing you are writing?"

Arjun put his hand, very lightly, over the onion-skin paper.

"That," said Hari, with a sigh that carried forty years of fathers, "is the *only* part of the shop that is not insured."

---

### SCENE II
*Mattancherry. A schoolroom. Nine in the morning. Rain.*

"Class Six," said Meera Menon, "who can tell me what a metaphor is?"

Thirty hands went up, which was a lie, because eleven of the children had been told by their mothers to put their hands up, and nineteen had no idea. Meera Menon was twenty-six, a schoolteacher, with a long braid, a green sari the colour of a parrot's wing, and the straight bold look of a woman who has never been afraid of a classroom. She taught English at the old Jewish quarter's school, in a room with four fans, two of which worked, and a view of the spice-merchants' lane, where on a dry day the whole air tasted of ginger and pepper and the dried-fish smell that is the real perfume of Kochi.

"A metaphor," volunteered a small boy in the front, "is when you say a thing is a different thing, and it is *not*."

"Very good, Anoop. Example?"

"The rain is a teacher."

"And why is it a teacher?"

"Because it comes every day," said Anoop, "and it is always wet."

A little girl at the back, fourteen years old and the oldest of the class by the grace of a long illness, lifted her hand, and Meera nodded. This was Sreeja Nambiar, the school's unofficial librarian, a solemn child with a plait to her waist who saw everything and reported nothing, which is the whole equipment of the born novelist.

"Teacher," said Sreeja. "You are going to the bookshop today."

"Whatever makes you say that?"

"It is the twenty-sixth."

There was a delicate pause.

"Every year on the twenty-sixth," said Sreeja, "you wear the green sari, and you go to Mr. Arjun's shop, and you come back with *The Schoolchild's Annual*, and for the rest of the day you smile at it like a person who has been told a very good secret." The solemn eyes did not blink. "That is a simile, Teacher."

"It is an *impertinence*," said Meera. "Sit down."

"I *am* sitting."

"Then be quiet in a sitting position." But she was laughing, and she coloured to the roots of her braid, and thirty children, who had not the faintest idea what was going on, applauded.

---

The rain, by noon, had stopped being weather and become geography.

It poured on the harbour and the ferries, which crossed from Ernakulam with the dazed look of ducks in a bath. It came down on the Chinese fishing nets, those great wooden cantilevers, with a roar that drowned the church bells and the muezzin and the temple drums alike.

"Madam!" shouted Vishnu Kurup, from the front of his auto-rickshaw. "I cannot take you down the lane! There is a river!"

Vishnu Kurup was forty-five, a rickshaw puller, a large grave man with a moustache like a wet broom, who had been Meera's friend since she was a student and who had adopted, on that basis, a severe and total authority over her life. He stopped the rickshaw at the mouth of Princess Street. Before them lay a thing that had been a road.

"Ten centimetres of water," he announced, "and rising. It is the high tide and the rain agreeing with each other. In my experience, madam, nothing worse can happen than two things agreeing."

"I must go to the bookshop."

"The bookshop is *drowning*."

"Then," said Meera, taking off her sandals and tucking them into her bag, "I had better go and help it drown *with dignity.*"

"You are going for the book."

"I am going for the *shop*."

"You are wearing the green sari," said Vishnu, gravely. "Everyone in Kochi knows what the green sari is."

She tucked up the border of the sari, which in Kerala is a thing a woman does the way a soldier slings a rifle, and stepped into the brown water with her chin up.

---

### SCENE III
*Karayil Books. A quarter to one. The tide in the lane has reached the doorstep, considered it, and come in.*

A particular silence falls over a bookshop when water enters it: that of a very large congregation holding its breath.

Arjun stood in water to his shins with a stack of Malayalam poetry in his arms and an expression of calm despair. The lower shelves were already gone: the cookbooks, the maps, the volumes of Marxist theory that nobody had ever bought and nobody had ever sold. They stood in the brown flood like a row of drowned gentlemen. Somewhere at the back, with a wet slap, a shelf of Agatha Christie gave way, and forty murders were solved all at once, with no detective present.

"Achan, take the dictionaries to the loft!"

"I am *carrying* the dictionaries!"

"You are *weighing* them!"

"A man must know what he is losing!" shouted Hari, with his scale in his arm and a Concise Oxford under it.

The door banged open. A woman in an oilskin poncho and rubber boots strode in, carrying a large red toolbox in each hand and followed by a short wet man with a sack of sand.

"Where is the problem?" said Sreeja Iyer.

She was fifty-five, a plumber from Alappuzha, who had been called out for a quite different job in the next street, and had seen the shop's door standing open in a drowning lane with the instinct of her calling. She regarded a crisis as a personal compliment. Her hair was iron grey, her forearms were a mason's, and she had a way of saying "problem" that made the problem feel it had been *found out*.

"The problem," said Arjun, "is the *sea.*"

"The sea is not a problem. The sea is a customer." She slung a tool-bag across the doorway. "Sand-bag the threshold. Sreenath! Raise the lower shelves onto the bricks. Who is in charge of this establishment?"

"I am," said Arjun, and Hari, simultaneously: "I am."

"Then you are both useless. Where is the pump?"

"We do not *have* a pump."

"Then you have a *bucket.* Madam—" this to a green-saried figure that had come through the door with a whoosh and a splash, and was already wading to the nearest shelf "—you, yes, are you family?"

"I am a customer," said Meera.

"Good. Customers carry things."

---

It was a very Kochi sort of rescue, which is to say it involved everybody.

Word went down the lane by that mysterious route by which news travels in the rain: through the café, the chai-shop, the fishermen mending their nets under the tarpaulin. A line of people formed, thirty, forty, then sixty, from the shop's back door up the narrow stairs to the loft. Fishermen in dripping lungis. Two nuns from the convent school. A Jewish antiques dealer from Jew Town, with a face like a kindly walnut, who announced that he had survived the great flood of 2018 on a single prayerbook and was happy to attempt another. A pair of Gujarati sweet-makers, a troop of Class Six children who had walked out of school without permission and were not, strictly, supposed to be there, and Sreeja Nambiar at their head with her plait tied round her head like a general.

They passed the books from hand to hand, hand over hand, up the stair, in the dark and the wet, in four languages and one rhythm:

*"Take it. Pass it. Take it. Pass it."*

And in the café next door, a young woman in a white apron watched through the window with her arms folded, delivering a commentary to a customer who had not asked for one.

Lakshmi Panicker was twenty-seven, a waitress, and the café's principal asset, with enormous good humour and no discretion whatsoever. She had been standing in the doorway since eleven, ostensibly serving banana fritters, in practice conducting a running broadcast to the lane.

"Look at that," said Lakshmi, to the customer. "Look at Arjun Chetta. The man has lifted four hundred books in the last half-hour, and he has not once looked at the one he is *afraid* of losing."

"Which one is that?" said the customer, a sleepy tourist from Hamburg.

"The one with the *poem*." She dropped her voice to a stage whisper that could have been heard in Ernakulam. "I do not know what it says. I know only that every November, a woman in a green sari comes, and the shop goes quiet, and he cannot look at her and I cannot look at him, and there is a banana fritter growing cold in my hand for *seven years*."

"I see," said the tourist, who did not.

"It is the greatest tragedy in Kerala. *I* would do something, but I am a waitress. I only bring tea to history."

"What a lot of rain," said the tourist, politely.

"That," said Lakshmi, "is also a metaphor."

---

Arjun, at the head of the line, had not looked at Meera for seven minutes.

This was not because he had not seen her. He had seen her at the instant she entered, as one sees lightning before the thunder, in the green sari with its hem tucked up and her braid coming loose and her face fierce with purpose. He had seen her seize a box of cookbooks and wade away with it as though it were a baby. He had thought, quite clearly: *I will never be able to speak to her again, because I have seen her in my shop, in a flood, and a man cannot be expected to survive that.*

So he gave her wide berth, in a foot of brown water, with a stack of Tagore.

"Arjun," said Meera, from behind him.

He dropped the Tagore. It floated.

"Oh," said Meera. "I am so sorry. *Gitanjali* is going."

"It is—it is all right, it is—" He plunged for it, and surfaced with a damp, sodden, reproachful volume. "It is a very forgiving book."

"It is *dripping.*"

"It was always a little *emotional.*"

She laughed. It was, he thought, the sound of a very bright coin on a stone floor. He felt his ears go hot, and prayed, in the way of the bookish, that the dark of the shop would hide it.

"It is the twenty-sixth," said Meera.

"Is it?"

"You know it is."

"I—the Annual. It came last night. It is on the top shelf. It is quite safe. It is—" He stopped, with a face of the purest panic. "I have not *looked* at it."

"Good," said Meera, very gravely, and a little pink. "I should not like you to have looked."

"No?"

"I would like to find it," she said, "as it is."

There was a silence. It was the silence of a very large number of things not being said, and it had room in it for the whole flood.

"I will fetch it," said Arjun, and fled up the stairs.

---

He found the crate at the top, safe and dry. He found the copy on the top, the best, with its cover unbent, and he opened it, and there between pages forty-one and forty-two lay the folded square of onion-skin, which he had slipped in at dawn.

*She asked to find it as it is,* thought Arjun. *Does she know? Has she known all along, and been waiting seven years for the shop-boy to say one word?*

He had a pencil in his pocket. It would take only a moment, and a half-sentence, in the margin: *It is I.*

And a voice from below, unmistakable, shouted: "Arjun! The dictionaries have gone! The Oxford is *floating!*"

He put the pencil back.

*Reader, I do not blame him. A man with one hand on a poem and the other on the Oxford English Dictionary is pulled by both ends of language. Few have recovered.*

---

By dusk the water had begun, grudgingly, to retreat.

It left behind a thin brown shine on the floor, a smell of wet paper that would last a month, and a shop that was, by some miracle of human arms, nearly intact. The Marxist theory was ruined. The cookbooks were a complete loss. But upstairs in the loft, in tall dry stacks, with the dictionaries on top and the poetry at the summit, were four thousand books, and in the lane below were two hundred people who had saved them and who were now being fed by Lakshmi's café on the house.

"I wish to record," announced Sreeja Iyer, wiping her hands on her poncho, "that this is the best flood I have been in. Normally the householder is *grateful.* Here everybody is *talking.* Gopal-sir! Come and look at the tide."

This was Gopal Varma, fifty-eight, a village vet from Alappuzha and the most unstoppable matchmaker in three districts. He had come that morning to propose to Hari Panicker a girl from Kottayam with a chemistry degree and a *jathakam* that matched Arjun's on nine points out of ten; he had been trapped in the loft since one o'clock, and had formed, in the interval, a different opinion.

"I have been looking at the stars," said Gopal, with a thoughtful frown.

"You cannot see stars. It is raining."

"I have been looking at them *internally.* The Kottayam girl's chart matches Arjun's on nine points. But I have also been observing this *other* young lady, in the green sari. I do not have her chart. But I have observed, in three hours, that when the boy drops a book, she picks it up, and when she drops a book, he picks it up, and neither has dropped a book at the same time. In forty years of horoscopes, I have never seen so *perfect* a synchrony." He closed the folder. "Hari-sir. I withdraw the girl from Kottayam. The stars have had an internal revision."

"What do the stars say?" said Hari, anxiously.

"They say," said Gopal Varma, "it is a *great deal of rain.*"

---

At the shop door, in the blue light of the end of the day, Arjun put the book into Meera's hands.

He said, "It is a little damp."

"It is a very good book."

"It was always a little damp," said Arjun. "In a way."

"I know," said Meera. She held it a moment, against her chest, and looked at him; and in the look was seven Novembers, and a green sari, and the flood. "Thank you, Arjun. For the…" She stopped. "For the shop. For everything. I will see you next—"

"Yes."

"—*soon.*"

"Yes."

She went down the lane in the failing light, barefoot, her sandals in her bag, with the book wrapped in the end of her sari to shelter it from the last of the rain; and Vishnu Kurup, with his rickshaw bobbing at the corner like a small steamship, took one look at her face, and did not say a word, which for Vishnu Kurup was an act of heroism.

On the back seat, in the light of a dim bulb and the ticking of the rain, Meera Menon opened the book to pages forty-one and forty-two.

It was there: folded small, on onion-skin paper, in pencil.

She read it by the light of passing shop-windows, as she had read it seven times, with her heart doing its usual absurd gymnastics. She came to the last verse, which she knew by heart, and read it nonetheless with the old fresh shock of gladness.

*And if one year the page should come out blank…*

And then she stopped.

For the paper had been caught, in the loft, in a single drop that must have fallen from the leaking roof, and it had run: and where the last line of the poem had been, the single line that said *Understand*, there was now a faint grey smear, the pencil dissolved into a cloud.

She stared at it. She turned it to the light. She could not read it.

"Madam?" said Vishnu from the front. "Madam. Are you well?"

"I am very well," said Meera, in a voice that was unusually small. "The rain has taken the last word."

"That is a great pity."

"Yes." She folded the paper. She kept it against her heart, damp as it was. "But I know what it was."

"Do you?"

"I have *always* known what it was," said Meera Menon, and looked out of the little window at the drowned and glittering town, "and I think, Vishnu, that it is time somebody said it out loud."

"Who?" said Vishnu.

She did not answer. She was smiling at the rain, with the look of one who has at last made up her mind.
