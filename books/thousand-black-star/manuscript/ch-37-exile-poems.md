# ACT IV — WHAT TIME TAKES

## Chapter 37
### Exile Poems

> *"I am writing your story before you have lived it."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0198

---

*Enter the Chorus, in the heat of noon, carrying a fan.*

> The summer comes. It does not ask permission.
> It sits upon the roofs of Cairo, Baghdad,
> And presses, like a palm, on every forehead:
> "Remember what you left. Consider what you carry."

---

**Giza, Cairo. A clinic on the second floor above a pharmacy, in the street where the Sudanese had opened, in nine months, three restaurants, two tailors, and this.**

It was forty-one degrees at ten in the morning, and the ceiling fan of the waiting room had been turning for eleven years in the belief that it was doing something.

Dr. Hassan Idris, thirty-five, courteous to the point of illness, bent over the chest of a four-year-old girl with a stethoscope, and said to it, under his breath, as he said to everything, "Please." He said it to the child, to the instrument, to the door, and once, on a bad night, to a generator. He had been a surgeon's registrar in Khartoum. He was now a doctor of no licence in a city of twenty million, practising under the signature of a kind Egyptian colleague and the discretion of a landlord, and he had learned, in nine months, that the first medicine for a man who has lost everything is to be called *doctor* by somebody.

"Breathe in, habibti," he said.

"It is hot," said the child.

"Yes. It is very hot. That is why you are coughing. It is also why I am asking you to breathe in."

On his phone, which lay on the desk face-up, a voice note had come in from Omdurman. It had been arriving in pieces since seven, in a staccato of dropped syllables, like a man running over stepping-stones. *Hassan... Mama... the fever... the pharmacy is... no, the road to... Hassan, I cannot... do you hear...* His brother Osman, who made umbrellas and was therefore, in that country, a prophet. He had said it nine times, this morning, in nine different broken ways, and it came to the same thing: *Mother is not well. Come. Or do not come, and send the money, and say nothing.*

He had not said anything. He had gone on listening to the child's chest, which was clear.

"Doctor," said a voice from the doorway, "your feet."

It belonged to Old Zuhura, who had been the cleaner of this building since it was a dentist's, and who would, in the opinion of everybody, be mopping its corridors when it was a mosque, a bank, and a ruin. She was seventy, from Aswan, as dark as a good coffee and as wide as a doorway, and she could not read a letter of any alphabet; but she had learned the whole building by heart, as a blind man learns a house, and she did not trust any person who was not standing on a dry floor.

"I am not wet," said Hassan.

"You are *always* wet, ya doctor. It is something in the walk." She took a few swipes at the tiles round his shoes, and straightened, one hand on her back. "I have been watching you since January. You walk like a man carrying two sacks of flour, one in each hand. One is the clinic. The other is Omdurman." She nodded to herself. "A man cannot carry two sacks and open a door."

"Zuhura—"

"It is not wisdom," said Zuhura. "It is flour. We had a delivery on Monday and the boy dropped it." And she moved off down the corridor, trailing wet, singing something in Nubian that sounded like a very long apology to a very large river.

It was her idea, too, that the walls of the waiting room should wear poems.

She had had it in January, when a young Egyptian woman named Amal Yusuf had come, with an enormous bag and a face like a lit match, to ask whether the clinic would allow her to read verse to the patients on Thursday afternoons, *as waiting rooms are the only churches without sermons.* Hatim Babiker, the manager, a nurse of sixty-two from Omdurman who was superstitious and could not keep time, and who arrived every day at noon with a fresh omen to account for it, had consulted a cat, and said yes.

Since then the poems had begun, like moss, to appear. They were taped to the wall beside *WASH YOUR HANDS*; they were stuck up with plasters; and Zuhura, who could not read them, had decided, with great confidence, that they were *the prescriptions of the clinic's second doctor, the better one,* and had begun to dispense them by instinct.

"Take this," she would say, peeling one from the wall and pressing it into the hands of a man with a bad back. "Three times a day. With bread."

"It is a poem about the sea," said the man.

"It is *medicine*," said Zuhura.

It must be recorded that the man's back got better.

**Baghdad. The Dijla, which the foreigners call the Tigris, at the east end of a bridge that did not yet reach the west.**

On the other side of a desert, at a quarter to twelve, in a heat of forty-six degrees, the most obstinate stonemason in the Republic of Iraq was refusing to answer her telephone.

"It is the butcher," said Shaimaa Al-Obaidi, twenty-eight, wiping her chisel on her thigh, with the voice of a woman who knows what she is looking at. "He has rung eleven times."

"Then answer it," said the engineer.

"Never. A telephone is a leash. A man at the other end of it wants something."

The bridge was a footbridge, six hundred metres of steel and pale stone, from the old book-market at the bottom of Mutanabbi Street across the brown water to the Karkh shore, and it was due to open in the autumn, and was, at the moment, exactly two metres of air short of the far bank. A gap, like the gap in a child's smile. The two halves stood in the river, each reaching, and in the middle the Dijla went through, as slow as a thought.

"*Habibti!*" came a roar from the quay, and up the steps, with a steaming foil parcel the size of a baby, came a short wide man in a stained apron, perspiring with the indignity of a fortune-teller whose predictions have been publicly mislaid. This was Zaid Jabbar, thirty-five, a butcher of Basra, who had driven eight hours with the lunch, and who made promises faster than he could keep them, a habit that had made him, at once, the best matchmaker in the south and its most unpopular. "I promised the kebabs by noon!"

"It is three minutes past," said Shaimaa.

"Then I am a liar and a butcher."

"It is the same thing, but the second one is on a skewer."

"Mustafa, my friend, *tell her!* I have not slept! I have been driving since the *dawn* prayer, and I have good news, I have news of the woman—"

"Zaid," said Mustafa Karim, civil engineer, thirty-four, hopeful, practical, with a safety helmet on the back of his head like a halo that had slipped, "not here."

"But the Basra gossip is *excellent*. Her father is coming on the bus!"

The engineer put down his drawings.

"Her father," he repeated.

"On the five o'clock! He will not telephone, because he fears the telephone, he is seventy-eight, he was a lifeguard on the Shatt al-Arab for forty years and he has never in his life been willing to say a thing to a machine that he could say to a face! I have it from the cousin of the baker's wife. He is coming up to Baghdad, to see his daughter, for the *answer*." The butcher put down the kebabs. His voice dropped by an octave, which for him was a collapse. "She has until tomorrow midnight. The Italians, I am told, are impatient."

Nobody spoke. Down on the water a boy in a very small boat passed under the gap, singing to himself, and a pair of pigeons crossed the open air above the Dijla in a short, scornful line, unimpeded by engineering.

For the past five months, Mustafa Karim had built a bridge. He had stood in the river at dawn with a surveyor's staff. He had drawn the piers and checked the tolerances and argued with a committee of sixteen. And on every one of those mornings, he had been joined, at six, with two glasses of sweet black tea, by the architect who had designed the lamps, the railings, and the little pavilion at the Rusafa end, in which a man could sit and read; Noor Hashim, thirty, ambitious, eloquent, with a pencil in her hair and a way of looking at a half-finished span as though it owed her an explanation.

He had built the whole of it so that she should have somewhere to come back to. It was a sentence he had written to a stranger in a ledger, and not once said aloud.

"Eat the kebab," said Shaimaa Al-Obaidi, unexpectedly, in the voice of a woman who, for all her war against the telephone, was a good deal kinder than she let on. "It will get cold. The rest is the river's business."

**Giza, Cairo. Thursday afternoon.**

At three, by the clock above the pharmacy, nineteen people sat in the waiting room in the heat, with their bad backs and their children's coughs and their ration of silence, and a young woman stood at the front in a long grey dress, with a notebook open in one hand like a hymn-book, and began to read.

Amal Yusuf was thirty-one. She was fierce and lyrical, and wrote, as she lived, at the edge of a thing; and her father, a retired railway guard of sixty-nine who believed in timetables and had never forgiven poetry for being late, said that she would starve for it, as other men's daughters did for love. She had a great deal of black hair and an inability to be dull. She read in a plain, hard, steady voice, with no performance in it, as one reads a ration card, and in the third row, with a tin of salt crackers on her lap, sat a girl of fifteen named Nahid Osman, who had come from Khartoum with her aunt and a battered book of Arabic grammar, and who drank in every poem as other girls drink fizzy orange.

> *He will not go home this year. The roads
> Have learned a new geography of fear.
> He will stand at a window in a borrowed city
> And carry, in two hands, a river and a house.
> He will heal the stranger's fever, and forget
> To eat; he will say "please" to the stethoscope;
> His mother's number will be busy, busy, busy,
> And then one morning, like a bird, it rings.
> He will not say what he has left behind.
> That is the whole of it. The leaving. And the doctor
> Who leaves to save, and lives in what he saves,
> And does not know that he is already home.*

A silence settled over the waiting room, the kind that comes after a thing has touched the right bone. Somebody blew her nose.

At the back, against the wall, with a clipboard he was not reading and a stethoscope that he had forgotten to take off, a man had gone entirely still.

"Is it about someone real?" said Nahid, from the third row, who had no talent for tact. "The doctor?"

"It is about a man I have not met yet," said Amal, which was true; and then, because she was honest, "or whom I met in January, and have been writing for five months, in the future tense, to see if he would arrive."

A ripple went through the room. Old Zuhura, who had been mopping a corner in an attitude of the deepest disinterest for the past twenty minutes, stopped, and put her mop upright against the wall.

"I wrote it," said Amal, "in November. In the dark. On the night the Register opened. I read a sentence there from a doctor in Cairo, whom I did not know. It said: *I left my family behind to save strangers.* And I thought: *somebody should write that man a country to walk into.*" She closed the notebook. "I wrote him a country. It is a poem. It is a rather bad one. It is only a poem. Thank you for coming."

She went out. She went out quickly, with the big bag and the lit-match face, down the stair into the white glare of the street.

Hassan Idris did not move. His stethoscope hung from his neck like a rope.

"Doctor," said Zuhura, at his elbow.

"I am not... I did not..." He took off the stethoscope with unaccustomed violence. "It is a coincidence."

"It is an *accident*," said Zuhura, with the stern gentleness of the old for the foolish, "and an accident is only a coincidence in a hurry." She reached into the bandage cupboard, which no one but herself ever opened, and took out an enormous brown envelope, softened with handling. "She leaves them here. Every Thursday. All of them. Not for the wall: for you. She did not know I knew. *I* did not know I knew, until the second month, when the knees told me." She put it in his hands. "Read it tonight. I will lock the door."

"Zuhura, I cannot read the private—"

"*Ya doctor,*" said Zuhura. "In forty years I have mopped every secret in this building. They are all very small. You will be quite all right."

**The clinic, ten at night.**

It was the hour at which the building ceased to be a clinic and became a place of old tiles and good silence. The fan had stopped turning. The street below was a long yellow sleep of lamps. In the empty waiting room, in the chair by the window where the mothers sat, with a single bulb on, Dr. Hassan Idris sat with a brown envelope open on his knee, and read.

There were thirty-one poems. They were written in a quick, black, impatient hand, as of one who writes at a kitchen table with a cigarette she is trying to give up. They told, in the future tense, the whole of a man's life, and he knew, from the first, that it was his. The long flight; the stairs; the room above the pharmacy. The sack in each hand. The sister-in-law's weeping in the voice note; the way he could not bear a woman's name said in a certain way. His mother's door. In the nineteenth poem, he found the bread; in the twenty-second, the wall; and in the twenty-seventh, with a clean stab of shock, a line about a boy of nine on a bad road, whom Hassan had never mentioned to a living soul.

*She could not have known.*

*She wrote it,* said another part of him, with weary logic, *before I lived it.*

He turned the thirty-first page. It was the last, and the shortest. It was not in the future tense. It was untitled, and the hand had changed, was smaller and less sure, as of a woman who had written it on her knee in a doorway:

> *I wrote you in the future tense because
> The present was too small to hold the thing.
> But here's one line that cannot wait: it's now.
> The door is open. I am standing here.*

He read it. He read it twice. He was a man who had spent a lifetime reading the faces of those in pain, and he read, without being able to say how, that the person who had written this had put down the pen at that moment and was, at this moment, in the building.

There was a sound behind him: a small, deliberate, human sound, the sound of a breath being taken by someone who has decided on a course.

Beneath the last line, in a hand grown so small that he had to tilt the page to the lamp, there was one more sentence, written, evidently, an instant before the poet had walked in from the stair. It was the only line in the whole of the cycle that had no verse in it, and the only one that spoke of the very moment in which it was being read, and it said that she was standing in the doorway behind him now, that she had been for some time, and that when he turned round she would

*Here the page is torn.*

**Baghdad. Five in the morning, on the bridge.**

The call to prayer came up out of the old city in a long, rising thread, and was answered by another, and another, until the whole of the Dijla seemed hung with sound. The sky over the Karkh shore was the grey of a pigeon's back. On the unfinished bridge, a girl with a pencil in her hair stood on the stone of the east half, with her toes at the edge of two metres of air, holding a telephone, and, on the opposite lip of steel, with his helmet in his hand, a man stood and looked at her across the gap.

Her father had come on the five o'clock bus. He had not telephoned. He had sat for an hour in Noor Hashim's kitchen with a glass of tea in his old gnarled lifeguard's hands, and had said, at the end of it, in the voice of one who has said all the prayers he knows and has only the plain ones left: *In forty years on the Shatt I pulled nine people out of the water. And I will tell you the one thing I know. You cannot save anyone by standing beside them. At some moment you have to go in.* And then, since he was a man who feared the telephone and the sea in about equal measure, and had conquered only one, he had gone to bed.

"I have written it," said Noor. "I am going to press the button."

"I know," said Mustafa.

"You do not know what I have written."

"I know what it *says.* I have known since the winter. I have never been able to say what I would have said, and so I built six hundred metres of it." His voice was quite steady. It cost him a great deal. "It will be a good bridge, Noor. It will be the best thing I ever do. I will not ask you to stay for it."

"You have not *asked* me anything," said Noor. "You never have. It is the most infuriating quality in a man."

"It is the only one I inherited."

She laughed, in the dawn, on the lip of the stone. And then the laugh went, and she said, very low, across the gap: "I am going, Mustafa. In September. Copenhagen. Two years. They want me to learn how a city should live beside its water. And I want to. I want it so badly that it frightens me. And I want *this*," and she lifted her hand, with a small, hopeless gesture, toward the bridge, the river, the shore, the man, "and I have never in my life wanted two things that were the same size."

"That is why one builds a bridge," said Mustafa. "Two shores. One road."

"Do not be an engineer at me. Not now."

"I am not being an engineer. I am being a man who will leave one bolt out."

She looked at him.

"The last one," said Mustafa. "In the centre of the last span. The bridge will be finished, and sound, and safe, and open, and in the very middle there will be one bolt that I do not tighten. For you. And I will not tighten it until you come and stand on it, and say it may be done."

"That is *unsafe*."

"It is a very small bolt."

"Mustafa Karim," said Noor Hashim, with her eyes quite bright and her voice shaking at the seams, "you are a terrible engineer."

"I am an excellent one," said Mustafa. "I know exactly how much a bridge can bear."

Behind her, over the old city, the sun came up over the minarets, a clean white coin. She raised the phone. She pressed the button: a small sound; a tiny whoosh; *sent*. Then she did a thing she had never done in her life. She took the pencil out of her hair and threw it, underarm, across two metres of air, and he caught it, in both hands, like a man catching a bird.

"Keep it," said Noor. "For the drawings."

They stood, the two of them, one on each lip of the unfinished span, with the Dijla going by below, and neither crossed, since it was not yet a bridge, and both knew it; and the pigeons passed over, and did not care.
