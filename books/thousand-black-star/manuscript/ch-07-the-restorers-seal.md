## Chapter 7
### The Restorer's Seal

> *"I knew it was false. I kept it because it was yours."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0666

---

In Cairo it is said that everything is for sale, and that the only question is whether it is the thing you were told it was.

Nowhere is the saying tested more cheerfully than in the Khan el-Khalili, that warren of brass and perfume and patient bargaining where for six hundred years the same lanes have sold the same lamps to the same astonished strangers. A man may buy there a coffee-pot that belonged to a pasha or a tray that belongs to nobody at all, and leave happy in either case. This is the true miracle of the place.

And in the narrowest, crookedest of its alleys, between a spice-seller's pyramids of red and gold and a boy who sold hibiscus in dripping glasses, stood a little shop with a green door and a sign painted in two languages: **HADDAD & SONS, ANTIQUITIES AND CURIOSITIES. EST. WHEN GOD WAS YOUNG.**

It had no sons. This was a matter on which the proprietor was sensitive, and on which the neighbours were not.

"Sons," said Karim Fouad, potter, thirty-nine, fearless, who had known the proprietor since the proprietor was nine and had once been sold, by him, a scarab, a camel and a share in the Suez Canal. "The sign says *sons.* How many do you have, Omar?"

"I have *potential.*"

They were sitting in the long, mirrored, noisy room of Fishawi's café, where the glass has hung since the days of Napoleon's cousins and every gilt-framed reflection holds a different century. It was the hour of the afternoon prayer, and the air was thick with apple smoke and with mint. At the next table, upon a velvet cushion, in a suit that had been fashionable in nineteen-sixty, a very old man was holding court to nobody.

"I am the greatest novelist in Egypt," announced Sherif Sabry, seventy-two, retired writer, vain as a peacock in a fountain, "whom nobody has read. This is the whole secret of my genius. Had I been *read,* I should have been *contradicted.* But this Register! A thousand confessions, every one *better* than my novels, and all of them under one hundred words. It is an insult to a profession."

"Read us one," said Omar Haddad, and his voice, which had been light, went very quiet. "Read us *six hundred and forty.*"

He was thirty-five, and he was handsome the way a very good counterfeit is handsome: so smooth, so apt, so exactly what one hoped to find, that it took a careful eye and a bad experience to look for the seam. He had a gold tooth he did not need and a ring he had not paid for, and he had once sold a tourist the same Fatimid door twice in a single afternoon.

Sherif scrolled. "*Entry six hundred and forty. Cairo, Egypt.* 'I sold you a forgery and have never confessed.'" He lowered the phone. "Well, that's a *confession.* That's a very lazy one. Where are the details? Where is the *crime?*"

"The crime," said Omar, "was a cat."

---

It was a small cat.

It was eleven centimetres tall, and made of bronze, and it sat upright upon a base inscribed with a line of hieroglyphs in a style that was not quite right and not quite wrong; and it had been sold, three years before, in a back room of the Khan, by a young dealer with a gold tooth, to a young woman with a very straight back, for exactly three thousand Egyptian pounds.

*A Ptolemaic Bastet,* he had said. *Late period. From a private collection in Alexandria. It has been in the family since the days of the Khedive.*

*It is lovely,* she had said, turning it in the light. *It is very, very lovely.*

And she had paid. And had carried it home in a handkerchief, as one carries a bird.

"It was a good one for the tourists," said Omar, rubbing his eyes. "I had it made by a man in Giza who had made six. I added the patina myself: vinegar, tea, a little cat-litter, a month in a drawer. I asked her name three times, because I wanted to *know* it. She turned it in her hand, and looked at the base, and I thought: *she knows.* I waited for her to say. She said it was *lovely.* And she *paid.* I have been sixteen years in this trade, Karim, and I have never been paid for a thing by someone who knew it was a lie. So I assumed she did not know. She has kept my cat three years on the shelf over her lamp, and I have not been able to *sleep.*"

"Why do you not simply tell her?"

"Because she would look at me," said Omar Haddad, in a voice that none of them had ever heard him use, "as a restorer looks at a thing which has been made to deceive; with perfect attention and no hope."

"So what will you do?" said Sherif, entirely sober.

Omar rose. His reflection rose with him in a gilt frame, looking suddenly much older, and he spoke, not in the brisk patter of the sales-floor, but slowly, as one says a thing one has not yet decided to believe:

> *I will confess in the one grammar she respects.
> She does not read men's words. She reads their hands,
> The tool-mark in the metal, the small slip
> Of a chisel that will not lie. She reads
> How long a man has loved a thing, by how
> The edges soften. So I'll make her one.
> I'll make a piece, a perfect piece, in brass,
> A forgery so exact, so obviously false
> To any eye that has an eye to see,
> That she shall turn it over, once, and find
> Beneath the hinge, in letters smaller than
> A grain of rice, the thing that I have never said.
> And if she throws it from the window, well.
> At least I'll be the first man in the Khan
> To tell the truth in brass.*

"That," said Sherif Sabry, after a considerable pause, wiping his eye with the tip of a silk handkerchief, "was an *extremely* good speech."

"Thank you, Ammu."

"I should like to have written it."

"I will give you the credit, in the obituary."

---

**The Conservation Laboratory of the Museum of Islamic Art stands behind a green iron gate in a quiet street, and within it a very clean silence is kept.**

It smelled of acetone and warm dust; it had white walls, a skylight, six benches, and on each bench a lamp. At the farthest bench, under the best of the lamps, a woman of thirty-two sat, with a loupe screwed into her right eye, a lacquered brush in her left hand, and a fragment of fourteenth-century glass held in a pair of tweezers to the light.

Her name was Layla Mansour. She was exacting, dry, small, and immaculate; she wore a grey linen apron, sleeves rolled to exactly the same height, and she spoke to objects in the voice that other people use to speak to a nervous child. She had been called, in the journals, *the finest eye in North Africa.* She had been called, in the corridors, *the Ice Pharaoh.* She had once told a German professor to his face that a Mamluk lamp he had proudly authenticated was *twelve years old and from Marseille,* and had been proved right in eleven days.

On the shelf above her lamp, as it had sat for three years, sat a small bronze cat.

"It is looking at you again," said Dina Shaker.

Dina, fourteen, was the granddaughter of the only woman cab-driver in Heliopolis; she had arrived at the Museum two years ago on a school trip, refused to leave, and had since been appointed, by a process nobody could reconstruct, Official Sweeper and Unofficial Witness. She had enormous round spectacles and an inability to let a silence lie.

"It is a *cat,* Dina."

"It is a cat that *looks.* I think it knows you've read the Register. You read it on Monday, at your bench, with your lips, and went the colour of a boiled egg. the Director said, *Is something wrong, Madam Layla?* and you said, *It is the acetone.* But the acetone was in the cupboard."

"Then say it to the *floor.*"

"The floor is not interested," said Dina Shaker, with dignity, and swept.

It is true that Layla had read it. She had read the whole of it, in her kitchen, on the Monday night, on a small cracked tablet, with a glass of hibiscus at her elbow; and she had found, beside the Register's long ribbon of Cairo, two entries she had known she would find. The first: *I sold you a forgery and have never confessed.* The second, which was her own, written on a night she did not wish to remember, to the Keeper in England: *I knew it was false. I kept it because it was yours.*

"Madam Layla," said a voice in the doorway, with a trembling deference. "Forgive me. Did I come at a bad time?"

The speaker was a thin, anxious man in an enormous overcoat, carrying a battered satchel with both arms, as a man holds a baby he is afraid to put down. This was Tarek Hassan, forty-one, librarian of Alexandria, who had come to Cairo for the week to consult with the Museum on a catalogue and who had not slept properly since the night of the new moon, because he remembered, with perfect clarity, the exact day on which every dreadful thing had ever happened to him.

"Not at all, Mr. Hassan."

"It is only that I was *told*—" he lowered his voice "—that there is *a certain dealer,* with *a package,* coming *here,* to be *authenticated.*"

"Many packages come here."

"But *this* package," said Tarek, with the glassy fixity of a man who recalls the Twelfth of March nineteen-ninety-eight, a Thursday, rain, a dropped ink-pot, "is *cursed.*"

"By whom?"

"By *love,*" said the librarian, and put down the satchel, and sat upon it, and was not seen to stand up again for some time.

---

He came at four o'clock.

He came in his best suit, a pale cream linen that had been, in its day, an act of financial violence; with a hat, a stick, a gold tooth and a wooden box. And he came in at the green iron gate with the swagger of a man who is trying very hard to walk as if he were not afraid of a woman in an apron.

"Madam Layla," said Omar Haddad. "May the day be blessed."

"May it be blessed twice, Mr. Haddad."

"I have something," he said, "for you to look at."

"I assumed you had. You have not been to this laboratory in three years. I take it it is not a social call."

"I have not been *invited.*"

"You have not been *willing.*" She did not look up from the glass. "Put it on the bench."

It was a small box of brass, inlaid with silver. He set it down upon the white cloth with both hands. It was a pen-box, such as scribes had carried in the belt in the days of the Mamluks: long, narrow, with a hinged lid, and upon the lid in lines of beaten silver an ornament of looping vines and stars, and about the rim a band of inscription in a firm, flowing, practised hand.

"A *qalamdan,*" said Omar. "Fourteenth century. Syrian work, I think; possibly Damascene; brought to Cairo in the time of Sultan Hasan. From a house in Giza. I believe it to be extremely fine."

"I should like your seal," said Omar, "upon it. If it is, as I hope, sound."

There was a silence. Dina, in the corner, had forgotten her broom. Tarek Hassan, on his satchel, had gone the colour of the wall.

Layla Mansour put down the glass, and screwed in the loupe.

She did it as she always did: in order, in silence, by the book. She looked at the brass under the lamp, and then under the ultraviolet, where an old patina glows a dull amber and a young one flares like a cheat. She tapped the rim and listened for the ring. And then she did what the great forgers have always feared more than any machine: she *smelled* it.

"It is modern," said Layla.

"Is it?" said Omar, faintly.

"Brass sheet, rolled in the last ten years. The patina is vinegar and tea, with a little *tobacco.* You have used cigarette-ash to dull the rim." Her voice was quite level. "The vines are good. The stars are better. The inscription is a stock verse of blessing, in a thuluth that is nearly right but for the *alif,* which the Mamluk scribes never drew with a serif. It is a very beautiful forgery, Mr. Haddad. I should say it is the best that I have seen this year."

"I am so very sorry to have wasted your time."

"You have not."

She had not looked up. She held the box in the lamp-light, close to her eye, and her fingers, which were small and square and as steady as a surgeon's, were turning it over slowly, as one turns a sleeping child.

"Because there is something *wrong* with it," said Layla.

"Wrong?"

"It is *too good.* A forger who makes a thing to deceive makes it to be *admired.* He makes the hinge as fine as he can. He makes the silver as bright as he dares. But this—" She stopped.

It was a very small repair. A hairline crack, in the brass at the pivot, such as comes of a hundred years of opening and shutting a lid; and over it, in place of the usual neat invisible mend, a mend made *visibly:* a single thread of soft yellow gold, laid in with a fine tool along the length of the crack, so that the flaw was not hidden but *shown,* made a thing of beauty, a seam of light.

Layla Mansour did not move.

It was a mend she knew. It was a mend she had made. She had made it, in that very manner, on forty-one objects in the Museum of Islamic Art, over the course of nine years, against the advice of three directors. It was her habit. It was her *signature.* She had never said so; she had never written about it; she had never, to her knowledge, told another living soul.

"Who did this?" said Layla.

"I do not know," said Omar, in a voice that was trying very hard to be a salesman's.

"It is my *mend.*"

"Is it?"

"It is not a *copy* of my mend. A copy would be neat. This is *crooked,* in just the place that mine goes crooked: *there,* at the third turn, where my wrist catches. It is a copy of my *mistake.*" She put down the box. "Mr. Haddad. You have forged not the piece, but the *restoration.* You have made a box that was found, broken, and mended by *me.*"

Her voice, which had never in thirteen years wavered upon a bench, now did, very slightly, as a thread does when it takes a strain. "That is not a forgery of an antique. It is a forgery of my *taste.*"

"Layla—"

"Under the hinge."

He did not answer.

"There is something under the hinge. I can feel it. There is an engraving." She had taken up the loupe again, and bent, and the long clean lamp lay over her face and over the little box, with its stars and its seam of gold. For a time there was only the sound of her breathing and a distant mutter of the muezzin from a minaret across the roofs.

Then she read it.

It was in letters smaller than a grain of rice, in a perfect, delicate, beautifully formed thuluth, cut with the point of a needle on the underside of the lid:

*Made by the hand of Omar Haddad, in the year of the Register, in a house in the Khan, with a good deal of vinegar. He sold you a cat three years ago and has been sorry every day since. He would like to be forgiven, and cannot see how.*

---

She sat for a long time with the loupe in her eye, which made it impossible for anybody to tell whether she was reading or weeping, and a woman in an apron is under no obligation to explain which. She put it down at last, and reached up to the shelf above the lamp, and took down the little bronze cat.

She set it beside the box.

"It was sold to me," said Layla Mansour, "as a Ptolemaic Bastet. Late period. From a private collection in Alexandria."

"I know what I said."

"It has the left ear of a reproduction cast in Brooklyn in nineteen-ninety-one. The base was recut in the last decade by a competent man with an electric tool. The patina is vinegar, tea and *cat-litter,* Mr. Haddad. *Cat-litter.* I could smell it from across the Khan."

"You *knew?*" said Omar.

His voice cracked in the middle of the word like a plate.

"Of course I knew."

"From the *first moment?*"

"From the moment you put it in my hand."

"But you *paid!*"

"I paid three thousand pounds," said Layla, "which was a *fair price,* for the hours of work, and the value of the lie." She did not look at him. She was looking at the cat, with a face of such formidable composure that it could only have been held up by a very great weight. "I had never in my life been *sold* anything with so much hope. You stood there with your gold tooth and your ring, and you said it had been in the family since the Khedive, and I looked at your face, and I thought: *he is lying to me, and he is not enjoying it.* And I had never seen that before."

"Layla."

"So I kept it."

"Why?"

"Because it was yours," said Layla Mansour.

There are silences made of nothing and silences made of everything, and this was the second kind. Tarek Hassan, who had been sitting on his satchel in a state of spiritual collapse, took out a large clean handkerchief and blew his nose like a trumpet. Dina Shaker said, very loudly and to nobody: "I *knew* it."

"So," said Omar, and sat down upon the nearest stool, which was Dina's, as though his strings had been cut, "I have been confessing for three years to a person who was *waiting for me to start.*"

"I would not put it so."

"How *would* you put it?"

"I would say," said Layla, drawing a clean sheet of paper from the drawer and uncapping a pen, "that you are, Mr. Haddad, a very bad forger and a very fine craftsman, and that the two have never before been found in one man."

She wrote. She wrote for some time, in her small upright hand, upon the Museum's cream paper with the crescent at the head; and when she had written, she took from the inside of her apron a small brass seal on a ribbon, which she had worn for nine years and used on five thousand pieces of the heritage of Egypt, and breathed upon it, and pressed it, hard, in red, at the foot of the page.

She turned the paper round.

*CERTIFICATE OF EXAMINATION. Object: one pen-box, brass inlaid with silver, of modern manufacture. Opinion: not a work of the fourteenth century. A work of the twenty-first, wholly and honestly made. Authentic — as to the hand that made it.*

*— L. Mansour, Conservator.*

Omar Haddad read it three times. He had the look of a man who has been given a kingdom, and is looking about him for the forgery.

"It is not for sale," said Layla.

"I know."

"The Director will wish to buy it. He will offer forty thousand pounds. I shall decline. It is not a museum piece; it is evidence."

"Of what?"

"That," said the Ice Pharaoh, and for the first time in three years the corner of her mouth moved, which Dina Shaker would afterwards swear was a *smile,* "is for the court to decide. Dina. Mr. Hassan. I think the laboratory will be closing early."

"Mr. Hassan," said Dina, hauling the librarian to his feet by one sleeve with the strength of a very small and determined crane, "I think it is time for a *tea.*"

---

The door closed behind them. The skylight had turned a deep and dusty rose. Across the roofs a muezzin began, in a high clear thread of sound, to call the faithful to prayer, and the whole of Old Cairo lay in the last gold of the afternoon.

Omar stood up. He did not know what to do with his hands.

"I should like," he said carefully, "to ask whether I might—"

"You may take me to dinner," said Layla.

"I was going to say *apologise.*"

"You have apologised. In brass. It is in the record." She took off the apron, folded it exactly in three, and laid it on the bench. Without the apron she seemed younger, and a good deal less formidable, and a great deal more frightened. "But you may take me to dinner. On the condition that you will tell me nothing that is not true."

"Madam Layla, I am a *dealer.*"

"Then I shall require provenance," said Layla Mansour, "for every word."
