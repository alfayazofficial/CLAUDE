## Chapter 35
### The Lighthouse Keeper's Son

> *"I keep the lamp lit for ships and for you."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0630

---

**Galle, Sri Lanka. Late April, in the weeks between the monsoons, when the afternoons are thunder and the evenings are forgiveness.**

At the south-western tip of the old Dutch fort, where the ramparts run out of wall and into the sea, there stands a white tower that has been telling ships where the land is since long before anyone needed telling anything else.

On the morning that concerns us, it was failing.

"It is the bearing," said Anil Perera, thirty-two, lighthouse keeper's son, lying on his back beneath the great lens with a torch in his teeth, so that what he said came out in the shape of a hum. "The monsoon got into the housing in March. It has been grinding for a month. Last night it stopped."

"What does that mean?" said the young woman who was holding the other torch.

"It means," said Anil, removing the first torch, "that for the first time in living memory, if you leave the lamp on, it will not turn, and if it does not turn, it is not a lighthouse. It is a very tall lamp."

"That is an unfair distinction."

"It is the only one the ships care for."

Nadeesha Fernando, twenty-nine, nurse and executor, had come home to Galle three weeks before with two suitcases, a death certificate, and a feeling, which she had not told anyone, that she had been left on the shore by a tide. The Register had been open for five months. She had read, by her own count, four thousand letters of thanks and eleven hundred of rage. She had a mother in Kandy who loved her and could not apologise for it, a brother who sold tea on the ramparts, and an aunt in a churchyard in Cornwall who had left her a house and a cabinet and a world.

What she had not got was a place to put down the torch. And so, when the tall quiet man who ran the Galle Light, whom she had known since she was nine, had said on the telephone, *The bearing has gone, the lamp is dark, and my father cannot see to help me,* she had said: *I shall come at six.* She had carried up one hundred and thirty-six steps a king coconut, a spanner, and a sense of mission.

"Hand me the sixteen," said Anil.

"The sixteen *what*?"

"The sixteen. The spanner."

"There are five spanners."

"The one that says sixteen."

"Anil, they *all* say sixteen. Someone has been buying the same spanner for fifty years."

"My father," said Anil, with a flicker that was the closest he came to a smile, "does not forget what he likes."

At which moment there was a great clanking on the stair, and a voice that came before its owner like a crier before a king: "*Aney, aney,* the god-blessed Perera boy and the nurse madam! I have brought *offerings!*"

Chaminda Abeywardena, forty-seven, proprietor of the newsagent's by the clock tower, was a devout man in the same sense that a sponge is a wet one. He carried in his arms, with the care of a procession, six king coconuts, a flask of tea, a bundle of white thread, and the front page of a newspaper dated the fourth of February 1989. "I have kept every paper since the day I started," he declared, "and today I thought: what is a lighthouse but a very old newspaper? It tells the news, and warns of danger. It is the same trade."

"Chaminda," said Anil, from the floor, "why have you brought the *paper*?"

"For the pirith! I will bless the lamp. I have brought the sacred thread. And for the *luck* I have brought the paper of the day the lamp was last repaired." He unfolded it. "This," he said, with great tenderness, "is Wimbledon."

"Chaminda, the lamp was last repaired in 1994."

"Then I have brought the *wrong* Wimbledon," said the newsagent, calmly, and sat down on the floor and began, in a noble baritone, to chant.

It was in the eleventh minute of this blessing, with Anil swearing quietly in two languages at a seized housing, and the sea behind the great curved glass the colour of a very old coin, that Nadeesha, who had been lying on her stomach holding a torch into the cavity behind the lens pedestal, said, in an entirely different voice:

"Anil. There is a tin."

---

It was an old biscuit tin, with a faded painting of a coach and four on its lid, and it had been placed in the hollow behind the brickwork so long ago that the rust had bonded it to the wall. They had to cut it out. Anil did it with a chisel, as gently as a man delivers a calf, and when it came free, he carried it to the window as one carries a sleeping child.

Inside lay the letters.

There were seventy-one. They were thin and blue and weightless, the old airmail kind that folds into its own envelope, bundled in the faded string of an age that tied bundles. The earliest were addressed in a girl's looping hand to *Mr. D. Perera, Keeper, Galle Light, CEYLON.* The later ones said *SRI LANKA.* On the back, in the corner, each carried the same return: *Miss S. Wyndham-Rao, Mrs. Alder's rooms, Kentish Town, London.*

Nadeesha did not touch them. She sat on the bottom step of the gallery stair, with her hands pressed between her knees, and a sound came out of her that was not a word.

"My grandfather," said Anil, very quietly, "was the keeper in those years. He never told us. I thought he had *no* letters." He turned the first envelope over, with a care that cost him. "It is your aunt's hand."

"I know it is," said Nadeesha. "She wrote *every list in her life* in that hand. She wrote my birthday card in it. She wrote *Wash your hands* on the kitchen door."

It was she who opened the first. It had been written in the summer of 1964, by a girl of eighteen about to go to London to study the stars:

*Dear Mr. Perera. Thank you for letting me stand in your lamp-room last August, and for not laughing when I asked what the light was for. You said, "for the ships." I have been thinking since. I think it is for the ones who do not know they need it. I am going to London. I shall write to you. You need not answer. A keeper is not expected to. A light does not reply, it only goes on being there. — Seraphine W.-R.*

She read it aloud. Chaminda had stopped chanting, which, in an age of miracles, was perhaps the whole miracle. When she had finished she held it against her chest.

"There is one more," said Anil. "At the bottom. It is the last. It is dated 1979."

---

The afternoon went. The bearing went in, a new bronze one that had come up from Colombo on a bus, on the knee of Nadeesha's brother Sampath, who had sold tea to the driver all the way. Anil set it with a tenderness that was almost indecent. The brass housing was oiled; the lens, which was the size of a small room and held all its glass in the exact attitude in which it had been set by a foundry long ago, was released.

At half past six, with the tropical dusk coming down like a cloth on a table, a girl of fifteen with a basket of jasmine appeared at the foot of the stair and shrieked something up the well of the tower. This was Kumari Senanayake, who had been telling the whole of the Fort for a month that the lighthouse keeper looked at the sea when the nurse was not there, and at the nurse when she was not looking.

"What did she say?" said Nadeesha.

"I did not hear it," said Anil, who had. He went to the switch. His father, who had kept the Galle Light for thirty-eight years and whose eyes were now two milk-white moons, stood in the doorway of the lamp-room in his old white jacket, with one hand on the rail and his head tilted, as a man listens to a thing he has heard ten thousand times. Nobody had asked him to come up the stairs. Nobody had been able to stop him.

"Throw it, putha," said Mr. Perera.

Anil threw it.

For a moment there was nothing but the light, white and round and enormous in the glass. Then, with a faint sigh, like a deep breath let out after a long swim, the lens began to turn. The beam swung out across the sea, a hard bright road laid down on the water, and went round, and found the horizon, and went on. Far below, on the ramparts, on the beach, on the road of the fort, people looked up, as a congregation looks up when the bell is finally rung.

"Twelve seconds," said Mr. Perera, listening. He had counted it by ear. "Twelve. Same as always." His blind face turned, with great accuracy, toward the nurse. "Madam. Your aunt used to stand at the rail where you are standing. She was twelve, I think. My father allowed it. She never once said thank you. She only looked."

"Mr. Perera," said Nadeesha, "I am going to cry."

"Do it on the rampart, madam," said the old man gently. "The glass is expensive."

---

She went down. It seemed the only thing to do. She went out through the old iron door at the tower's foot and along the rampart to the place the fort-people call Flag Rock, where the stone drops sheer to the surf, and there, with the wet wind blowing the first of the cool evening against her face, she stood and let herself weep for the first time since a night in November.

Behind her, high up, the beam went round.

It found her, as it passed. It left her. It came back. Twelve seconds, a circle of light, a long turn of dark, and again the light on her wet face, and again the dark, a pulse like the beat of a slow heart. She could not have said why this should comfort her. Perhaps because something in the world had been turned, at last, in the right direction and was going on being turned whether or not she could stand it.

Above her, in the gallery, with a spanner still hanging forgotten from his belt and his father humming softly beside him, Anil stood at the rail and watched the small figure on the rock. Each time the light passed over her, he saw her face for the length of a breath. Then the dark. Then, twelve seconds on, the face. He found he had begun to count. He found that it was the longest he had ever waited for anything, and the shortest.

He spoke then, quietly, as men do who have kept silent for a very long time and find that the night is not listening, and are mistaken:

> *The lamp goes round. I never see the ship.
> I send the light to find a thing it cannot see,
> And trust it will be found. This is my trade.
> For ships, I keep the lamp. For her, I keep…
> I have no word. The beam goes round again,
> And finds her face, and leaves it, and returns.
> There is no kindness like a thing that turns
> And passes you in the dark, and comes again.
> I'll turn like this, if she will let me. Slow.
> For twelve long seconds, and for as many years
> As there are stairs. I'll come round. I'll come round.*

"It is for the ships," said Anil, to nobody, to the glass.

"*Putha*," said his father, who had very good ears, "it is for the ships *and* for the stairs."

---

She came up when it was dark. It was a hundred and thirty-six steps, and she took them one at a time, with the last letter in her fist. She stood in the doorway of the lamp-room with her face still wet, and the beam went over her like a hand.

"I found the one from 1979," said Nadeesha. "I read it on the way up."

"What does it say?"

"I'll read it."

She read it in the doorway of the lamp-room, by the light that went round:

*Dear Mr. Perera. A man wrote to me from a ship in the autumn of '77, and I have kept his letter, and I find that I want to keep others. I have placed a notice in three foreign papers: if there is a thing you cannot say, write it to me. I will keep it. I will number it. I will say nothing. I do not know why I tell you, except that you taught me what it is to keep a light for someone who may never come to harbour.*

*P.S. If any of my blood should come to your door one day, needing to see further than she can, let her up the stairs. — S.*

She folded it.

"You let me up," said Nadeesha.

"Of course," said Anil.

"She asked you to."

"She asked my grandfather."

"You were not *asked*," said Nadeesha. "You came with a spanner and a torch and a king coconut." And then, because there are limits to what a person can carry in one evening, she laughed, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and walked across the room and put her hand on the brass rail of the lens beside his own.

They did not speak. The great glass turned above them, and every twelve seconds the room flashed white and dark and white again, and in each flash the two of them were standing a little closer, as in a flickering reel of film, until at the last their hands lay together on the rail, which was warm from the lamp, and neither one said anything at all.

In the corner, Chaminda Abeywardena, who had been present throughout and had been entirely forgotten, took out the front page of the fourth of February 1989 and, with the greatest delicacy, laid it over his face.

---

**Kyoto. The same evening, by a different light.**

The workshop of Kenji Watanabe smelled of cypress and glue and the old sweet dust of shell. It stood at the end of a lane in the north of the city, in a wooden house the colour of strong tea, and it contained, on shelves, in baskets, hanging from nails, some two hundred faces.

They were all of one family: the long pale women of the Noh stage, with brows high-painted and mouths like closed leaves; the old men with beards of horsehair; the demons with gold eyes; the girl called *ko-omote*, whose face is neither smiling nor weeping and has been copied, by carver after carver, for six hundred years. They watched the room with the patience of the dead.

"You may not touch the chisel today," said Daichi Kobayashi, twenty-five, apprentice, drinker of too much tea at night, and a young man of strong superstitions, to a figure bent over a workbench in the corner. "It is *Butsumetsu*. It is a day of ill omen. A man who carves on Butsumetsu will make a face that will not look at him."

"I am not carving," said Kenji. "I am finishing."

"It is a different *kind* of bad luck."

Kenji Watanabe was twenty-seven, quiet to the point of furniture, a man whose hands were far more fluent than his tongue. He had been apprenticed at fifteen, and had learned in twelve years that a mask is a face with its mind removed: it holds a feeling as an empty cup holds the shape of water. He was very good at it. It was perhaps because he could not show his own.

On the bench before him, wrapped in a square of purple silk, lay the thing that had taken him four months.

"Master," said a voice at the door, "the wood you ordered has come."

It was Yuto Shimizu, the river-boatman from Arashiyama, a hot-tempered man of twenty-nine who believed what he was told by anyone who looked sure of themselves, and who had brought with him, on a handcart, an enormous slab of timber with the air of a man presenting a firstborn. "Genuine three-hundred-year-old cypress. From a temple that burned down. My cousin's friend got it. Look at the *grain.*"

Kenji looked at the grain. He tapped the slab. He bent and smelled it.

"Yuto-san," he said, with great gentleness. "This is the top of a chopping board from a sushi shop in Osaka."

"No!"

"There is a small fish-scale in the corner."

"It is a mark of *age!*" said Yuto, and sat down on the handcart, abruptly, with the expression of a man betrayed by a cousin and a cousin's friend. "I paid four hundred thousand yen."

"You were robbed," said Daichi, with delight.

"I was *educated*," said Yuto, with dignity.

The door slid, and there was a hush, because there is always a hush when a certain kind of person comes into a room. She came in as she always came, without sound. She was twenty-six. She wore a plain grey kimono and her hair up, and there was a small, correct, immovable smile on her face that she wore through the streets, and the station, and her mother's table, and the green-room of every theatre in the city, like a mask she'd never learned to remove.

Haruka Mori was a Noh actress, and a very fine one, in a tradition that had not been built for her. On the stage she could, in the turn of a head, be a ghost of a thousand years. Off it she was a woman with a polished smile, who said *Yes, of course,* and *It was nothing,* and *Please don't trouble,* and who had told no one in six years what it cost.

"Watanabe-san," she said. "Your boy said you wished to see me."

"Mori-san," said Kenji.

He said nothing for some time. It is a method of speech, in certain trades. Daichi, with an almost audible click of the brain, took Yuto by the elbow and removed him, protesting, along with his chopping board, to the lane.

When they were alone, Kenji took up the silk bundle and placed it, with both hands, on the bench between them.

"It is not for the stage," he said. "I did not make it for the stage. It will not be good on the stage. It is for you."

She unwrapped it.

It lay in the purple silk, a young woman's face of pale cypress, finished with a thin pearl of shell-powder; and it was *wrong*. It was perfectly formed, and entirely correct in every curve, and it was wrong in that it had no expression whatever. The brow did not lift. The mouth did not close. There was no sorrow in it and no joy. It looked, in its blank, restful, patient beauty, like a face before it has been told what to be.

"It has no face," said Haruka.

"It has *all* of them," said Kenji. He took it, and tilted it a little upward, so that the lamp fell across the brow, and the face seemed to lighten, as if on the edge of a laugh. He turned it down, and the shadow ran into the eye-sockets, and the same face, without a mark altered, held a grief as quiet as a pond. "It is called *terasu* and *kumorasu*: to brighten and to cloud. All the masks do it. But this one has no mind of its own to get in the way." He set it down. His fingers were not quite steady. "I make faces because I cannot show my own. You wear one because you will not set yours down. I thought: if you had a face that was permitted to be nothing, you could rest. You could put it on in the evening, in the dark, and not have to choose."

The workshop was very quiet. The two hundred faces on the walls seemed, with great delicacy, to look elsewhere.

Haruka lifted the mask. She held it before her own face, not yet touching, and for a moment he saw the little perfect smile she wore, and the blank cypress one, side by side, and he understood, as a carver does, which was the hollow.

She put it on.

She did not tilt it up. She did not tilt it down. She stood, with a young woman's blank face over her own, in the lamplit shop of a quiet man, and said nothing at all; and from behind the wood a very small sound came, such as a person makes who has been holding her breath for six years and has been given leave to let it go.

When she took it off, there was no smile on her face. There was only a wet, tired, entirely honest face, which was, he thought, the most beautiful thing that he had ever carved or ever would.

"It is the first one," said Haruka Mori, "that lets me stop."

On the inside of the silk, in a hand so small that it might have been a worm's, Kenji had written, in the old five-line form of the court:

> *Carved from one block,
> a face that has no country.
> Tilt it to the lamp:
> spring. Tilt it from the lamp: rain.
> Whichever you are, be it.*

She read it. She folded the silk. She said, in a voice quite without the smile, "Watanabe-san. You will have to teach me to be *sorry* in front of people."

"I do not know how," said Kenji. "I have never done it."

"Then we will be bad at it together," said Haruka. And at that, in the lamplight of a Kyoto evening, with a blank mask between them and two hundred painted faces turned politely to the wall, the carver and the actress did, for the first time in either of their lives, the same thing at once: they laughed.
