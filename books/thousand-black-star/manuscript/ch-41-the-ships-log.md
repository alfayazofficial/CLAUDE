## Chapter 41
### The Ship's Log

> *"My captain wants me; so does the sea. You are third."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0991

---

*Enter the Chorus, in an oilskin, with a lantern, a compass and a very small violin.*

> A ship has but two mistresses: the shore
> She leaves, the sea she goes to. Let it be
> That one small heart, which neither's, wants her more
> Than either, and is told to wait. For she
> Is owned by tides; he, by a harbour wall;
> And log-books keep what lovers cannot say.
> Here is the log. We read it, one and all,
> And mark the hour where each one lost the way.

---

### SCENE I
*Split, Croatia. The Riva, the long stone promenade under the walls of Diocletian's palace. The last evening of the month. A hot, heavy wind from the south, the* jugo*, which makes the dogs bite and the philosophers weep.*

> **LOG OF THE M/V *MARJAN*, bulk carrier, Split. Entry in the hand of Ivan Horvat, deck rating.**
> *1900. All stores aboard. Crew complete but for the cook, who is late and is, he says, "not late, merely *anticipated.*" Wind S, force 4, jugo. Barometer falling. Spirits: high. Heart: not recorded; the form has no column.*

The Dalmatians have a word, *fjaka*, for the blessed state in which a man, on a warm afternoon, is neither working nor resting nor thinking, but simply *being,* as an olive is being on a branch. The jugo brings it. It lay now over the whole Riva like a damp, sweet blanket, and the palm trees stood in it motionless, and the old men on the benches argued about football in the unhurried way of men who have been wrong together for sixty years.

Through it, at a rapid, determined pace, strode Marija Kovač, thirty, second officer of the *Sveti Duje*, in her blue shore-uniform and a face from which no emotion had ever been permitted to escape without a pass.

"You are late," said Ivan Horvat.

"I am nine minutes early."

"For the *sea,*" said Ivan. "For me, a woman who is nine minutes early is nine minutes *late* in a better coat."

He was thirty-three, brown as a walnut, with the loose, windblown look of a man whose smile had been rubbed smooth by weather, like a stone at the foot of a quay. He sat on the sea wall with his legs swinging, an enormous canvas bag at his feet, and a paper bag of fritule in his lap, and he held it out to her, and she took one, because refusing him anything was a thing she had never learned to do properly.

"Six months," said Ivan.

"One hundred and eighty-two days."

"I have counted too. I make it one hundred and eighty-*three*, with the leap of my heart."

"You are not a mathematician."

"I am a *deck rating,*" said Ivan, "which is the same thing with less pay." He licked sugar off his thumb. "Marija. You said you had something to tell me. You said it four times this week. Each time you said it, you said it was nothing. A thing that is *nothing* does not need to be said four times."

She looked out at the water. Beyond the harbour, low and violet in the haze, lay the island of Brač, steady as a lie.

"Captain Šimić," she said, "has offered me a post."

"The Captain wants you."

"As chief officer. On the *Sveti Duje.* Twenty-four months. Rijeka, Suez, Busan, Shanghai, and back. There is no shore leave for the first year. The pay is—" she named it, and Ivan whistled. "I have until eight o'clock tomorrow morning to answer him."

"Well," said Ivan, "that is wonderful."

"Yes."

"That is *magnificent.* You will be the youngest chief officer in the Adriatic fleet."

"I know."

"Then why," said Ivan, and he did not stop smiling, because he was the kind of man who smiled as a ship keeps its lights on in a gale, "why are you looking at me like a woman reading out a sentence?"

"Because," said Marija, "I wrote to an astronomer in England, three winters ago, and it was the only honest sentence I have ever written. It is in the Register. I will not tell you which number."

"You do not have to."

"I know. I am going to tell you, anyway, what it says." She took a breath, as one takes a bearing. "*My captain wants me; so does the sea. You are third.*"

There was a very long silence. "Third," said Ivan Horvat.

"I am sorry."

"Third." He turned the word over like a coin. "Do you know, Marija, that on a ship the third mate is the officer who stands the watch from midnight until four? When the Captain is in his bunk, and the chief is in his, and the sea is at her blackest and every other soul aboard has gone to sleep. It is the *third* who is awake. Who holds the ship. Who sees the dawn before any of them." He grinned, wide, with an effort that cost him something. "I have always thought it the most honourable post afloat. I would be glad of it."

"Ivan, that is not—"

"I will take the dog watch, then. Gladly. Somebody should be up."

"I did not want you to be *glad.*"

"What did you want?"

She said, very low, "I wanted you to be angry. I wanted you to *ask me to stay.*"

"Ah," said Ivan, and for once he did not smile. "That is the difficulty. If I ask you to stay, you will; and if you stay, you will be a woman who stayed. I knew a girl at school who never forgave a man for a thing he did not ask. I would rather be third than be the reason."

### SCENE II
*The same night. A quay, a hundred paces down the harbour. A tall gangway, a large man, and a very large supper.*

> *2130. Cook arrived. Reports he was "detained by destiny." Destiny, on inquiry, turned out to be a barmaid in Kaštela. Wind backing SW, force 5. Visibility moderate. Cook says it is the first time in thirty years he has been seasick *before* sailing.*

The ship's cook of the *Marjan* was a man called Luka Matić, fifty-five, from Zagreb, who had never in his life been outdone in vanity by anyone but his own moustache. He was a chef of great and terrible talent. He had once, he claimed, been dismissed from a presidential luncheon for "being too delicious," and had taken to the sea at fifty because "a man who will not be a legend on land must be one at sea, where no one can check."

He stood now at the foot of the gangway with a stockpot in his arms, regarding the departing deck rating with a long, shrewd, meddling eye.

"You have *told* her."

"I have said nothing, Luka."

"You have said *nothing,* in the loud way. Nothing, with the shoulders." He put down the pot. "There is a technique. You do not say 'I love you.' That is for the amateurs. You say, 'I have made you a stew.' You see? It means the same, and nobody can sue."

"Luka. She has been offered the sea."

"The sea!" Luka threw up his hands. "The sea is a *brute.* The sea has no manners. It will not remember her birthday. It has never once thanked me for a meal." He lowered his voice; his hand, Ivan noticed, went briefly to his side, below the ribs, and came away again, in the quick way of a man brushing off a crumb. "The truth is, boy, that most people spend their whole lives choosing between two things they love, and the thing they *lose* is whichever they were too busy to hold. Do not stand there like a bollard. Go and *do* something."

"What?"

"Something stupid!" said Luka. "That is the only kind of thing that has ever worked!"

He picked up the pot and went up the gangway, humming, a little unsteadily.

Ivan stood alone on the dark quay. Above him the *Marjan* creaked at her lines. Along the Riva, a klapa of six old men in white shirts had begun to sing in the close harmony of that coast, about a girl who waited by the sea and a sailor who could not say when he would come. He listened. Then he walked to the waterfront bar, borrowed a pen, and did a stupid thing.

### SCENE III
*Dawn. The* Sveti Duje*, at her berth. A steel ship, a steel sky, and a steel-grey woman at the head of the gangway, with a piece of paper in each hand.*

> **LOG OF THE M/V *SVETI DUJE.* Entry in the hand of M. Kovač, Second Officer.**
> *0540. Wind NE, force 3, the* bura *arriving, cold and clean. The jugo has gone. Decision outstanding.*
> *0610. Received, delivered by hand by a deckhand of another vessel, a small package, no name. Contents: a paper bag of fritule, now cold; and on the back of the paper bag, in pencil, a note.*

She had read it eleven times.

It said: *I did not ask you to stay. I am asking you, instead, to come back. It is the only question I know how to ask of a person who is going away. — I.*

And under it, smaller: *P.S. The third mate takes the watch you cannot. I will be awake.*

Marija did not weep; it was against regulations. But her chin did something it had never done on duty: it *trembled,* once, like the needle of an instrument finding north.

At 0745, Captain Vlaho Šimić came down the gangway, sixty-one, a large grey walrus of a man in a cap that had survived four ships and two marriages.

"Well, Kovač?" he said gruffly. "I will have your answer."

She looked at him. She looked at the sea beyond him, wide and cold and bright, and at the harbour mouth where, even now, a bulk carrier with a white funnel was easing out under the shadow of Marjan hill, with a small figure at the stern, a long way off, who was too far to be seen and who she knew, without any evidence whatever, was waving.

"I will take the post, Captain."

"Ha!" said the Captain, and clapped her on the shoulder so hard that she very nearly went into the harbour.

"For six months."

"For *six* months?"

"For one rotation. At the end of it I will tell you if I stay."

The Captain's eyebrows travelled up his forehead like two caterpillars discovering a new leaf.

"Is it a man?"

"It is a rotation," said Marija Kovač, with the flat, unarguable calm of an officer at the end of an argument. "That is the whole of the answer. And I will take the watch from midnight until four, whoever is on the roster."

"That is the *third* mate's watch."

"Yes," said Marija. "I know."

He looked at her for a long time, and then, being a man who had been in love twice and wrong only once, he grunted and went up the gangway.

### SCENE IV
*Cairo. A Thursday evening, under a sky the colour of a ripe apricot. The Khan el-Khalili, drums, lanterns, and a family trying to get through a door at once.*

Elsewhere, where the Nile slides under the old bridges like a slow brown thought, a wedding was beginning.

I must be brief, reader, for it was a very long wedding. The *zaffa* began at six: drums, tambourines, a reed pipe that could weep and make you dance in the same bar, forty cousins with candles; and in front of them, in a cream-coloured jacket cut by a tailor who had not been paid and was only attending to see he would be, walked **Omar Haddad,** thirty-five, antiques dealer, and the finest liar in the whole of Egypt, with the look of a man who had, for the first time in his life, nothing to sell.

The *katb el-kitab,* the signing of the contract, was done in the courtyard of the bride's family house, beneath a trellis hung with jasmine. The officiant was a venerable sheikh with a beard like a cloud. The witnesses were Karim, a potter, built like a wardrobe and weeping like a fountain, and Tarek Hassan, a librarian of forty-one, who drank tea at every hour and had read too many contracts to trust any of them.

"Read the conditions," said the sheikh kindly.

Tarek unrolled the paper. Layla Mansour, thirty-two, the bride, in a gown of ivory silk, sat beneath her veil with a back as straight as an argument. For she was a restorer of antiquities, and the Islamic law, in its wisdom, permits a bride to add her own conditions to the contract; and this one had added her own with the exactness of one cataloguing a tomb.

"*'The first,'*" read Tarek, with a quiver, "*'that the husband shall never sell the wife anything without a receipt.'*"

"*'The second: that if the husband should at any time describe an object as "genuine," he shall provide a certificate; and if there is none, he shall provide a confession.'*"

"Third," read Tarek, who had begun to enjoy himself and so had begun to sweat. "*'That the husband shall come home on time on Fridays; and that, should he be late, he shall say plainly that the clock was wrong, and not that he was detained by an Italian count.'*"

"That," said Omar, "was *one time.*"

"*'Fourth: that the bride was not deceived.'*" Tarek read slowly, and the courtyard fell still. "*'She knew it was false. She kept it because it was his.'*" He cleared his throat. His eyes had gone suddenly very bright. "I do not think," he said, "that I have read a contract before in which the *clauses* made me want to cry."

They proceeded. The bride said yes. The bridegroom said yes, a little louder than needed, as a man says yes who has had much practice saying no to himself. And the women in the courtyard let loose the *zaghareet,* that long, high, trembling trill of joy that goes up from the throat of Egypt like a flock of white birds, and for one long minute the whole of Cairo seemed to be ululating.

There was a gift. Omar's was a small clay lamp from the Fayoum, plain, chipped, entirely genuine, with a certificate of provenance signed by three experts and a customs officer.

"It is *real,*" said Omar. "I swear it on every receipt I ever lost."

"I believe you," said Layla.

"Truly?"

"I have tested it." She smiled, behind the veil, for the first time. "Now. Mine."

She gave him a flat package wrapped in a newspaper. He opened it. Inside, in a frame of plain wood, restored with infinite care to its own original imperfection, was the forgery, the little false scarab he had sold her six years ago and never confessed to, with a typed card beneath: **GENUINE FAKE. Circa six years ago. Provenance: the heart.**

Omar Haddad, who had once sold a pyramid to a Dutchman, stood for a long time with his mouth open, and said nothing at all.

At the back of the courtyard, among the aunts, a fourteen-year-old girl named Dina Shaker went very pink and very still. For she had told no one that it was she, one wet afternoon last year, who had posted a certain small, anonymous note through a certain shop door, and the thing had grown and grown, as these things will, until here it was, in jasmine, with drums.

"Why are you smiling like a cat in a dairy?" asked her aunt.

"It is the blush of the wicked," said Tarek, passing with fresh tea. "A child who has done good in secret and cannot bear it. I am a librarian. I know where the bodies are shelved."

### SCENE V
*Naples. The Via dei Tribunali. A pizzeria older than the street's last three governments. A Saturday night, queue to the door, and a baker in a state.*

I promised a third wedding, did I not? It is not a wedding. It is the thing before: the question.

> **Alessandro Greco** was thirty-three and had spent six years in a war with a pastry chef. It had involved a stolen recipe, a rigged contest, and one very memorable incident with a hundred kilos of flour and a Vespa.

Now he stood in his own pizzeria, with a clean white apron, a hand that was not quite steady, and a calzone.

"A calzone," said Paolo Ricci, thirty, choir director, his oldest friend and his worst gossip, from the end of the counter. "You are going to propose with a *folded pizza.*"

"It is a *symbol.*"

"Of what?"

"A pizza," said Alessandro, "is open. A pizza is a thing that shows you everything. A *calzone* is a pizza that has closed its heart and is only waiting for the right person to cut it."

Paolo wiped an eye. "It is almost *sacred.* Shall I sing? I have composed a short aria."

"Paolo, if you sing, I will bake you."

The door chimed. In came Chiara Esposito, thirty-one, pastry chef, with flour in her hair and a smile on her face that was not entirely trustworthy, because she had been told by seven separate people that she was needed urgently at the pizzeria and had worked out why by the fourth.

"You have summoned me," she said, "in front of everyone."

"I have not summoned you."

"Matteo Ricci told me there was a fire."

"He is a welder, he sees fire everywhere."

"Alessandro." She came up to the counter and looked at what lay on it: a golden half-moon, crimped at the edge, sleek with oil. "What is this?"

"I stole your recipe," said Alessandro, in a tremendous rush. "Six years ago. The one for the dough. I told myself it was *competition*. It was not competition. It was that it was the most beautiful thing I had ever read, in the most beautiful handwriting, and it said at the bottom, *for whoever finds this,* and I thought, *that is me,* and then I was too proud to say so. I made it every day. I have never once said sorry, and I am sorry. This is made from it. Cut it."

"Cut what?"

"The *calzone.*"

Chiara looked at him, and at the whole queue, which had stopped breathing.

"I wrote that recipe," she said slowly, "for you to find."

"I *know,*" said Alessandro. "I worked it out in the fourth year. I am slow. I am a pizzaiolo. We are a patient people."

"You are an *idiot.*"

"I am a *closed calzone,*" said Alessandro. "Cut me."

She took the knife. She cut it, and the steam rose, and out of the ricotta and the ham and the heat tumbled a small gold ring, which rolled across the marble and came to rest against her wrist, and stopped.

"Your dough," said Chiara, picking up the ring and wiping it on her apron, "is too thick."

"That is *not an answer.*"

"It is the beginning of one," said Chiara Esposito, and put the ring on her finger, and the whole of the Via dei Tribunali, which had heard nothing in the last four minutes but its own heart, burst into noise. Paolo Ricci began his aria, which was dreadful. Nobody stopped him. Alessandro, at last, was crying into a bowl of flour, and being kissed, once, on the forehead, by a woman who tasted of cinnamon and fury.

"*Ué, guagliò,*" breathed the lottery man, wiping his eyes. "Now *that* is a Saturday."

### SCENE VI
*Night. The open Adriatic, a hundred miles from any shore. Two ships, a mile apart, on opposite courses. The VHF radio on the bridge of the* Sveti Duje*, in the dark.*

> *0140. Wind NW, force 4. Sea slight. The* Marjan *has just been raised on the radar, bearing 232, range 1.4 miles, steering a reciprocal course.*
> *0141. I have not slept since Wednesday.*
> *0142. Hailed her on Channel 16. For operational purposes only.*

"*Marjan, Marjan, Marjan.* This is *Sveti Duje.* *Sveti Duje.* Over."

There was a crackle. The sea hissed along the hull. A long, very long, ten seconds went by, in which the whole Adriatic seemed to lean in.

"*Sveti Duje, Sveti Duje.* This is *Marjan.* Receiving you, strength five. Over." A pause. "Is that the *chief officer?*"

"It is the officer of the watch. Over."

"The *third* watch?"

"The third watch," said Marija, and her voice shook very slightly, and she cursed it. "Over."

"I am on watch also. Over."

"Understood. Over."

"It is a fine night. Over."

"Affirmative. Over."

Another long, hissing silence. On the *Marjan,* a hundred miles from anywhere, a deck rating who ought to have been asleep stood on the wing of a dark bridge with a radio in his hand and a cook breathing down his neck. "*Say it!*" whispered Luka, from the shadow of the funnel. "*Say anything stupid!*"

"*Sveti Duje.*" Ivan's voice was careful as a man crossing a plank. "I have a message. Did you receive my paper bag? Over."

"Received. Over."

"Did you eat the fritule? Over."

"Negative. Over."

"Did you read the message? Over."

"Affirmative. Over."

"And?"

"Say again, *Marjan.* You did not say 'over.'"

"*And—over.*"

There was a very long silence. Marija Kovač stood alone in the glow of the radar, with the stars uncountable above her and a boy in a canvas coat a mile away across the dark. She lifted the handset.

"*Marjan,*" she said. "I have revised the order of priority."

"Say again?" said Ivan, hoarsely.

"Negative," said Marija. "I will say it in port. Over."

"Which port?"

"The first one," said Marija, "that either of us arrives at. Out."

The radio clicked. The two ships slid past one another in the dark, a mile apart, their running lights red and green like two small questions, and went each their way.

On the *Marjan,* a deck rating sat down very suddenly on a coil of rope.

On the *Sveti Duje,* a second officer wrote in her log, in her small, level hand, the one entry that for twelve years she had never made. It was a single line. It was not a position. It was not a bearing. It said:

> *0151. Course steady. Heart: recorded.*
