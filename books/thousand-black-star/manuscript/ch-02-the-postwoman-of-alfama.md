## Chapter 2
### The Postwoman of Alfama

> *"I wrote to a woman whose face I have never seen."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0753

---

Lisbon is built upon seven hills, and the Post has been trying to climb all of them since eighteen hundred and something.

It does not climb them well. A letter in Alfama does not travel; it is *carried*, up stairs that were laid out by a medieval drunkard, past walls tiled in blue by a hundred dead hands, beneath washing-lines strung from window to window like the bunting of a small and unwinnable war. And the carrier of the letters in that quarter, for nine years, had been a young woman named Inês Carvalho, who knew every door in it by its knock, every dog by its opinion, and every widow by the particular quality of her gratitude.

She did not sleep that night. At six, with the sky going from pewter to pearl above the river, she was already on the Rua de São Miguel with her yellow satchel and her red bicycle (which she pushed, for no bicycle born could ride that street), and in the satchel, beneath the gas bills and the pension slips, lay one envelope that was not hers.

She did not need to take it out. She had it by heart.

It had come to the sorting office seven months before, among the dead letters, which is the name the Post gives to mail that belongs to nobody: wrong addresses, vanished tenants, the tender misspellings of the very old. The envelope was cream, a little soft at the corners, addressed in careful block capitals to *SENHORA, RUA DA SAUDADE 14, LISBOA.* There is a Rua da Saudade in a number of Portuguese towns, for we are a people who name our streets after what we miss. There is none in Lisbon. Inês had held it up to the lamp, and a thing she had never before done in nine years of honest service had happened: she had opened it, and had told herself, with the ease of a woman who is lying to the one person who will believe her, that it was a matter of *procedure.*

*To the lady who writes small kindnesses on the backs of other people's envelopes:*

*I am a man who mends things that carry others, and I would like, once, to be carried.*

It was that second line. She had read the rest, a page and a half, in the bath, with the taps running to cover the sound of her own breathing, and every word of it had been courteous and clear and slightly frightened. But it was the second line that lodged in her like a fish-bone. *I would like, once, to be carried.*

She had never answered it. There was nobody to answer. It was signed with no name at all, only a drawing, a very small drawing, in pencil, of a wheel.

And now, on the screen of Pereira's cracked phone, in the night just gone, there had been a sentence beneath a number: *I wrote to a woman whose face I have never seen.*

*Entry 0753. Lisbon.*

She had sent the lamp-seller home. She had stayed on alone among the sleeping sorting machines, with the night shift gone to its coffee, and there on her own small phone she had pressed thumb and forefinger against the screen to enlarge the scan, as one pulls back a curtain.

The Register, you understand, did not keep only the words of its confessors. It kept the *paper.* Seraphine Wyndham-Rao had been an astronomer, and astronomers do not trust a report of a star; they want the plate. So each letter had been photographed whole, cream and blue-lined and stained and creased, and the hand of 0753 was there in the open air for anyone to see: small, square, careful, upright as a row of soldiers, every letter built as if load-bearing.

Inês laid the phone upon the table, and beside it, very gently, the envelope.

The capitals were the same.

"*Ai,*" said Inês Carvalho, to the machines. And then, as a precaution, "*Ai, ai, ai.*"

---

By nine o'clock the news had gone round Alfama the way news does there: by no known channel, and faster than light.

"It is *you*," said Pereira, who had arrived at the foot of the Escadinhas with a barrow of lamps and the expression of a man who has found a truffle. "I said it last night, did I not? I said, her ears have gone red. And what does a woman do with red ears and a Register? She searches for a *man.*"

"I am not searching for a man."

"You are searching for a *handwriting*, which is a man with his clothes off."

"Pereira!"

"I am a lamp-seller, Inês. I deal in revelation." He held up a brass lamp the size of a cat. "For you, a special price, eighty euros. Read your love-letters by a better light."

"I do not have love-letters."

"Then why are you smelling envelopes?"

She was. It is a thing postwomen do. She had been going down the street with the morning's post and holding each envelope a moment to her nose, as a sommelier holds a glass, for the cheap ink of the Tax Office, the lavender of the widows, the cigarettes of the men who wrote to football clubs. She was searching, by smell and by script, for a square hand that built every letter as if it carried weight.

It was not a good method. It produced, in the course of one hour, the following events:

She delivered Senhor Matos's pension to the widow Matos's cat, which sat upon the step and refused to be signed for.

She gave the Tax Office's final demand to a young man in a bakery who had been the one *sending* it, and who took it, read it, and went quite grey.

She stopped a stranger outside the Sé Cathedral, a pale German in new walking boots, and asked him with great solemnity to write the words *BOA NOITE* on the back of her hand.

"Why?" said the German.

"It is a matter of *procedure.*"

"Is it a crime?"

"Not yet," said Inês, studying the capital B, "but I am hopeful."

It was in the Rua dos Remédios, outside a plumber's van, that she came to the first suspect.

"Inês, my *life,*" said Rui Costa, plumber, thirty-eight, in a voice like warm gravel. He had been, for eleven months at the age of twenty-six, the great sorrow of her life, and had since become a mild irritation, as great sorrows do when they are properly stored. He was wearing a vest and a look of suspicious innocence. "I saw it. I read it. The Register. And I thought to myself, Rui, you fool. She has been waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For the man who wrote to the woman whose face he never saw." He placed a hand upon his heart. "It was I."

"Rui."

"It was *I*, Inês. I wrote it years ago. In block capitals. I am a man of block capitals." He indicated the invoice pad on the dashboard, where indeed the numbers had been stamped in a hand of formidable squareness. "You see? The hand!"

She looked. It was a square hand, certainly. It was also a hand in which the number *four* had a small curl at the foot, like a pig's tail, and the capital *R* had a leg that kicked out at the world.

"Rui," she said gently. "You have seen my face."

"Naturally."

"You pinched my cheek at my sister's wedding."

"It was a *tender* pinch."

"You told me I looked like a boiled egg in a hat."

"It was a very *affectionate* egg."

"So you cannot have written to a woman whose face you have never seen."

Rui Costa considered this. He was not stupid, merely hopeful, which is the more dangerous condition.

"Then perhaps," he said, "I wrote it to another."

"Perhaps you did."

"Very likely it is *her* I meant." He stared into the middle distance. "A *mystery* woman. Hm. I will write to her again."

"Please do. Take your time."

She was walking away, with the German's *BOA NOITE* drying on her hand, when she heard behind her the long, aggrieved sigh of a man who has been wrong and will shortly be very loud about it. She did not turn round. A postwoman, like a sailor, learns not to look back at weather.

---

It was on a bench at the Miradouro de Santa Luzia, that same noon, with the whole bright shabby city laid out below her like a letter spread to dry, that her sister found her.

Rita Rocha, thirty-two, journalist, wore the face she kept for other people's scandals: bright and attentive and entirely without mercy. She sat. She unwrapped a pastry. She held it out.

"I have been wanting to say a thing," said Rita.

"Do not."

"It is about your entry."

"My *entry*?" Inês did not look round. "I have no entry."

"Entry zero-zero-fifty-three. 'I kept one letter that was never mine.' Lisbon. It is quite beautifully put."

There was a pause, in which a tram went grinding up the hill behind them, and a pigeon, which had been planning to approach the pastry, thought better of it.

"I never posted that," said Inês.

"No," said her sister. "You did not."

"I wrote it. I put it in a drawer."

"You did."

"It was in an envelope that I *sealed*, Rita, and it had no stamp on it, and it was in the drawer in the *kitchen,* under the string."

"It was," said Rita, "an excellent place for it. Very secure. I had to move the string."

"*Rita.*"

"Do not be angry. You were crying into the sink. I am your elder, and you were crying into the sink. And a person who cries into a sink about a letter should not keep the letter in a drawer; she should post it to a woman in England who will keep it for ever and tell no one." She took a bite of pastry. "As, you will observe, she did."

"She was seventy-eight!"

"She was *efficient,*" said Rita.

Inês opened her mouth, and found that all the sharp things she wished to say had melted, and that in their place was a soft, hot, helpless feeling, like a letter discovering that someone has finally read it.

"You are the worst journalist in Portugal," she said at last.

"I am the *best,*" said Rita. "I have just got you to admit it is yours."

She stood, brushing sugar from her coat. "And the man? The one who wrote to the woman with no face?"

"I have a handwriting."

"Have you a name?"

"No."

"Then find him," said Rita Rocha, "before somebody else is *found*," and she was gone down the steps in a flurry of scarf, leaving upon the bench a bitten pastry and a much-embarrassed postwoman.

*Mark this, reader.* It is a curious thing, but the people who say *I never told* are very seldom the ones who did not. The truth is a letter of its own, and it finds its post.

---

*A street in Santo Amaro. A tram depot, long and green-lit and smelling of oil and hot metal. In the pit beneath an old yellow carriage, a pair of boots, and a voice.*

"Now, Dona Conceição," said the boots. "We have discussed this. You are a lady of ninety-eight, and you have a gear-box like a general's stomach, and you will *not* seize up on me today. I have a feeling about today."

"*What* feeling?" said a second voice, above, from the platform. This was Nuno Pereira, twenty-seven, mechanic, best friend, and liar by vocation. He held his phone aloft like a priest with a relic. "Did you see it? The Register? There is a woman in Lisbon with a line like a knife. *'I kept one letter that was never mine.'* Entry zero-zero-fifty-three."

"Who keeps a letter that is not hers?"

"A thief. A romantic. A postwoman."

"Oh, come." The boots shifted. "There are three hundred postwomen in Lisbon."

"But how many kept yours?"

There was a short, hard silence in the pit, in which the cooling engine ticked like a clock.

"Mine," said the boots, "was a dead letter. It went to a nonexistent street. It was supposed to be *unfindable.* That was the *point.*"

"A man who sends a love-letter to a street that does not exist," said Nuno, with deep admiration, "is either a poet or a fool, and I have always said you were both."

Tomás Villaverde, twenty-nine, tram-mechanic, slid out upon his creeper as if the engine were delivering him. He had a spanner in one hand and a smear of black grease from the temple to the jaw, and the look of someone who has stared into the dark so long that it has begun to stare back with shapes.

"I reasoned it out," he said. "I did not know her address. I know only her hand. So I wrote to *nowhere.* And the Post, if it is a decent institution, sends nowhere to somebody. I thought: the dead letters will go to the desk of the woman who writes on them. And she will either read it, or she will not." He wiped his face, and made it worse. "That is seven months, Nuno. In seven months I have had one reply. It was from the tax office, and it was about my *car.*"

"Perhaps she is shy."

"Perhaps she is sixty, with a moustache."

"She is," said Nuno, who had never seen her, "exactly sixty, and has a moustache like a Habsburg admiral."

"You are a liar."

"And a very good friend of liars," Nuno agreed. "I can recommend it."

But Tomás had slipped back beneath the carriage, and his voice, thinned by the iron above him, took on the strange musical quality of a man who is talking to himself in the one place where he is sure of no audience. He was not speaking in prose. He had not meant to. The engine had asked him, in its way, as engines do.

> *They say a man who mends the things that move
> Is moved by none. I say it is not so.
> I have been underneath this yellow thing
> A thousand nights, and listened to it ache,
> And known the very shiver in its wheel
> Before the driver felt it. That is love,
> Or else it is a trade, and I forget
> Which one I practise. Lady, if you read
> The small square words I sent into the dark,
> I do not ask you answer. Only know
> That somewhere underneath a tram, a man
> Is lying on his back, and looks at iron,
> And sees, quite plainly, through the iron, you.*

"That was very good," said Nuno, from above, after a respectful pause.

"It was the gear-box."

"It was *poetry.*"

"It was *oil,*" said Tomás, and hit the gear-box, which, as if repentant, began to hum.

---

At twenty minutes past four, at the Largo das Portas do Sol, tram twenty-eight stopped.

It had not intended to. It stopped as an elderly person stops upon a staircase: suddenly, with dignity, and with an air of having been betrayed by the stairs. A hundred tourists leaned out of the windows to photograph their own disappointment. The driver spread his hands to heaven. Up the hill, in a white cloud of ceremony, came Pereira with a barrow, selling lamps to the stranded, and behind him, shaking her head in the steady rhythm of a woman who has seen too many things go wrong in Alfama to be shocked by another, came the postwoman.

"A *call* has been made," said Pereira. "The mechanic comes."

"I don't care."

"He is said to be the best in the depot."

"I do not care *at all.*"

"And his name," said Pereira, who had a nose for it, "is *Tomás Villaverde.*"

Inês stopped.

Now it must be said, for the sake of accuracy, that she had not known the name. She had known a hand. But Rita, who is a journalist, had known someone who knew someone who had once fixed her washing machine, and Rita had sent a message at noon: *Tram depot, Santo Amaro, a Villaverde. Hands like a surgeon's, jokes like a cousin's, no wife.* To be right on a hunch about a stranger is a sorrow and a joy together, and Inês felt both at once, like two hot coins in one pocket.

She did not look at the tram. She looked at the pavement.

Beneath the carriage, from out of the shadow beneath it, protruded a pair of old black boots.

They did not move. A voice came from within the iron, the voice of a man engaged in an argument with a mechanism and slightly winning. "*Come on,*" it said. "Come on, my lady. Have a little courage. One more turn of the bolt and we shall both be heroes."

Inês, who had a satchel to deliver, a round to finish, and a handwriting to find, stood there with the whole city looking on and discovered, to her considerable annoyance, that her knees had gone.

The boots slid out. A body followed, in a blue boiler-suit gone black at the elbows. A face looked up from the pavement: long, serious, rather tired, with a smear of oil from temple to jaw and a pair of eyes the colour of a wet slate roof.

It was not, she thought, a face she knew. And he, for his part, looked upon the yellow satchel, and the red bicycle, and the cold-reddened ears, and the hand on which a faint blue *BOA NOITE* was running in the sun, and did not know hers either.

You will observe, reader, what is happening, for neither of them does. She has come by a hundred roads to the one man in Lisbon, and he has lain under a hundred trams for the one woman, and here, at the top of the hill, with a hundred tourists photographing the sky, the two of them stand a yard apart and do not know it. It is the cruelest of the old comedies, and the kindest.

"I am sorry," said Tomás. "I'm afraid I am in your way."

"You are not in my way. You are in the *road.*"

"A fine distinction."

"I am a postwoman," said Inês. "I am *rather* precise about roads."

"Ah." He sat up. A peculiar thing happened to his face, a tightening and a slackening together, like a man who has caught his own name called in a crowd. "A postwoman."

"Yes."

"In Alfama?"

"Nine years."

"Then you must know," said Tomás, with the elaborate lightness of the frightened, "what becomes of a letter when it has no address."

The hundred tourists had gone quiet. Pereira, who was the soul of discretion when it paid him, put down a lamp.

"We keep it," said Inês.

"For how long?"

"Seven years. Then it is burned."

"*Seven.*"

"The Post is very patient. It is only people who are in a hurry." She was looking at the oil on his chin, and at nothing else, since she did not dare. "Seven months so far."

She saw him do the arithmetic. She saw it go through him as a current goes through a wire, and was glad of it, and alarmed, and could not in the whole world have said why, except that a man who counts months has plainly been counting them.

"And is the lady," said Tomás, very slowly, "who keeps such things... is she kind?"

"She is nosy," said Inês.

"I had heard that."

"She reads everything twice."

"I had heard *that,* too." He was getting up. He was taller than she had expected; he had to put a hand on the tram to find his balance. "She is, I am told, sixty, with a moustache."

"She is *twenty-seven,*" said Inês, crimson to the hairline, "and has never shaved her lip in her life."

For a second there was nothing in the whole hill-top. Then a tourist from Ohio began to clap, and then his wife, and then the carriage full, and Pereira, who had never missed an occasion, said in a very loud and sacred voice, "A special price on lamps!" and Tomás Villaverde, wearing an expression no mechanic has ever worn in the history of the Carris, said:

"I think my name is Tomás."

"I think mine is Inês."

"The post," said Tomás, "is slow."

"But sure," said Inês, and took out of her satchel a cream envelope, soft at the corners, and held it for one moment against her chest, where he could see it, and did not give it to him. "I will have to *check* the address."

Behind them, with a long metallic sigh of relief, Dona Conceição gave up her objections and shuddered into motion.
