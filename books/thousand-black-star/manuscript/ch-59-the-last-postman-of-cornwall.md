## Chapter 59
### The Last Postman of Cornwall

> *"There are forty letters I never delivered."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0266

---

**Penhallow Cove, Cornwall. 6:10 a.m. The last Saturday of October.**

The sub-post-office at Penhallow had a counter, a scale, a stamp machine that spoke only in Cornish grunts, and Mrs. Trelawny, who had the ears of a bat and the discretion of a church bell.

"It is a disgrace," said Mrs. Trelawny, to the empty shop, which agreed with her. "Forty-five years. Forty-five years the man has walked that round, in hail and fog and the great snow of eighty-seven, and now they will send a van from Truro with a boy in it who cannot tell a Trevithick from a Tregenza. *And* they are shutting the counter. By the New Year I shall be selling stamps from a *bucket.*"

"Morning, Ruth," said Harry Whitcombe.

He stood in the doorway in the red jacket he had worn for forty-five years, with the bag across his chest and a shortbread tin under his arm. The tin was tartan, dented, decorated with a Highland cow whose expression had worn thin from handling.

Harry was seventy, with the stoop of a man who has been bowing to letterboxes since the days of decimalisation, a face as creased and kind as a well-used envelope, and the largest store of other people's secrets in the county, which he had never, in all his years, spent.

"Last round, Harry," said Mrs. Trelawny, with tears in her eyes and a saffron bun in her fist. "Eat. You will need it."

"Thank you, Ruth."

"What's in the tin?"

"Biscuits."

"It does not rattle like biscuits."

"It is a very quiet biscuit."

"I have known you since you were twenty-five," said Mrs. Trelawny, "and in forty-five years you have never carried a tin. You have carried a *face.* That tin is *something.*" She leaned across the counter, with the entire small village behind her eyes. "Harry Whitcombe. Is it a *woman?*"

"It is," said Harry, going red from the collar up, "a matter for the Royal Mail."

"Which is no longer in this building," said Mrs. Trelawny, with satisfaction, and handed him the sorting list, and a second bun.

---

**The round.**

It was a morning of the colour of slate. The lanes of Penhallow ran down the hill to the harbour like loose threads, and Harry walked them, as he had walked them for forty-five years, in a particular rhythm that his feet knew and he did not, letterbox, gate, step, letterbox, until it felt less like a job than a prayer in a language of doors.

At Trevean Farm, a collie came round the barn at a low run, with his ears flat and the light of an old and sacred purpose in his eye.

"Not today, Boscawen," said Harry. "It is my last."

The dog, the fourth Boscawen of that name, who had inherited from his father, his grandfather and his great-grandfather a settled determination to bite the left cuff of the postman's trousers every Tuesday and most Saturdays, paused, consulted his conscience, and bit it.

"Thank you," said Harry. "I would have been disappointed."

At Mrs. Penhale's he left a catalogue of seeds, at the fishmonger's, Tamsin Trevithick, a postcard from her sister in Perth, at the Methodist chapel a bill, and, at every door along the way, an item of news, for he was the last man in the district who could be trusted with it. "She's had a girl." "He's got the job." "It's from the hospital, but it's good." They came out to say goodbye with their hands in their sleeves and their eyes on his tin, and Harry said goodbye to forty-three houses, and did not once look up the hill at Chapel Row.

For he had spent forty years not looking at Chapel Row. He had grown so skilled at it that he could walk past number three with his eyes on the gutter and know, through the soles of his boots, whether the light in the window was on.

It was always on. It was a window in which a woman sat to sew, for the light, she told the village. The light was very good. It was the best light in Penhallow.

At the head of the lane he stopped, and, for the first time in his life, looked in his tin.

There were forty letters inside, each in a cream envelope, each addressed in the same neat upright hand to *Miss D. Penrose, 3 Chapel Row, Penhallow,* each stamped, not one posted. They lay in the tin like a stack of small, patient, folded doves.

He had written one every year, on the night before the fourteenth of March, which was her birthday. He had never sent a single one. A postman knows better than any man alive the size of the gap between a letter and its door: it is eleven paces and a flap of tin, and it can last a lifetime.

He stood, and spoke, and the wind took the words, as it takes most things in Cornwall:

> Forty times the winter, forty times
> The primrose, and I carried every word
> To every door in Penhallow but one.
> I knew the weight of what she'd had from banks;
> I knew her bills, her birthday cards, the lilt
> Her sister's handwriting took when she was well.
> I did not know what I should say to her.
> There is no form for it. No pre-paid slip.
> A man may carry love through all the weather
> That God sends down on Cornwall, and not once
> Contrive to lay it on a mat and leave.
> And now the round is done, and I am retired,
> And what is left to carry? Forty envelopes.
> Forty. And one more street. I'll try the cliff.

He turned the other way, up the long road to the headland, and left Chapel Row, as he had for forty years, for later.

**Lantern House. 10:05 a.m.**

"Mr. Whitcombe," said Nadeesha Fernando. "You are in time for the tea, and for the world."

"Miss Fernando."

"Korea, India, Zambia, Japan." She held the door. "I have been waiting for you all week. Everyone has been writing to the Keeper, and the Keeper, as you know, has not been in a position to reply. I have four of the letters, and I have not read them aloud, because they did not seem like letters to be read alone, and you are the nearest thing in this county to a *postman,* and I thought you should hear them, since you carried the first thousand."

He had. He had carried every one of the thousand letters up the cliff path to the oak cabinet in this house, over forty-five years: letters in a hundred languages, with stamps from places he had to look up. He had never opened one. He had known, though, as a postman knows, from the weight of the paper and the hesitation of the handwriting, that they were a different kind of mail. They were not for the post. They were for the thing the post was for.

She made him tea in the kitchen, in a cup the size of a small bucket. And then, at the long table, among the wool and the seagull feathers and the great cold plain of the Atlantic in the window, she opened them, one by one, and read.

> **Seoul.** *Dear Keeper: I have now measured everyone in this city, and one man twice. Daniel has resigned from his team and sits in my shop in the afternoons, folding silk and complaining that it is quiet. I have sewn him a coat. It fits. I enclose the lining; it is the colour of the quiet he asked for. — Ji-woo Han (#0351). P.S. He says to tell you that he has stopped checking the score of things.*

> **Delhi.** *Dear Madam: Platform six. 5:40. For twenty years the train has not stopped there, and the woman in the blue sari has stood there anyway. On the fourteenth, I stopped it. I have received a letter of warning from the Railways, which I have framed. It is the best letter I have ever received. — Ravi Chaudhary (#0363). We are to be married at the new year. She says I should apologise to the signalman. I shall.*

> **Lusaka.** *Dear Keeper: We were married on Saturday in the school yard, with two hundred children singing in four keys and none of them ours. Grace sold maize to the wedding guests. She says a wedding is no reason to close a stall. My children call her "Mama Grace-and-the-Maize." She does not mind. — Joseph Tembo (#0133).*

> **Sapporo.** *Dear Madam: He built me a sculpture that would not melt, from a kind of resin. I made him take it down. I told him I prefer the ones that melt, because then he has to come and make another. He says that is the most sensible thing anyone has said about art. I have kept the first one in a bottle: it says "Takumi, February." — Yuki Nakamura (#0556).*

Harry sat with his tea going cold. He had the look of a man who has been hit very gently with a very large object.

"They are good," he said at last, "at saying."

"They are," said Nadeesha. "They had a thousand letters before them, and they learned from them."

"Miss Fernando." He cleared his throat. "I have a tin."

"I know you do."

"How—"

"Mrs. Trelawny telephoned at six." She smiled, and her eyes were tired and bright. "She says it is a matter for the Royal Mail. And Mr. Whitcombe, I have read Entry 0266. I read all of them. I read it on the first night." She reached over, very gently, and put her hand for a second on his. "I read another one, too, in the same county. It is Entry 0918. I am not at liberty to say what it says. The Keeper would not allow it. But I will say that it has been *waiting,* and I will say that you know the street."

Harry looked at the great grey window.

"Eleven paces," he said. "And a flap of tin."

"It is generally less far than the heart supposes," said Nadeesha. "One more thing. If you are going out again—" She put an envelope in his bag. "Would you deliver this to Mr. Ashby, at the old coastguard cottage? It is an invitation. For the night of the new moon, a fortnight on. He has not answered the last three."

"He does not read his post," said Harry. "In forty-five years I have taken that man nothing but a seed catalogue and the council tax. He sits in the garden at night with a telescope he can no longer see through." He tucked it in. "I will take it. It is my last day. I will take it *dreckly.*"

"*Dreckly,*" said Nadeesha, "in Cornwall, I understand, means *not in this life.*"

"Not at all," said Harry Whitcombe, rising, with a great new dignity, and the shortbread tin in both hands. "It means *directly.* It is only that the sense of the word has taken a long road."

---

**Chapel Row. Noon.**

Number three had a green door, a brass knocker in the shape of a fish, and a window.

In that window, at a Singer machine of 1951 with a treadle worn pale by sixty years of feet, sat Dorothy Penrose, sixty-eight, retired seamstress, with a mouth like a taut thread, spectacles on a chain, and a back as straight as a ruler.

She was not sewing. She had been not-sewing since ten.

Forty years she had sat in that window. She had taken in wedding dresses, funeral black, christening gowns, a surplice for the vicar and a kilt for a Cornishman who claimed ancestry; and she had told the village it was for the light. It was a good light. It was also, from that chair, the only one that fell exactly on the lane, where, at ten-fifteen every weekday morning, there walked past, with his eyes on the gutter, a postman.

She had never once called him in. She did not know the sentence for it. A woman could take in a hem for a stranger, and put the pins in her mouth, and hold a man's trouser leg and look him in the eye; but there was no regulation, in the great book of what is done, that permitted a woman to say, *Come in and stay.* So she had said instead, forty times, with her mouth full of pins, *That will be one first-class stamp.*

She spoke now to the lane. She did not know she was speaking aloud.

> The light is very good. I tell them so.
> I tell them it's the light. It is, in part.
> And part of it is that a woman who waits
> Should wait at something: wheel, or thread, or glass.
> A woman needs a thing to hold, to do,
> While all the clocks of Cornwall go the long way round.
> Today he comes the last time down the lane.
> They've told me at the shop. The last. The last.
> And I shall hem a seam, and say good morning,
> And watch him walk away with his eyes on the gutter,
> Forty years of Tuesdays. And I shall say—
> What shall I say? I have had forty years.
> I have a jar of stamps and not one letter.

Then she did a thing that she had never done: she stood up from the machine.

She walked the four steps to the front door. She stood with her hand on the latch, and took it off, and put it back. She was, at that moment, in her private estimation, a fool of the first water, in a cardigan.

Outside, in the lane, a number of persons had unexpectedly discovered that the day was fine for hanging washing. Mrs. Trelawny was weighing a sack of potatoes on the pavement, for lack of any other excuse. Tamsin Trevithick, at the fishmonger's, had come to her door with a mackerel in each hand. At the end of the row, with his ears flat and the light of an old and sacred purpose in his eye, sat Boscawen the collie, who had followed the postman into the village for reasons of his own.

And up the lane, in the red jacket, with a shortbread tin held before him like a man bearing a small, delicate casket, came Harry Whitcombe.

He did not look at the gutter.

He walked straight up to the green door, to the brass fish, and he knocked. It was three small knocks, in an old, polite rhythm: the knock of a man who is accustomed to knocking on a door to ask whether the door is open.

Dorothy opened it.

For a moment neither said anything. Behind them the lane held its breath, in every window, in every stoop, with a thoroughness that would have been the envy of the Admiralty.

"Mr. Whitcombe," said Dorothy.

"Miss Penrose."

"You are not delivering."

"I am," said Harry. "A little late."

"How late?"

"Forty years, give or take a Tuesday."

She looked at the tin. She looked at the cow on the lid. She looked, for the first time in forty years, straight into his face without a trouser-leg between them, and found it to be much like her own: lined, and tired, and not at all ready.

"Is that the whole of it?"

"There are forty," said Harry. "One a year. On the night before your birthday. I put on a stamp every time, so they would be correct. I never posted one. I have no excuse. I am a postman. It was the one regulation I could not break."

He held out the tin. She took it. Her hands were quite steady; it was her breath that was not.

She took off the lid. She lifted from the top the last of them, in the cream envelope, and held it, and then, because she was a woman of method, set it back, and reached all the way to the bottom of the pile for the first. The paper had gone the colour of tea. It had the fold of forty years of waiting to be opened.

She read it standing on her own step, in front of the whole of Penhallow:

> *Dear Miss Penrose,*
>
> *I hope you will pardon a postman writing to you, which is not in the regulations. I have delivered to your door six hundred and twelve items since I began the round, and I know the weight of every one. I do not know the weight of what I should like to say, so I am sending only this. Your light is very good. I notice it when I pass. — H. Whitcombe.*

Dorothy lowered the page. Her glasses had misted.

"That is a very short letter," she said, "for forty years."

"I did not want to be a nuisance."

"You were a *postman,*" said Dorothy Penrose, "in a village of three hundred people, and you were forty years walking past a woman who could see you from her window. You were not a nuisance, Harry Whitcombe. You were a *stile.* I climbed over you for forty years, to look at the field."

"I am sorry."

"I will tell you what you are. You are the most expensive pair of trousers I have ever hemmed." She held up the first, and with her other hand, from the pocket of her cardigan, drew out a small glass jar, in which lay, flat and dry and in perfect order, forty first-class stamps. "I charged you a stamp every time. I did not use one. I kept every one. A woman with forty stamps and no one to write to is a woman who knows exactly what she is waiting for."

Harry looked at the jar. He looked at the cuff of his left trouser leg, bitten that morning, as ever.

"There is a thing," he said, "I ought to confess."

"There is nothing else *to* confess. You have confessed a tin."

"The dog."

She paused.

"On the first Tuesday," said Harry, to the pavement, in a very small voice, "I put a sausage in the cuff. In 1986. The first Boscawen never forgot it. He told his sons."

There was a silence the length of Chapel Row.

"A *sausage,*" said Dorothy.

"A small one."

"For forty years."

"The other leg has never had a hole in it."

"I am aware," said Dorothy. "I am a seamstress. I *noticed.*" Her mouth was doing something very difficult. "Forty years I mended a trouser leg that a man had loaded with pork. You, Harry Whitcombe, are the only man alive who has stuffed a collie in order to get a hem."

Behind them Mrs. Trelawny gave a sound like a kettle going off. Tamsin Trevithick dropped a mackerel. Boscawen, who had heard his own name and the word *sausage,* sat up with great expectation.

Dorothy Penrose laughed.

It was a startling sound, because it had been so long coming, and it went up the lane like a bird out of a shut room. She laughed until she had to hold the door; and then, in the middle of it, she was crying, with her glasses off and one hand over her eyes; and Harry Whitcombe, who had been standing at the door of that house for forty years in the only way he knew, put down the tin on her step, quite gently, and held out his two arms, with no regulation whatever.

She walked into them.

"You are forty years late," said Dorothy into the red jacket.

"I know."

"You are not dead. It puts you ahead of most of my customers."

"I try to keep up."

She stepped back. She was crisp again; she dried her face with the back of a hand and looked at him with the eye of one pricing a lining.

"Now," said Dorothy. "I want these done properly. Not all at once; I am sixty-eight, and I will not have forty years of feeling in an afternoon, I should expire on the mat. I want them delivered. By *you.* To this address. One a day. You shall put them through the box at ten-fifteen, in the red jacket, and I shall read each at the machine, and you shall wait on the step for the answer."

"I am retired, Dorothy."

"Then you shall deliver them out of the goodness of your heart. It is a perfectly respectable way to travel." She tucked the jar of stamps into his hand. "These are for the postage. I shall not be using them. The Royal Mail is to be paid."

"It will be a pleasure."

"It will take forty days."

"I have forty days," said Harry Whitcombe, and found, to his great surprise, that he had a great deal more than that.

"Then come in," said Dorothy Penrose, and opened the green door, and for the first time in forty years, did not shut the window of that house on the lane. "The kettle is hot. And we shall see about that sausage."

*In the lane the whole village, as one soul, discovered that it had urgent business elsewhere, and went to it extremely slowly. Mrs. Trelawny took the potatoes, and, by dint of repeating the story, the rest of the day. The dog, who had been given a sausage at last, in public, by a woman with a seamstress's eye, was never heard to bite another cuff.*
