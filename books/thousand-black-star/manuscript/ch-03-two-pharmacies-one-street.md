## Chapter 3
### Two Pharmacies, One Street

> *"I know who paid it. I was waiting for you to say."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0941

---

In Accra there is a lane called Palm Lane, though no palm has stood on it since the British left, and it is eighty yards long, and it holds two shops, one taxi yard, nine opinions, and a war.

At the northern end, in a cream building with green shutters and a red cross painted by a sign-writer who had been paid in kenkey, stood **Owusu Chemist & Dispensary**, whose proprietor, Abena Owusu, twenty-eight, wore a white coat as another woman might wear armour, and counted tablets in fives as if she had caught nature cheating. At the southern end, behind a gate of corrugated iron painted the yellow of a ripe plantain, lay **Mensah Cabs**, forty-one taxis and a goat, belonging to Kwame Mensah, thirty-one, who had never in his life been seen to count anything but other people's blessings.

Between them lay eighty yards of cracked tar, which for six years neither had crossed.

"It is a *scandal,*" said Quaye.

Quaye was fifty-eight, the senior driver of Mensah Cabs, and the leader, by acclamation and by volume, of the Taxi Rank, which is what the drivers of Palm Lane called themselves when they sat upon their bonnets at dawn and considered the affairs of the world. They were a Chorus in all but name, for they spoke when the thing happened and not before, and said what everyone else was thinking a little too loudly.

"A scandal," agreed the Rank.

"Eighty yards."

"Eighty yards," said the Rank.

"I have measured it with my feet. A man could cross in a minute, with a bag of oranges. And these two" (he waved at the cream building and the yellow gate) "have not crossed in six years. What is that? Is that pride? Is that *hatred?* My friends, I have been a taxi driver for thirty-one years, and I tell you that when a man will not cross eighty yards, it is *love.*"

"It is parking," said a young driver, called Yaw, who was twenty-three and worked also as a waiter, and thus had a reputation for realism.

"Everything is parking," said Quaye. "That is how love is carried out in Accra."

For this, in fairness, was the history of the war. The Mensah taxis had begun parking, six years ago, in front of the pharmacy, and the pharmacy's queue of patients, aunties with prescriptions and boys with rashes, had begun to spread across the Mensah yard; and a notice had been affixed to the pharmacy door reading **NO TAXIS. THIS IS A PLACE OF HEALING**; and the Mensah gate had received, two days later, a reply in letters a yard tall: **AND THIS IS A PLACE OF GETTING THERE.** Since that morning the two had communicated exclusively through signs, and the signs had grown steadily in size, wit and venom, until the Ghana Standards Board, passing in a Land Cruiser, had reportedly slowed down to read.

It was against this background that, at nine minutes past six on the morning after the new moon, Kwame Mensah's phone began to play a hymn.

---

"*Chale,*" said Nii Tetteh.

He was Kwame's oldest friend, choir director of the Methodist church at Adabraka, a large devout man with the tender, theatrical face of one who has led nine hundred Sundays of "Rock of Ages." He held the phone out at arm's length, for he had left his spectacles in the choir loft, and he read aloud in the full resonant baritone that he used for the Gospel and for football.

"*Entry six hundred and twenty-five. Accra, Ghana.* 'I paid your father's debt and let you think it was forgiven.'"

The yard went very still. A goat looked up. Under the mango tree, Akosua Tetteh, Kwame's mother, who was sixty, a brilliant cook and a hopeless remembrancer, put down the groundnut she was shelling and said, "Who has paid whose father's debt?"

"Mama," said Kwame.

"Is it a *pharmaceutical* debt?"

"Mama, it is nothing."

"It is the last line of a hymn," said Nii. "You wrote it, Kwame. Two years ago. I drove you to the post office in the rain. You said it was a *medical letter.*"

"I never said that."

"You said it was for your *back.*"

Kwame Mensah could discuss the carburettor of any engine ever manufactured, and had once talked a police captain out of a bribe by asking after his mother. He took the phone with a very steady hand and read the thing he had written. He had written it at a kitchen table, after a bad week, to a woman in England he had never met, because she had promised to tell no one. It was a sentence that had cost him eleven drafts.

He had not expected it to be read in his own yard, by a hundred strangers' eyes, at six in the morning, with a goat.

"And the next one," said Nii, who was a man of kind intentions and limited mercy, scrolling further. "Lord help us. *Entry nine hundred and forty-one. Accra, Ghana.*"

"Do not."

"'I know who paid it. I was waiting for you to say.'"

Now, there is a particular silence, in a place where thirty people have heard a thing, and the thirty are all attempting to appear deaf. It lasted perhaps four seconds. Then a voice from the mango tree, belonging to Ama Tetteh, eighty-two, retired welder, who had said nothing in the yard since Easter, remarked pleasantly:

"Well, *finally.*"

"Ma?"

"I told her six years ago, if she had eyes in her head she'd know." She shelled a groundnut. "A woman does not need a *letter* to know a taxi."

"Which taxi?" said Kwame hoarsely.

"The *one that paid,*" said Ama Tetteh, and cracked her nut.

---

At the other end of the lane, in a dispensary that smelled of menthol and clean linoleum and a faint, honest sweetness of cough syrup, Abena Owusu was reading the same two entries aloud to the same silence, and for the same reason: she had no choice.

For her friend Adwoa, who owned the kiosk beside the pharmacy and who kept everything in the world that happened within eighty yards of Palm Lane in a mental card-index, had arrived at five forty-five with her phone held aloft and her whole face lit like a church window, and had said, "You are *in* it. You are in the Register. And so is *he.*"

"Who?"

"You know *who.*"

"I do not know who," said Abena, with a pharmacist's frigid accuracy. "There are four hundred thousand people in the district. One of them is a vet who owes me eleven cedis."

"The yellow gate."

Abena had put down her pestle.

She was a small woman with a straight back and quick hands and a tongue that she kept, like the poisons cabinet, locked, labelled and open only to the properly qualified. She had run this shop for six years: ever since the spring when her father, Kojo Darko, a good, proud, hopeless man who had founded the dispensary and then remortgaged it twice on the promise of a cocoa scheme, had been told by the bank that it would be taking possession at the end of the month. She had been twenty-two. She had been counting the stock for the last time when a letter arrived, on the bank's thick white paper, to say that the debt had been *settled in full by a benefactor who wishes to remain unnamed.*

She had taken her mother's name, Owusu, over the door that very week, to see how it would look. Her father had died in June. He had been proud of her, and had told her it was nothing, in the last week of his life, as people do who find the great things difficult to say and the small things easy. She had cried at the funeral for the shop, which was easier than crying for the man.

And she had known, in the way one knows a number before the bill arrives, who had paid.

"It is a *taxi* kind of generosity," she said now to Adwoa, who had become very quiet. "It does not leave a name. It leaves a smell of diesel and goodwill. The bank clerk said *a gentleman, tall, very polite, who laughed at his own car.* And then the landlady, Madam Naa, who said she'd had *a clerical error* with my rent, in my *favour,* for six years running. What clerical error is consistent? What clerical error pays on the first of every month, in cash, on a Tuesday?"

"A very *devoted* one," said Adwoa.

"It is a *theft,*" said Abena, her voice cracking. "It is the cruellest theft in Accra. He took from me the chance to *thank* him. I have been walking past that yellow gate for six years with a sentence in my mouth like a stone, and he is *sitting there,* and he *knows,* and I cannot say it because he never said it first."

"So say it."

"I am *waiting for him to say.*"

"Abena," said Adwoa, gently, "so is he."

---

By midday the whole Rank knew, because Quaye had put it on the radio.

It was not exactly the radio. Quaye had, in the roof of his taxi, a small loudspeaker for the announcement of fares, and he had found by experiment that any sentence addressed to it carried over eighty yards of tarmac with the clarity of a sermon. And since the Register of Black Stars had been published, he had been making use of it for the public good.

"*Attention, attention,*" said the roof of the taxi, to the lane, the cream building, the goat and the sky. "*This is your Rank. We read the Register. We have read the Register, and I can tell you the entry of Mr. Kwame is a very fine one. Wait. I will read both of them, since they are not far apart.*"

"Quaye, turn it off," said Kwame, from the yard.

"*It is for your benefit, my friend.*"

"Quaye."

"*'I paid your father's debt and let you think it was forgiven.' Good, good. That is Mr. Kwame, our employer, who is also a pharmacist of a kind, in the sense that he dispenses lifts. And the reply: 'I know who paid it. I was waiting for you to say.' That is Madam Abena, from the pharmacy. She is not here but she is perhaps listening. It is a thing I would not say if I were not an old man who has driven across Ghana nine times, and has learned that there is no eighty yards in the world like eighty yards of two proud people.*"

"*QUAYE!*"

"*I hope you will excuse the liberty.*"

Behind the green shutters, the pharmacy had gone entirely silent. Behind the yellow gate, forty-one taxis and a goat had done the same.

And then, from the northern end, there came out, quite slowly, into the white noon light, a small figure in a white coat, with a biscuit-tin held in both hands.

---

*The harmattan was early that year. A thin brown dust hung over Palm Lane, so that the sun was only a pale coin and the colours of the world were all faintly eaten. The Rank, to a man, had gone silent upon their bonnets. A woman in a white coat was walking down the middle of the lane, as if she had always done so.*

It was eighty yards, and it took her a minute, and in the course of the minute a number of things happened that nobody afterwards would be able to agree upon. Quaye maintained that he heard a clock. Yaw, the waiter, said that she smiled once, a small and terrible smile, at the goat. Adwoa, from the kiosk, said only that she saw a woman who had been very frightened for six years take a decision, as one takes a pill: briskly, with water, and without looking at what it was.

She stopped at the gate.

It swung open. Kwame Mensah stood there in a clean white shirt and an expression of the most profound dread, as if summoned to be thanked.

"Mr. Mensah," said Abena.

"Miss Owusu."

"I have something for you."

"I have nothing to say that—"

"I have *not* asked you to say anything." She held out the tin. It was a round blue tin, such as is used for Danish butter biscuits in every home from Aflao to Tamale, and it was held out to him with the stiff correctness of a bailiff presenting a summons. "This is yours."

"What is it?"

"It is the debt."

"There is no debt."

"There is a debt of forty-two thousand cedis to the bank, settled in the spring of the year I was twenty-two, by a gentleman, tall, who laughed at his own car." She said it very fast, as one pulls a plaster. "And there is rent, paid in cash every first of the month for seventy-one months, by a clerical error. I have kept the figures. I have kept *every* figure. I have been putting aside for six years, in a tin, in the dispensary safe, behind the morphine. It is not all. I have not got interest. I do not know *what interest to charge on a kindness.* But it is the principal, and it is yours, and I should like you to take it, so that we may be *even.*"

There was a long, high, unbearable silence, with a goat in it.

"No," said Kwame.

"It is not a *request.*"

"No," said Kwame Mensah, and his voice, which was rich and easy and had never in its life been shaken, shook now, once, like a plank over a stream. "I did not want you to feel owed."

"I do not feel *owed,*" said Abena. "I feel *robbed.*"

"Robbed?"

"Of the chance to thank you! Of the whole occasion!" Her hands were trembling around the tin. "I would have done it so *well,* Kwame. I had a speech. I had it for months. It had a *beginning.* And you went and made it unnecessary, in your yellow gate, with your goat, and you said *nothing,* you let me think it was the bank, you let me think the world had been generous, and I walked around for six years being grateful to *nobody.* Do you know what that is? It is like swallowing a prayer with no God at the end."

For a moment he said nothing. Then, very quietly, and rather to his own surprise, in the language he had been taught at his mother's knee, Kwame said something in Twi that the Rank had no business hearing, and which will not be set down here. It was brief. It was a proverb, about water, and the person who carries it to the thirsty without being asked.

She replied, in the same tongue, with another.

It was shorter.

Quaye, who understood both, took off his cap.

"So," said Abena, in English, recovering herself with an effort that the entire lane could hear, "will you take it?"

"No."

"Then I will leave it."

"Where?"

She looked about her with sudden decision, and set the tin, with a small dry click, upon the bonnet of the nearest cab, which was a very old green Corolla known to the Rank as *Auntie Beatrice,* and then folded her arms and said, to nobody, scrupulously: "You may count it."

"I will not count it."

"Then you may *not count it,* but I have left it."

"And I will not open it."

"Then it will sit."

"Then it will *sit,*" said Kwame, "on Auntie Beatrice, in the sun, for ever."

"So be it," said Abena.

They stood a yard apart, in the white dust, with the tin between them like a small round moon. And it seemed to every driver watching that this was a quarrel of such perfect and symmetrical courtesy that it could not be settled by any further word; that it would simply be carried over, like a debt, to the next day and the day after, a perfectly balanced sorrow, in which each held the other's gift and refused it, and each was glad.

"The loudspeaker on that taxi roof," said Abena abruptly, looking away. "It is too loud. It disturbs my patients."

"The queue outside your door," said Kwame, "disturbs my *taxis.*"

"Then it is war."

"It is a great pity."

"It is *thoroughly* a pity," said Abena, and turned, with her back straight and her coat flapping and her whole slim body radiating offence, and walked the eighty yards home.

She did not look back. A pharmacist does not.

But at the green shutters, she paused, with her hand upon the door, and a long, shy, secret curl came into the corner of her mouth, which Adwoa, from the kiosk, would swear to on any Bible you cared to name.

---

*And here the Rank, who had held its breath through the whole of it, let it out together, and since a Chorus must say something, said it in the only measure it knew, which is the rhythm of a taxi on a bad road:*

> *Eighty yards of tar, and neither one would take it;
> A tin upon a bonnet, and neither one would break it.
> She said, I owe thee. He said, Thou owest not.
> The sun went down on two proud hearts, both caught.
> Oh, who will count the cedis? None of us, we think.
> The debt is made of kindness, and kindness has no ink.*

"That is not bad," said Quaye, wiping his eyes. "Who wrote it?"

"You did," said Yaw, "just now."

"Then I am a poet, and I demand a rise."

---

That night, when the Rank had gone home and the dust had settled and the lane was as dark as a good conscience, Kwame Mensah went out to the green Corolla alone, and took the tin from the bonnet, and carried it to his office, and sat before it for a very long time.

He did not count the money.

He had promised.

But he turned the tin over once, as a man turns a stone, and under the lid, in neat violet pharmacist's ink, on a white sticky label of the kind that she used for the dosage on every bottle in her shop, was a line in her small straight hand:

**TAKE AS DIRECTED. MAY CAUSE DROWSINESS, DIZZINESS AND UNEXPECTED FEELINGS. DO NOT OPERATE HEAVY MACHINERY UNTIL YOU HAVE CALLED.**

And, beneath that, smaller, as if she had changed her mind and been too stubborn to cross it out:

*(Eighty yards. I counted.)*

Kwame Mensah, who had never been seen to cry, sat in the harmattan dark with a biscuit-tin on his knee, and laughed like a prisoner released from a long sentence, till the goat in the yard woke and complained.
