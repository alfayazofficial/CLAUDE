## Chapter 34
### Puppets Without Strings

> *"My puppets are braver than I am."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0418

---

**Prague. Seven o'clock on a spring evening, in the lane behind the Church of Our Lady beneath the Chain.**

The cloakroom of the Theatre of the Golden Hare had been guarded for eleven years by a woman who had been, before that, a lifeguard at the Podolí swimming pool, and who regarded the two professions as one with less water in it.

"People come in wet with the world," said Markéta Veselý, forty-five, taking a coat from a trembling pensioner, "and I hold it for them. No tickets lost, no one drowned, and nobody dares say the water was my fault." She hung the coat. She was a vain woman, and her hair was the colour of a good copper kettle. "Two hundred crowns, madam, for the privilege of being looked after."

"It is *twenty*," said the pensioner.

"Twenty for the coat," said Markéta. "Two hundred for the dignity."

The Golden Hare held sixty-three seats, a stage the size of a generous kitchen, and a puppeteer. He was a man of forty named Petr Novák, who had made, in eighteen years, three hundred and twelve puppets and spoken in his own voice, on a stage, in the presence of a living audience, precisely never.

For Petr was a person to whom words came easily, as long as there was something wooden between them and the world. Give him a Kašpárek, the little red-capped fool of every Czech childhood, and he would be funny, wise, cruel, tender, and shrewd for two hours, in a dozen voices. Take the puppet away, and he would say, *Yes. Thank you. That is correct,* and go and stand in the corner like an umbrella.

His brother Vojtěch, a cobbler, who had the wit that comes of spending a life at other people's feet, put it best. "Most human trouble," he liked to say, "is a matter of soles. The rest is a matter of souls. And people confuse the two at their peril. You cannot mend a heart with leather, Petr. But you can sometimes get it to walk."

He had come that evening with a dozen pairs of tiny shoes, to be glued to tiny feet, and was now seated cross-legged in the wings with a pot of glue, wearing the absent-minded air of a man who has forgotten whether he has his own.

"Strawberry-coloured heels for the Queen," said Vojtěch. "Are you sure?"

"I am not sure," said Petr, behind a curtain.

"Good. Certainty is for cobblers who have never met a foot."

Beside him, a girl of fifteen with a stage-hand's black T-shirt and a pencil through her bun was threading a silk line through the head of a dragon. This was Jana, Petr's niece, and she was watching him as the young watch the old when they have decided to make a project of them.

"Uncle," said Jana.

"Yes."

"The seat is taken."

He went still. A glove puppet, the Kašpárek, was on his left hand, and for a moment the hand and the man seemed to share one pulse.

"Which seat?"

"You *know* which seat. F7. Second row from the back, on the aisle. It has been empty for fourteen months. Every night I have put a program on it and every night nobody sat in it. I thought you did it so you could look at a hole in the room." She swallowed. "Tonight there is a woman. A scarf up to here. A hat like a bowl. She gave Markéta two hundred crowns for the coat."

"For the *coat*," said Markéta, from the door, with immense dignity, "two hundred. For the dignity. I told her so." She lowered her voice. "She asked me not to tell you who she is. I said, madam, I have been guarding the wet for eleven years and I have never named a drowner."

"She is not a drowner," said Petr, quietly.

"She is a *critic*," said Markéta. "It is the same thing, but with ink."

There was a pause, in which Vojtěch, with great care, set down his glue.

"Ah," he said.

---

She had been a critic for fourteen years. Her name was Lucie Horáková; she was thirty-six, and she wrote for a Prague daily in sentences that were beautiful and exact and had the odd property of making the people they described feel they had been measured for a coffin. Once, fourteen months before, in a sentence she could not now bear to reread, she had written of a small theatre in a lane behind a church:

*A man who speaks only through wood has, for all his eloquence, nothing to say. The puppets are very well made. Their maker is not in the room.*

Within a month the Golden Hare's subscribers had halved. Within two, the landlord, a gentle old trader named Kučera who drank too much tea at night, had given notice. It had survived. It survived, as small things do, by the stubbornness of three people and a girl with a pencil through her hair. But it had been a near thing, and Lucie knew it, in the secret place in which a critic keeps what she has done.

And then, in the November of the Register, in the first week of the opening, she had sat at her kitchen table with a glass of Moravian wine and the screen of a laptop, and had typed *Prague* into a search bar, as she had typed it, in her time, into the press releases of the damned. And there it was, the third entry of the page: **ENTRY 0418. "My puppets are braver than I am."**

She had known the hand at once. A critic knows a hand. She had put her own beside it, at midnight, for no reason she could name, and sent it into the dark: **ENTRY 0227. "I wrote the review that nearly closed your theatre and I regret it."**

She had not thought it would be read. One does not expect an answer from the dark. She had only wished to be, for one night, a woman who had said something true.

She sat now in F7, in her hat like a bowl, and held her program as one holds a hot plate. The house lights went down. A single lamp came up on the little stage, and on it, with nobody visible, a small red cap rose over the edge of the curtain, followed by a very large nose.

"*Good evening,*" said the Kašpárek.

The audience, twenty children and thirty parents and a few who had come in out of the rain, laughed with the joy of those who have just been recognized.

"*Is there a person from the newspapers in the house?*" said the Kašpárek, leaning out, with his hand to his brow. "*No? I thought not. Newspapers do not come to small theatres. They send a man with a very large opinion and a very small umbrella. Therefore, I shall sit at the front and tell the truth.*"

He sat. He told it. The evening was called *Bez nitě*: *Without Strings*. It was a one-man piece, and it was about a puppet-maker whose puppets, one by one, rebelled. A king refused to be crowned. A dragon declined to breathe fire on a cabbage. A Queen in strawberry-coloured heels announced, to a scandalised court, that she would no longer speak the lines in which she was loved; she preferred to speak the lines in which she was *known*. The children howled. The parents wept, when they thought nobody saw. And somewhere in the second half, as the Kašpárek strolled to the edge of the stage with a lantern in his tiny wooden fist and let the light fall across the rows, he stopped, as though he had seen a thing in the dark.

"*Ah,*" he said.

The room was still.

"*There is a person in F7,*" said the Kašpárek, gravely. "*The seat has been empty for fourteen months. It has become a kind of monument. I thought it should be left to the pigeons. And tonight, look: a hat. A hat like a bowl. Madam, forgive me. I have been warned to say nothing. But your seat is warm.*"

Sixty heads went round in the dark, like a field of sunflowers. In F7, Lucie Horáková did not move. A critic, in such an hour, has only one resource, which is to appear to be taking notes.

"*I have decided,*" said the Kašpárek, "*to forgive the newspaper, though not the headline. But I will say this, to the hat.*" And he took off his own little red cap, and held it over his heart, and the voice that came from the stage was, for the first time in the history of the Golden Hare, a voice she knew was not the puppet's. It was lower, and a little rough, and it cracked a little, like wood in the first warm day of spring:

> *I am not braver than the wood I carve.
> I put a word inside it and it lives.
> And you, who read me through, and called me hollow,
> Were right, for I was hiding in the hollow.
> I am come out. It is not much to see.
> A man; two hands; a nose that is his own.
> If you should wish to write of it again,
> Write that he came out slowly. That he came.*

And from behind the curtain, in a spill of lamplight, with the puppet dangling forgotten at his side and nothing between himself and sixty strangers but his own forty years, stepped a tall, thin, shy man in a black jumper, who said, to the room, in a voice which shook like a ladder:

"*Děkuji, že jste přišli.*" Thank you for coming.

It was four words in Czech, and the first speech of his life. A woman in the third row began to clap, and then a child, and then, like rain on a roof, the whole house.

In F7, a critic put her pencil on her knee and did not write one word.

She was gone before the second curtain-call, hat and scarf and all. Markéta returned the coat at the cloakroom as one returns a rescued infant. There was an envelope on the counter, with a coin in it that was far too large.

"For the dignity," said Markéta.

The note was folded in four. It had been written in the sort of handwriting that has spent years correcting other people's. It said, in Czech, in a hand that shook:

*I was wrong. Not about the wood. About the man. — L.H.*

Petr read it in the wings, with Jana looking over one elbow and his brother over the other. He read it twice. Then, because he was a man who found it easier to say a thing through a hand, he put the Kašpárek back on his fist, and the Kašpárek read it aloud, in the voice of an immensely solemn judge.

"*It is the first time,*" said the Kašpárek, "*in the history of Czech criticism.*"

Jana began to laugh, and could not stop, and had to sit on the dragon.

"*Also,*" said the Kašpárek, who had a good deal to say for a bit of wood, "*it is a very short note. The lady is economical. I like this in a woman.*"

"Petr," said Vojtěch, very gently.

"Yes."

"The hand is yours. The voice is yours. Do not leave it on the puppet." He tapped, with a cobbler's thick finger, the little wooden chest. "A sole will last you a year. A soul, I am told, takes a little longer to wear in."

**Crete. The same week, in the hills above Chania, before the sun was well awake.**

Kostas Mavros, forty-four, taxi driver, had one eye on the road and the other on his passenger, an arrangement that has made orphans of whole families on Cretan mountain roads, and which he practised with serene indifference.

"Women," said Kostas, as the old Mercedes took a bend at the speed of an argument, "are like the White Mountains. You do not conquer them. You wait for the snow to melt."

"I am sixty," said George Papadakis, who was holding the door handle.

"The snow has been *melting* for thirty years!"

"Mind the goat."

"What goat?"

There was a goat. There had been a goat for some time, and it was now a little behind them, in the mirror, wearing the look of a creature that has just seen the hereafter.

George had been made by sun and olives into one piece, with a face like a good boot and a heavy hand taught to be gentle by thirty years of branches. He sat very straight in his one good suit, with a wooden crate across his knees. In the crate were two things. The first was a tin of oil from the first pressing of the year, the green, peppery *agiourelaio* that has been known to make grown men cough and ask for their mothers. The second, tied with kitchen string, was a bundle of thirty letters, which had come back to him, one winter morning, in the hands of a niece who would not meet his eyes: every one that he had written to Eleni Stavrou, in the thirty springs since she left, returned unopened.

He had not reread them. He had not needed to. He knew them as he knew his own terraces.

"You will go in," said Kostas, "and you will say, *Eleni, I am here.* And she will say, *George, what is this, a tragedy?* And you will say, *No, a delivery.* Ha! Because you have the oil! You see? I have the whole thing arranged. All you have to do is open your mouth."

"Kostas."

"Yes."

"Stop the car at the corner, please. I will walk."

They stopped. Kostas put his hand over his heart, a thing he did to prove that, in his case, one existed. "My friend," he said, with unwonted quiet. "Thirty years is a long time to be right and not be asked."

"I have not come to be right," said George, getting out slowly, like a man in a tall boat. "I have come to be seen. It is a smaller thing, and it is quite enough."

---

The shop was in a lane behind the Venetian harbour, where the walls are the colour of apricots and the cats have the manners of landlords. Over the door was painted *STAVROU — HERBS, HONEY, & EVERYTHING ELSE*, and the *everything else* was true: it held diktamo from the gorge and thyme honey from the mountain and nine kinds of olives, a poster of the Virgin, and one hand-lettered sign that said *CREDIT IS A SIN*, and beneath it, in a smaller hand, *ASK ELENI.*

On a stool in the doorway, with her arms folded and the expression of a magistrate, sat a girl of twelve in a school uniform, Katerina Nikolaidis, who was minding the till for the afternoon and had decided that this was the afternoon.

"Are you the olive man?" said Katerina.

"I am an olive man."

"Mrs. Eleni cries in the storeroom every April, when the almond flowers. I have heard it. She says it is the dust." The girl surveyed him, from boots to hat, with merciless attention. "Is it true you wrote thirty letters?"

"It is true."

"*Thirty?*"

"One a spring."

"What did you say in them?"

"I do not know," said George, with perfect truth. "I never opened them again."

"Katerina," said a voice from within. It was a dry voice, stern, a voice that ran a shop and a family and a good deal of the street. "Go and see if the Kalogeropoulos woman has paid for her beans."

"But Mrs. Eleni, there is a—"

"*Go.*"

Katerina went, looking back over her shoulder like a comet.

Eleni Stavrou stood in the doorway of her own storeroom, fifty-eight, in a black cardigan, a widow of six years, with a carved wooden spoon in one hand like a sceptre. Her hair had gone silver before she had permitted it, and she was not a handsome woman, but a *finished* one, as a good wall is finished, and the two of them stood and looked at each other across a floor of cold stone and thirty springs.

"You have got fat," said Eleni.

"I have got *old*."

"It is the same thing at our age." She did not smile. The wooden spoon moved, once, like a metronome. "What is in the box?"

"Oil," said George. "And what is yours."

He put it on her counter, the crate and the tin and the string, and stepped back, as one steps back from an altar.

"I am not asking you to open them," he said. "I am not asking you for anything. I am only bringing you what is yours. They were never mine, after I had posted them. A letter belongs to the person it is for. I should have understood that thirty years ago."

For a long moment she did not move. Then she said, in a voice whose steadiness cost her a good deal, "You will sit. I will make coffee."

She made it. It was a long, strict, ceremonial business, a copper *briki* on a low gas, a spoon of sugar, the froth watched like a child. She did not look at him once. When it was done she set before him a small cup, a glass of water, and a saucer on which sat a single spoon of quince sweetness, and she sat down across from him, and for twenty minutes they spoke, with every appearance of calm, of the weather, and the olive fly, and the price of fuel, and the mule he had once owned that was a most excellent judge of character.

Not a word of the letters. Not a word of the thirty years.

At the end of it, when the cup was empty and the light on the harbour had turned from white to gold, George rose and put on his hat. And then, because he was a Cretan, and because in that island the heart has always had a form, he said, in the old, easy, singing way that the old men use in the *kafeneio*:

> *Thirty springs I sent my blossoms down the road to you;
> The post said "not at this address," but still the blossom grew.*

It was a *mantinada*, one of those rhyming couplets the Cretans fling at one another across a table like olives: a verse that asks no question, only leaves a space where an answer might sit.

Eleni did not look up. The wooden spoon lay on the table. A long breath came out of her, and then, quite quietly, in a dry, half-broken voice which belonged entirely to the girl of twenty-eight who had left, she gave the answer. It was the old rule of the form, that you must not leave a couplet standing alone:

> *The tree that learns to bear must learn to let its fruit fall down.
> Go home, old fool. I'll send for you. Don't bring me any crown.*

George stood quite still. His hat was in his hand. Something went across his face that was too large for it, and was gone.

"*Efharisto,*" he said.

"Go *home*," said Eleni Stavrou.

He went. Katerina, coming back from the Kalogeropoulos house with an air of devastating success, found him in the lane, looking at nothing with great attention, and watched him walk away down toward the harbour with his hat in his hand, like a man leaving church.

---

She did not open it that afternoon. She did not open it that night. She served eleven customers, scolded a fishmonger, and told Katerina at six that she might go home. At eight, with the shutters down and one lamp burning above the till, she sat on a sack of flour and took the bundle of letters out of the crate and held it in both hands, as a woman holds a creature that might bite.

She said, aloud, to the Virgin on the poster, in the plain tones of one who has been on speaking terms with her for fifty years:

> *You know that I am not a coward. I buried
> A husband and I did not flinch. I've sold
> Dried herbs to princes and refused their credit.
> I'll tell you what I feared. Not blame: I know
> That tune. Not hate. A hate may be outlived.
> I feared a letter that said nothing cruel,
> And nothing that required of me an answer,
> And only said: I'm here. I'm here. The door
> Is on its hinges. That is what I feared.
> That he would be, for thirty springs, quite kind.*

She cut the string with her kitchen scissors. The first envelope was soft with age. It was dated the April of the year she left, in a hand she had known as well as her own, and when she unfolded it there was only a half page, written, one could see, with great care and no hope:

*Eleni. The almond is in flower. It is not the same without you to say it is too early. I have nothing to ask. The house is warm. Come, or do not come. The door is on its hinges, either way. — George.*

She read it. She read it again. She laid her head down on the flour sack, among the herbs and the honey, and there, in the one lamp's light, in a shop that said *CREDIT IS A SIN*, the sternest woman in Chania began, at last, to laugh, and the laughing turned, and turned back, and she let it.

It was kind. It was exactly as kind as she had feared.
