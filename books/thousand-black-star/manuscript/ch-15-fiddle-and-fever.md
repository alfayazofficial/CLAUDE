## Chapter 15
### Fiddle and Fever

> *"There is a song I cannot finish, and it is about you."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0025

---

### SCENE I
*Galway. A flat above a closed bakery, on a Thursday in December, with the Atlantic doing what it does to windows. A suitcase, open on a bed. A woman who has been packing for nine days and has not yet put in a single thing she means to take.*

Aoife Brennan, thirty, veterinary surgeon, was a woman of great directness, and she had never been less direct in her life.

"Socks," she said to the room. "Two weeks of socks. Why do I own so many socks, when I have only two feet?"

The room, which was small, damp, and loyal, made no reply. On the dresser stood a framed certificate, a jar of coins from the pockets of farmers who paid in kind, and a hoof-pick shaped like a small silver oar. On the bed lay a letter of acceptance from a large-animal practice outside Antigonish, in Nova Scotia, a county that, as she had discovered on the internet at four in the morning, held a Highland games, kept a great many horses, and was short of a vet who would shout. On the pillow lay a ticket: *Shannon to Toronto. Toronto to Halifax. Sunday, 6:40 a.m.*

She had told no one. Not the clinic, where her resignation lay in her bag, signed and sealed and heavy as a stone. Not her mother, who would say *Canada!* in the voice used for *cancer.* Not Niamh, her oldest friend, who sold fish on the Long Walk and could not say goodbye to a bus.

And not, God help her, the fiddler.

She sat down on the bed, among the socks, and, since there was nobody in the room to be direct to, she was direct to the dark:

> *I have been brave with bulls. I have put my arm
> Into the throat of weather, to the shoulder,
> And turned a calf that did not wish to come.
> I have said "Hold him" to a hundred men
> Who held, because I said so. I am not
> A woman who has ever been afraid
> Of anything with hooves, or fangs, or horns.
> And yet there is a man on Dominick Street
> With a bad fiddle and a worse coat, who has
> Not asked me anything in six years' time
> Except to stay for the last tune, and I
> Have never stayed. I leave before the reel.
> I tell myself it is the early rounds.
> It is not early rounds. It is that if I stayed
> To hear the last tune, I would hear its end;
> And if I heard its end, I would not go.*

She laughed, once, at herself. Then she laid her hands flat on her knees, like a woman who has delivered a verdict, and took out of the suitcase a pair of socks and replaced them in the drawer, and took out of the drawer a different pair and put them in the suitcase, which is the sort of thing a person does when she is leaving and wishes not to be.

On the pillow, beside the ticket, lay her phone, and on the phone, where she had left it open for three days, lay a page of the Register of Black Stars.

*ENTRY 0025 — Galway, Ireland.*
*"There is a song I cannot finish, and it is about you."*

She knew the hand. She did not need the name. Nobody else in the west of Ireland would have written a thing in which the second clause was a promise and the first was a lie.

The doorbell rang like a fire alarm.

"*AOIFE!*" cried a voice from the street. "I have a *hake!*"

---

Niamh Quinn, forty-one, fishmonger, stood on the step in a coat the colour of a wet gull with a hake under one arm and a bottle of something under the other. She had the face of a woman who spends her life in the cold being gentle to dead things, which is to say, a face that had never in its whole career managed to hide a feeling.

"It's Thursday," she said. "I know what Thursday is. Thursday is session, and you've a face on you like the last cow at the mart, and I thought: I'll bring her a fish."

"I do not need a fish."

"Nobody needs a fish. That's the *beauty* of the fish." She pushed past into the hall with the confidence of the long-acquainted. "I'll put him in the sink. Don't look at his eyes, they follow you. What's that you're hiding?"

"I'm not hiding anything."

"You're standing in front of the bedroom door with your arms out, like an unconvincing Christ."

"It's a... it's a *draught excluder.*"

"Aoife."

"It's *laundry,* Niamh."

And at that moment, with the timing of a thing that has been waiting nine days, the suitcase on the bed, which had been shut with a hasty hand and a prayer, burst its clasps and sent forth upon the carpet a shower of socks, a hoof-pick, a rolled-up map of Nova Scotia, and a small framed photograph of the sea at Blackrock, which Aoife had not known she had packed.

There was a silence in which a hake, in the sink, seemed to take an interest.

Niamh looked at the map. She looked at the socks. She looked at her oldest friend, who stood in the doorway holding the doorframe with both hands as though the building had begun to move.

"Ah, *Aoife,*" said Niamh.

"It's not what it looks like."

"It's a map of *Canada.*"

"It's a map of Nova Scotia, which is *part* of—" Aoife stopped. She had not cried in front of another person since the funeral of a Labrador in 2009. She did not cry now. She merely sat down rather quickly upon the edge of the bed. "I've not told anyone. I was going to tell you. I was going to tell you on Saturday."

"Saturday?"

"Sunday's the flight."

"*Sunday.*" Niamh sat beside her, still holding the bottle. It did not seem to be a bottle in which either of them had any further interest. After a time she said, in the small flat voice of a person who has never learned to say what the next sentence is for: "I'm no good at this."

"I know."

"I'll cry in the airport."

"You'll not come to the airport."

"I'll cry in the *car park.*" She wiped her face with the sleeve of the wet-gull coat, which smelled of ice and mackerel. "Does he know?"

"Who?"

"Don't be thick."

"He doesn't."

"He's going to finish it tonight, you know. The song. He told me on Monday over the haddock. He said, 'Niamh, I've a fever, and I've a last verse, and I think God has decided the two should be sung together.' And I said to him, 'Liam, that is a *flu.*'"

"It's a flu?"

"He's thirty-nine degrees. He took it himself with a thermometer from the chemist, and then he asked the chemist if it was *romantic.*" She turned her wet and honest face on her friend. "Come tonight. You never stay for the last tune. Come, and stay."

"I'll come," said Aoife slowly. "I'll stay for the last one. And then—"

"And then?"

"And then I'll see," said Aoife Brennan, who had never in her life said such a thing, and meant it, as she had meant everything: with her whole hand.

### SCENE II
*Mulroy's of Dominick Street. A long low room, a turf fire, a ceiling of black beams, and a Thursday-night session already three tunes deep. Rain on the windows. Steam on the rain.*

Mulroy's pub held, on a good night, sixty souls; on a Thursday in December, with the Christmas market packed into Eyre Square and half of the west of Ireland looking for a roof, it held eighty-four and a dog.

By the fire, on a stool that had been shaped over decades to the exact curve of his grievance, sat Fergal Walsh, forty-five, who sold roast chestnuts from a cart on Shop Street by day and by night, for the love of God and a free pint, told stories, which he called *the real news*. He had coveted the Thursday slot for eleven years. He had fourteen stories, each with at least six endings, and a nose that had been broken in one of them.

"Ladies and gentlemen," said Fergal, rising, and the room, which had learned over many winters that it was easier to let him than to argue, turned its eighty-four faces toward the fire. "A story. An old one. I had it from my grandmother, who had it from a man in Inis Meáin, who had it from the sea, which is *notoriously* unreliable."

"Is it the one with the donkey?" someone called.

"It is not the one with the donkey. The donkey is *separate.* This is the story of the two fiddlers."

"Here we go," said a voice.

"In the old time," said Fergal, "there were two fiddlers on the Aran Islands who played a tune between them that had no end. The first would play a phrase and the second would answer, and the first would answer the answer, and on they went, for forty years, through weddings and wakes and one very bad christening. And everyone said: *Isn't it marvellous, the tune that has no end.* And it was. Until the day a stranger came off the boat, a man with a clever face and a leather case, and he said: *That's a grand tune. But where does it finish?*"

He paused, with an expert's cruelty, and sipped his pint.

"And the first fiddler, who was a decent man, said: *It doesn't. That's the point of it.* And the stranger said: *Ah, but the best tunes have a last note, and until you play it, you've never really said anything.* And the second fiddler—who was a woman, and sharper than the pair of them—looked at the first and said: *He's right.* And the first said: *But if we play the last note, we'll have to stop.* And she said: *Yes.* And do you know what he said?"

"What?" said eighty-four voices.

"He said," said Fergal, "*I'll tell you tomorrow.* And he never did, because the next day she took the boat. And that—" he spread his hands "—is why, to this very hour, there's a tune on Inis Meáin that has no ending, and a boat that never comes back, and a man who has nothing but his own two hands, and that, ladies and gentlemen, is why you should never, *ever,* buy a chestnut from a man who's had a drink."

There was a roar. It is the peculiarity of a fool, reader, that he says the wise thing by accident and the foolish thing on purpose, and so is never forgiven for the first.

But in the corner, near the door, where she had come in out of the rain with her coat on and her hair dripping and a hake's worth of resolve in her chest, Aoife Brennan did not laugh. She had been listening for a long time, and she stood against the wall with the very particular stillness of a woman who has heard a story that was not about her, and found her own name in it.

---

On the low stage, which was a platform of three planks and a tablecloth, a thin man with a worse coat than any she had imagined was tuning a fiddle that he had played, by his own account, since the age of eight and fixed, by his own account, with nine different sorts of glue.

Liam O'Connor, thirty-two, pub musician, was ill.

It was visible to anyone with eyes. His face had the high, bright, dreadful colour of a man lit from within by the wrong lamp. He wore two jumpers. His hands, which on a sound day could make a reel sound as though it had been born in a thunderstorm, trembled slightly on the neck of the instrument, and from time to time, between phrases, he coughed into his elbow with a fine sense of drama.

"He's *dying,*" said a woman at the bar.

"He's *thirty-nine degrees,*" said Niamh Quinn, from the corner, with the authority of a woman who has taken his temperature through a haddock. "It's different."

Beside him sat Maeve Ryan, seventy-two, retired guard on the Galway–Athenry line, with a bodhrán across her knee and a ticket-punch on a string around her neck, which she clicked in time with the reels, because for forty years she had never known a rhythm that was not a timetable. It was Maeve who had taught him his first tunes, in a front room in Spiddal, smacking his knuckles with a ruler for sloppiness. She was a woman of few words and many coughs, and she watched him now with an expression that people took for sternness, and was in fact the face of a person counting something she did not wish to lose.

Next to her, on a low stool, Sinéad Murphy, twenty-four, electrician, who had rewired the pub's lighting in October and could not be persuaded to stop looking at it, held a tin whistle in one hand and, in the other, a small screwdriver for emergencies.

"Right," said Liam, into a microphone he did not need. His voice cracked on the *R* and he swallowed. "That's a set of reels. Thanks, now. Thanks to Maeve, who's been punching my ticket since I was six. And to Sinéad, who has rewired the room, so if the lights go, that's *her.*"

"It'll not be me!" said Sinéad.

"And I've one more tonight. A new one. Well, not *new.* It's been the same one for six years, and I've never finished it. I'll tell you now, folks, I've been *saying* it was nearly finished since the Famine." A little laughter. "But I've a fever, and a last verse, and I thought, *God sent me the one, so he should have the other.* So if I fall off the stage, somebody throw a coat on me."

He took a breath. The fiddle came up under his chin.

He did not look at the door. He had known she was there since the first note of the first reel: a man who has played to a room for ten years knows who has come in, as a sailor knows weather. He played the first notes, and the room, which had been loud, went still.

> *The tide goes out at Blackrock Pier,
> It leaves the weed, it leaves the stone,
> It leaves me standing, year on year,
> With a tune I cannot call my own.
>
> You set the leg of my old collie
> And told me he was fed too well,
> And I went home with a sort of folly
> That no vet's bill could ever tell.
>
> You'd stand at the back with your coat still on,
> And leave before the last reel's done,
> And I'd play on till the lamps were gone
> For the one who never stays for one.*

He was not a great singer. His voice was low and rough and slightly flat on the long notes, and in the third line of the third verse it nearly gave out, so that for half a bar there was only the fiddle, speaking on its own, and in the corner by the door a woman in a wet coat put her hand flat against the wall, as if the building had moved.

He opened his eyes. He looked straight down the long smoky room, over eighty-four heads, over the turf-smoke and the faces and a dog, and found her.

"And this," said Liam O'Connor, very quietly, so that the room had to lean in, "is the last verse. It's the one I've never had. Hold on to your hats."

He bowed the fiddle. He sang:

> *So here is the end, and I'll sing it plain,
> For I've the fever and I've the nerve:
> If you are going, Aoife, go on the morning tide,
> I'll not be the one to say you've to stay,
> But before you go, I'll say what I should have said
> At the back of the bar, on the first wet night,
> When you took off your gloves to look at my dog:
> I have loved you since the winter that you*

*Here the page is torn.*

### SCENE III
*Lantern House, Cornwall. A little before midnight, in the same hour. A gale. A laptop. A postman's bag, empty at last.*

Nadeesha Fernando had answered four thousand, one hundred and twelve messages in fourteen days, and she had come to understand that grief and clerical work have a great deal in common: both are done by lamp, in the dark, until they are done.

"*Is Entry 0388 me?*" said the first. "*I think Entry 0388 is my husband. Is it possible to find him?*" said the second. "*Please remove Entry 0219. It is true, and I would like it not to be.*" The Register, she had learned, could be opened by anyone but could not be closed by her; and she sat at the oak table in the library of Lantern House, in a blanket, with a pot of tea gone cold at her elbow and the wind trying every door in the house in turn, and typed, to each of them, the same four lines that Seraphine had once said she should say: *Thank you for writing. I cannot tell you whose words are whose. I can tell you that they were true. Be kind, and be brave in that order.*

The laptop chimed.

A small square opened. A man, in a white shirt with the sleeves rolled, in a room full of grey dawn and brass fittings and a great wheel of glass, looked out of the screen and smiled at her with the diffident honesty of one who has not yet decided whether he is allowed.

"You are awake," said Anil Perera.

"It is midnight."

"It is five in the morning. The lamp is going off. I have been keeping the Galle light since four, and I thought: I will call. I will not say anything important. I will say good morning, and ask whether the Register is well."

Nadeesha looked at him for a while. She had been looking at strangers' confessions for fourteen days, and she found that the plainest face in the world was rather a rest.

"It is a thing like a tide," she said. "It does not stop. I thought the thousand would be quiet when they were read. They are not. They are *loud.*"

"And you? Have you read your own?"

"I have not written one."

"You did. You wrote, *I opened the Register and now I cannot close it.* It is Entry 0223. I read it before I read mine." He said this all at once, and then looked appalled. "I am sorry. That was private."

"It was *published,*" said Nadeesha, "by me."

"Even so."

The wind knocked once, politely, at the shutter. She laughed, which she had not done in fourteen days, and the sound surprised both of them.

"Tell me about the weather," she said. "At the lamp. I should like to hear someone say a fact."

"Calm," said Anil. "Southerly, five knots. A tanker off the Fort. A woman in the market selling king coconut who has argued with me every dawn for twenty years about a mango, and I have not once conceded." He leaned his chin on one hand, in the pale light, and his eyes, steady and dark as a harbour, held hers across six thousand miles of sleeping water. "I have been reading a Galway entry, Nadeesha. A man who cannot finish a song. I have been thinking that he will, tonight. It is a thing I know, as I know the tides, without being able to say how."

"You have never been wrong about the weather."

"No."

"And about people?"

He thought about it, honestly, for the whole of a long moment, with the great glass wheel turning behind him, and the first light of a tropical morning gathering on the water.

"I have never been asked," said Anil, "by anyone who wanted to know."

Nadeesha turned the laptop's small lamp down, and the room filled with the sea's dark noise, and a gale, and a thousand voices waiting to be answered; and, in the centre of it, with her tea cold and her blanket slipping, a nurse from Galle sat in the house of a woman she had loved and found that there was, in the middle of the long night, a light that had come on at the far end of the world, and that she had not put it there, and that it did not mind.
