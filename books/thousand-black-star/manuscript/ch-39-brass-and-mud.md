## Chapter 39
### Brass and Mud

> *"The band is folding and you are the only thing I would keep."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0542

---

*Enter the Chorus, in a raincoat, which in Manchester is the only costume that never goes out of fashion.*

> Two things this city lost and could not mend:
> A ground whose grass has run its final mile,
> A band whose brass must lay its music down.
> Yet one small note, held faithful to the end,
> May outlast stand and bandroom, turf and town.

---

### SCENE I
*Marsden Road Ground, Manchester. Dawn, the last Saturday of the season. Drizzle, as ordered.*

There are two kinds of grass in Manchester: the kind that is grown, and the kind that is *kept*. The first needs only rain, of which the city has a surplus. The second needs a man.

William Hartley was that man. He was thirty, he had been at Marsden Road since the age of sixteen, when he had been handed a roller as big as a bus and told to make the world flat, and he knew every blade of the pitch the way a shepherd knows a flock: by temperament. The left wing sulked in October. The penalty spot drank water like a Methodist at a wedding. The centre circle, which he had painted ten thousand times with a chalk-wheel and a steady hand, had a slight tilt to the east, as though even the middle of things were leaning toward something else.

This morning he mowed it for the last time.

He did it slowly, as a man eats the last of anything. Up the length of the pitch, a stripe dark where the grass bent away; back again, a stripe pale where it bent toward him. Behind him the stands rose, grey and empty and a little rusted, like the ribs of some patient animal that had been told it was to be put down and had decided to be dignified about it. On the east gate a banner, already tired, said: **MARSDEN GARDENS: 214 LUXURY APARTMENTS. COMING SOON.**

"Coming soon," William told the roller. "They always say *soon*. Nobody says *sorry*."

"I say it!" cried a voice from the tunnel. "Sorry! Sorry! Terribly sorry! I have been trying to say it for a week and it keeps coming out as a whistle."

It was true. It generally did.

Out of the tunnel, with a bunch of keys the size of a small bagpipe and a flat cap that had seen three decades of Saturdays, came Mr. George Lowe, forty-five, caretaker of the Marsden Town Silver Band's rehearsal room beneath the east stand, known to every living soul in the district as **Mr. Pennywhistle**, because he could not walk, think, grieve or be afraid without whistling, and because no one had ever heard him whistle a recognisable tune. He had been writing the Great Salford Novel for nineteen years. It was, by his own account, "nearly there"; its first chapter had been nearly there since 2006.

"Pennywhistle."

"Will! A word. Two words. Three, if you will pay for them." He leaned on the rail, panting, all anxious elbows. "I have news. Poppy Ashworth has been seen in the bandroom at six in the morning, *alone*, playing the one piece she says she has not played since Easter. A woman who plays by herself at dawn, William, is a woman with a thing on her mind."

"She practises."

"She practises *you*."

"Pennywhistle."

"A groundsman and a cornetist," said Mr. Pennywhistle, with the air of a man unveiling a statue. "One keeps the pitch; the other hits it. If that is not Providence, I do not know what is, and I have read the Bible twice and a great deal of the Yellow Pages."

"It is not Providence. It is a pun."

"Providence *is* a pun, William. It is the pun God makes when He is too tired to explain." He wagged a finger. "You have been mowing round that centre circle as though you were afraid of it. Mark me. If a man will not look at his own middle, he will not look at anything."

William did look at it. The circle sat in the green, pale and perfect, a whole round O, and for a moment he thought that it looked like nothing so much as a note.

He put the thought away. A groundsman does not entertain thoughts that cannot be rolled.

### SCENE II
*The same night. The pitch, empty. A single floodlight, the last still working, throws his shadow a hundred yards.*

He had told no one about the letters.

There were two. The first, from the club, offered him a place at the *new* ground, four miles east and shining like a new tooth, where the grass was plastic, and the man who looked after it was called a *surface technician*, and the work consisted of sweeping rubber crumbs from one end of a carpet to the other. The second came from an academy in Southampton, three hundred miles away, where they grew real turf under real sky and wanted a head groundsman who knew the difference.

He had folded both in his breast pocket. He had carried them to the pitch. And now, in the one remaining light, with the rain thinned to a mist and the stands dark as a sleeping choir, William Hartley did what the lonely do when they are certain that nobody is listening: he spoke it out.

> *Good ground, old ground, that held me up so long,
> I never learned to stand on anything else,
> Forgive the man who leaves you to the diggers.
> I fed you, and you fed me back in silence:
> Each autumn's mud, each summer's patient green,
> The hush before the whistle when ten thousand
> Held in a single breath. I knew you, ground.
> I knew you by your moods, and by your drains.
> And yet it is not you I cannot leave.
> It is a girl who plays a cornet badly
> At six a.m., on purpose, so I'll hear.
> She thinks I do not know. I know. I know.
> And she is going, too — to Leeds, they say;
> A school, a salary, an end to brass.
> So both of us are leaving, and the grass
> Will keep the footprints longer than the feet.*

He stopped. The floodlight buzzed. Somewhere beyond the wall a late bus coughed up Marsden Road and went on without anyone.

"That was very good," said a small voice.

William nearly left his own skin on the turf.

At the touchline stood a girl of thirteen in a school coat three sizes too wide, holding a torch like a sword. It was Hannah Hughes, the neighbour's daughter, who had climbed the back fence nightly for two years to count the foxes and who had never once, to anyone's knowledge, been where she was supposed to be.

"How long have you been there?"

"Since *good ground, old ground.*" She considered. "It doesn't rhyme."

"It is not meant to rhyme."

"Then it's a speech. You should say it to her. Poppy. Not to the grass. The grass can't say anything back."

"Hannah, go home."

"I am *going*." She turned. Then she said, with the withering patience of the very young, "Grown-ups always say things to the wrong thing. My nan says things to the cat." And she was over the fence and gone, and William was left alone with the floodlight and the unpleasant sensation of having been correctly judged by somebody who still had a primary-school timetable.

### SCENE III
*Kisumu, on Lake Victoria. The same hour of the clock, which in Kenya is three hours further into the day. The beach at Dunga, before sunrise. The lake the colour of weak tea; the sky preparing something larger.*

*Mark this, reader, for we must cross a continent between two paragraphs. Pack lightly.*

A woman in a green anorak stood among the moored boats, with a notebook she had decided not to open.

Her name was Wanjiru Kamau. She was twenty-seven, she was a technology journalist of the exact and unflattering kind, and four months ago she had written an article that had begun, *The man who calls himself Captain Kali has never caught a Nile perch heavier than his own ego.* It had been true in every particular. It had also cost a fisherman from Kisumu four hundred thousand followers and, as far as she could judge, a good deal of sleep. It had cost her the same sleep, on the other side of the ledger, and she had not yet worked out the exchange rate.

She had taken the first flight from Nairobi. She had not told him. She had come to correct the record, which is what she told herself, and which was what the notebook was for.

"You are standing in my light," said a voice from a boat.

Otieno Odhiambo, twenty-nine, was sitting in the stern of a wooden boat called *Mama Akinyi* (after his grandmother, who had fished this gulf for sixty years and had never once worn a cape). He was untangling a net. He did not wear the cape either. He wore a torn grey sweater and the expression of a man who had been very loud for a long time and had recently discovered how pleasant it is to be quiet.

"Wanjiru."

"Otieno."

"You came to fact-check my net?"

"I came," said Wanjiru, "to say that I wrote a true thing and I am not sorry I wrote it, and that I have been sorry every day since. Both are the case. I could not find an article that fits both. So I came to say it aloud."

He looked at her for a long moment, and the sun, which had been waiting politely behind Kit Mikayi, put one pink finger on the lake.

"Get in," said Otieno. "The omena do not run for apologies."

They went out. He rowed first, and then ran the little motor, and the gulf opened round them, wide as a held breath, papyrus along the margin and water hyacinth drifting in green islands, with egrets standing on them like waiters who had not been told the party was over. He told her he had posted nothing in four months. He told her the truth about the persona, which was that Captain Kali had begun as a joke to sell dried fish, and then the joke had begun to sell *him*, and then there was a day he could not find where the Captain stopped and he began.

"The hippo story?" said Wanjiru.

"Never met a hippo. They frighten me."

"And the sixty-kilo perch?"

"I bought it from Mama Njeri in the market. She gave me a discount on the *story.*"

"Otieno."

"I know."

"Why?"

"Because a fisherman who catches tilapia is a fisherman," he said, "and a fisherman who wrestles a perch is *content*, and nobody would pay for the truth, which is that I am good at a quiet job and was afraid of that." He cut the engine. The silence that came was enormous and full of water. "You wrote it was a lie. It was. But you also wrote, in the last paragraph, *the man has real talent, and I would rather watch him use it honestly.* I have read that line four hundred times. I am not sure you meant to put it in."

Wanjiru, who had never in her career been caught by her own sentences, looked at the water.

"I did not mean to," she said. "It was the only true thing I could not edit out."

Then, thirty yards off the starboard bow, the lake bulged. A grey back the size of a wardrobe rose, blew a plume of spray into the dawn, and opened a mouth that could have comfortably accommodated a family saloon.

Otieno screamed.

It was not the scream of Captain Kali. It was a long, high, honest scream, the scream of a boy, and it went on until he ran out of lungs, whereupon he clapped both hands over his mouth, and Wanjiru, who had begun to laugh somewhere around the second second, was laughing so hard that the boat rocked.

"Never met a hippo," she wheezed.

"I *have* now!"

"You are not filming it."

"I forgot the camera!" said Otieno, wide-eyed. "I forgot to *perform!* Did you see? I was only frightened!"

"I saw." She wiped her eyes. "That. That man. He was very good."

"He has fewer followers."

Wanjiru Kamau looked at him in the first true light, a fisherman in a torn sweater, afraid of hippos, with the whole bright lake around him like a verdict.

"He has one," she said.

The hippo sank. The omena ran. Neither of them noticed.

### SCENE IV
*Marsden Road. The following Saturday. Last match, last whistle, last light. The ground full for the first time in a decade, and every seat damp.*

They lost two–one in the ninety-third minute, which the crowd agreed was the only fitting way to end, as the club had never in its history been on time for anything.

Then, as the referee's whistle died, the old tunnel doors swung open and out marched the Marsden Town Silver Band, twenty-six strong, in their maroon jackets gone to the colour of old wine, with Poppy Ashworth at the front.

She was twenty-nine, she was loud in every sense a woman can be, and her cornet was dented from a fall off a coach in 2019 and played all the better for it. She marched with her chin up and her eyes straight ahead, which is how you march when you are afraid to look at the thing you most want to see.

Behind her came Callum Brown, the euphonium, forty and shy as a clam, who was William's best friend and who had once spent an entire wedding trying to ask a bridesmaid to dance and had asked the cake instead. Behind him came the basses, the trombones, the old man on the bass drum who had played at the Cup final in a blizzard and had never let anyone forget it.

They marched into the middle of the mud.

For it was mud now, a century of drainage in the rain, black and sticking, up to the ankle, and the bandsmen's polished shoes went into it with a noise like a sigh. They did not stop. Brass bands, as Mr. Pennywhistle would tell you, do not stop for weather; they stop for *death*, and then only after the last verse.

"Have you looked at the pitch, Poppy?" hissed Callum.

"I am not looking at the pitch."

"You should look at the pitch."

"*I am not looking at the pitch, Callum.*"

But in the press box, high on the roof of the west stand, Mr. Pennywhistle, who had climbed up there with a thermos and a notebook and had been writing nothing at all for forty minutes, had seen it. He was the only man in the ground who could. And when he realised what it was he stood up so fast that the thermos went over the edge, bounced three times, and landed in the executive car park to a sound that has been described in the literature as *a terrible gong*.

"Look!" he shrieked, to no one. "*Look at the grass!*"

For this is what William Hartley had done on the last morning of his craft. He had not mown it in stripes. He had mown it in *lines*: five long bands, straight as rails, down the whole length of the ground, dark and pale and dark and pale and dark, until, seen from above, the pitch was a great musical stave laid across the earth. And in the very middle of it, on the third line, where the centre circle sat in its tilt toward the east, there was a single round, hollow, perfect mark.

A whole note. A semibreve. The longest thing in music, the one you hold until the conductor lets you go. And on a stave in the treble clef, the third line is **B**.

It was the note Poppy had been practising, alone, at six every morning since Easter. It was the note that began the piece the band had never played in public because she said it was too private. And only one man in the whole of Manchester knew that she played it, because only one man had been standing outside the bandroom window at dawn every day for seven months pretending to check the drains.

The band reached the centre. They turned. Callum lifted his baton, because the conductor had gone home with a cold, and nodded to Poppy.

She stood in the mud with her cornet at her lip, and for the first time in her life did not know whether she could breathe. She looked up. Not at the stands. At the grass. Across the field, stripe after stripe, she followed it, and found at the end of it all the one round O at her feet, and, standing in the technical area with his cap in his hand, soaked to the skin, the man who had made it.

William did not wave. He did not smile. He only nodded, very slightly, the way you nod to someone across a railway platform when you have decided not to be a coward.

She played.

Mark this, reader, if you have never heard a cornet in the rain. It is not a sweet sound. It is a *bright* one, a bright hard thread pulled through the wet air, and it climbed out of the old ground in one long note that held and held and would not let go, past the rusted stands, past the banner, past the cranes already waiting beyond the wall; and when she ran out of breath and had to lift her lip, the whole band took it up beneath her, twenty-six brass voices in the mud, and the crowd who had come to bury a football ground found that it had been given instead a hymn.

Nobody left. Ten thousand people stood in the rain and sang a tune they had known all their lives, and the groundsman stood at the edge of the grass he had made and wept, very quietly, into his cap.

### SCENE V
*After. The tunnel. A bucket of water, a towel, a cornet case with the lid open, and two people who had both been very brave and are now very tired.*

"You mowed a stave," said Poppy.

"I mowed a stave."

"On purpose."

"A groundsman does nothing by accident. We do not have the budget."

"William." She turned the cornet in her hands. "I'm going to Leeds. There is a school. They offered me the music department. The band is folding, and I thought—" she laughed, one short crack—"I thought, well, if I cannot keep the band I may as well keep the *pay.* I wrote to say yes. I have not posted it."

"I am going to Southampton," said William. "There's an academy. They want a head groundsman. I wrote to say yes. I have not posted it."

They looked at each other.

"We have *both* got letters in our pockets," said Poppy.

"That is the most Manchester thing I have ever heard."

"You were going to leave me at the same time I was going to leave you, and neither of us said."

"I said it to the grass."

"I heard," said Poppy. "Hannah Hughes told me. She told me in the chip shop. She said you said it wrong."

"Hannah Hughes has no respect for privacy."

"She's *thirteen*, William. She is the only honest person in Salford."

He laughed, and it came out of him like a held breath. Then, because it was the sort of evening on which everyone had said too much, he said a little more.

"I'm not going to Southampton," said William. "I tore it in two on the way down the tunnel. There's a vacancy with the city parks. Seven playing fields and a bowling green. It's not Wembley. It's got a lot of mud. I'd be good at it."

"I'm not going to Leeds," said Poppy. "I wrote to the school across the road from the park. They've an empty music room. They have, I'm told, *nine* unplayed cornets in a cupboard, and nobody to tell the children which end to blow."

"The band's still folding."

"The band's folded," said Poppy. "It's what we do. We fold, and then we're a very good *shape*." She put the cornet in its case and snapped it shut. "Mr. Pennywhistle says the room under the east stand is mine till the diggers come. After that, I'm told, we may rehearse in his flat. He has promised us biscuits. Which means we shall have none."

"Poppy."

"Yes?"

"The band is folding, and—" He faltered. A man may mow a stave in perfect straight lines and still be unable to finish a sentence. "And I would keep *you*. If that is a thing a person can say. In a tunnel. Wet."

She did not say *love.* She was Mancunian; she had her pride. She said, "I know. You mowed it in B."

And then, in the cold smell of the tunnel, among the buckets and the wet boots and the last of the Marsden brass, Poppy Ashworth kissed him once on the cheek, as one stamps a letter, and walked out into the floodlit rain with her dented cornet and her head very high. William stood quite still, with his cap in his hand, and the whole ground hummed.

From the shadows came a small, sniffling, delighted whistle, tuneless as ever.

"Pennywhistle," said William, without turning.

"I was not here."

"You have been here all night."

"I am the *caretaker*," said Mr. Pennywhistle, with immense dignity, wiping his nose on the Great Salford Novel. "It is my duty to take care. And I'll have you know that I left that door unlocked on purpose."
