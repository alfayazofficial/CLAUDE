## Chapter 55
### Cassava and Code

> *"I breed seeds and I cannot grow a way to say your name."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0385

---

### SCENE I
*Ibadan, Nigeria. A research station on a red-earth hill above the city, where the rooftops below lie like a sea of rust. Seven in the morning, and the harmattan not yet come.*

Victor Adeyemi, thirty-two, agricultural scientist, had been trying to breed a perfect cassava for eleven years, and in all that time had never once been late for a plant.

He was late now. He stood in the long green field of Plot Fourteen with a clipboard against his chest and let the sun come over the hill, and looked at the stems, which were taller than he had expected, and the leaves, which were whole where the mosaic disease should have curled them, and the roots, which he had dug up at dawn with his own bare hands and weighed on a kitchen scale, because the lab scale was being repaired and the gods of science are not proud.

Eleven kilograms a plant. Disease-free. It had never been done.

"Professor Afolabi," he said, into his phone, with the voice of a man announcing a birth, "it is ready."

"Is it a miracle?" said the voice at the other end, which belonged to Segun Afolabi, sixty, the director of the station, a dry, rumpled, comical figure in a tie that had been a wedding present to somebody else.

"It is a *line.*"

"I asked if it was a miracle."

"It is a line with a high yield and a tolerance to mosaic and a low cyanide content."

"That is the Nigerian definition of a miracle," said Segun. "Come and register it. I have the form. The only thing missing is the name."

Victor looked at the clipboard. On the line marked *Variety Name* a small neat blank awaited him, a little white doorway.

He had named a hundred lines, in his time, by codes: *TMS-14-A, IBA-9-11, ABD-02.* Machines had no feelings. But the registry of the Federal Ministry required, for any variety to be released to a farmer, a *name*: a word that a woman in a village could say across a market without being thought a clerk. He had had the form for a month. He had written on it, and crossed out, forty names. They were the names of mountains, of old kings, of his grandmother.

He had not written the one he wanted. He did not know how.

> *I breed the seed, and cannot grow the word.
> I know the genome of a thousand roots;
> I know which gene will carry which resistance;
> I can make drought and blight lie down like sheep.
> But I have sat these eleven months beside
> A woman at a loan desk, and I could
> Not tell her that her laugh was my best crop.
> A name is such a little thing to put
> On a form. And yet the blank has stood there since
> The month she came to the institute with chickens
> To ask me, shyly, if the peel could feed them.*

### SCENE II
*The same morning, on the other side of the hill. A poultry farm, forty thousand birds, and a loan.*

Funmi Balogun, thirty, poultry entrepreneur, had been smiling at people for two years in the hope that they would not ask her for money.

She was good at it. She smiled at the bank manager, the feed merchant, the man from the electricity company, and the pastor of the church, who had also lent her something. She smiled in the voice of a woman who owes six million naira to four institutions and a cousin; she smiled in the rhythm of a woman who has learned to say *next week* with a face like a sunrise. She was sharp and funny and exhausted, and she kept her books in a green exercise book with a cartoon hen on the cover, which was the only honest ledger in the building.

"Aunty Funmi," said a boy in the doorway of the office, "the man from the bank is at the gate."

"Tell him I am in the shed."

"He says you said that last time."

"Then tell him," said Funmi, "that I am in a *different* shed."

On the wall behind her hung a calendar from the feed merchant, with a photograph of a very contented hen, and, tucked in its frame, a card from the research station on the hill, with a diagram. On it, in a careful engineering hand, a man had drawn how cassava peel, properly dried and ground, could replace forty per cent of her maize in the feed. Beneath it ran a few lines of code for a spreadsheet, which he had written himself one night, to work out her ration to the kilo. He had sent it six months ago. He had never sent anything else.

The man from the bank came in. He was a patient man named Mr. Tunde Obi, fifty, bank manager and in a former life a dentist, who had learned that extracting a debt and a tooth required identical bedside manners.

"Mrs. Balogun."

"Miss."

"Miss Balogun, I have to inform you that on the thirtieth the board will call the loan unless there is a payment."

"The thirtieth is a Friday."

"It is."

"I will have it on the Thursday."

"You said that in July."

"And God was a witness," said Funmi, "and He has not corrected me."

Mr. Obi looked at her, a long, kind, professional look, from under a face that had seen eleven hundred tragedies in the form of a missed repayment. "Madam," he said, "I will tell you a thing that I should not. There is a rumour that the research station on the hill has a new cassava. A great one. It will cut the cost of feed by a third. The word is that the man who bred it has been asking where to find a poultry farmer who has the nerve to try it first."

"Which man?" said Funmi.

"The one who sent you the card," said Mr. Obi. "It is a small town, madam, and I was once a dentist. I am still told everything through the mouth."

### SCENE III
*Lisbon. A small white church in Alfama, on a street so steep that the groom had arrived on all fours. Saturday.*

Meanwhile, in a city where all roads lead down to the river and none of them are straight, a tram mechanic and a postwoman were being married by a priest who had known them both since the day the mechanic's tram broke down in front of the church and he had said a prayer to it.

"You are shaking," said Nuno Pereira, twenty-seven, best man, in the vestry, with an expression of deep concern.

"I am not shaking. I am vibrating at the *frequency of joy.*"

"You are talking to the cufflinks."

"They are very good cufflinks," said Tomás Villaverde, twenty-nine, who spoke to engines when he was frightened and had been reduced, in the absence of an engine, to a pair of silver ovals. "I am saying that I *appreciate* them."

The winter had asked its payment. It had asked it of the pair of them in full, and they had paid, as people do, with what they had; and in the paying they had learned the one thing the Keeper's note had not told them, which is that a debt well paid makes a marriage out of a courtship. It was enough to say that they had come through. It was enough that they were standing in a church in Alfama on a Saturday in October, in the autumn light, with a scent of grilled sardines coming in at the door.

The bride came up the aisle on the arm of the lamp-seller.

It was a great honour, and it was entirely against his wishes. Pereira, sixty, a dealer in lamps who had never sold one in his life and had told her, daily, for nine years, that business was bad, had said that he would walk her up the aisle only if she promised not to tell anybody he had cried. He was crying now, noisily, into the sleeve of a suit that had been in a trunk since nineteen-ninety, and all the way up the nave he was audibly telling her that it was a *good* sort of aisle, a *straight* aisle, a very *reasonable* aisle, as though he had built it himself.

"Pereira," whispered Inês Carvalho, twenty-seven, postwoman, in a white dress and a pair of her mother's pearls, "you are leaning on me."

"I am *guiding* you."

"You are leaning like a lamp-post."

"A lamp," said Pereira, with great dignity, "is a thing that shows the way. You have never once thanked me for it."

At the altar, Tomás saw her, and his face went completely, helplessly open, like a tram door.

Inês took a folded paper from her sleeve. Seven months it had lain in the pocket of her postwoman's jacket; she had read it in the bath, and on the tram, and once on the roof of the sorting office in the rain. It was addressed to a street that did not exist.

"I have a thing to read," she said to the priest.

"It is not in the liturgy."

"It is in the post," said Inês.

And she read the second line aloud. We shall not repeat it. Those who wish to know it must go back to the beginning, and stand in a doorway in Alfama, and ask the tiles; they are old, and they hold their tongues until they are asked in the proper voice. It is enough to say that it was short. It is enough to say that Tomás Villaverde, who had written it in the dark to a woman whose face he had never seen, had been begged by his own blushes to forget it, and could not; and that it was a perfectly good second line, and the best thing she had ever delivered.

"I do," said Inês, when the priest had finished weeping.

"I do," said Tomás, to the cufflinks, and then, hastily, to her.

### SCENE IV
*Vienna. A concert hall in the Third District. A Sunday matinée, a full orchestra, and a bride.*

Clara Weber, thirty-two, conductor, had a perfect ear and an imperfect patience, and had never in her life permitted a note to go flat.

She stood now on the podium of the great hall, in a white concert dress and her baton, at her own wedding, and the orchestra, who had been sworn to silence, were looking up at her with the pale terror of men who have never seen her smile.

In the front row, with a violin in his lap, sat a meticulous, shy, sandy-haired man of thirty-six, a violin-maker, who had built an instrument that only one woman in the world could play, and who had come, as he always came, in a clean shirt, an hour early, to be sure of finding his seat.

"Ladies and gentlemen," said Clara, in the voice she used for the brass. "The A, please."

The oboe sounded the A. The orchestra, as one, tuned.

All of them, to a man, to a woman, to the last second violin, tuned a quarter-tone flat.

It was the most appalling sound in the history of Vienna. The ushers winced. The old professor in the third row put his fingers in his ears. And Clara, with her baton raised, stood quite still in the middle of it, with tears running down her imperious face, and spoke the only verse she had ever written, in four plain lines to a man in the front row:

> *I have kept the pitch for thirty years, and I am tired of it.
> One note in all my perfect life rang false, and it was you:
> The wrong, the dear, the unforeseen, the flat and human one.
> I tune the world to you today. Come up and play it through.*

Jonas Müller, who had never in his life been the subject of a verse, stood, and picked up the violin, and walked down the aisle between the weeping horns, and with a perfectly steady hand played, in front of eleven hundred of the best-trained ears in Europe, the one note that was wrong.

It was the most beautiful sound in Vienna.

### SCENE V
*Gothenburg, on the sea. The southern archipelago, a ferry, a rock, a glassblower's two hands.*

Erik Johansson, thirty-four, glassblower, had burned his hand to see a woman again, and had been frankly insulted by how well it had worked.

It had been a small burn, a few seconds against the pipe, deliberately mistimed on a Tuesday, in the winter, in the sure and shameful hope that a certain nurse at a certain clinic would be on duty. She had been. She had dressed it, with a firm, blunt, tender competence, and looked at him over her glasses and said: *You did that on purpose, Mr. Johansson. The edges are too clean.*

"I did," he had said.

"I will not tell the clinic." She had taken his pulse, for no clinical reason. She had held it a second too long. "It is a very good pulse."

Two summers later, on a rock in the southern archipelago, with the sea going out in long grey silk to the edge of the world, thirty guests in woollen jackets stood in a ring around a table on which stood two tall glasses, blown that week, a pale green like the inside of a wave. They were the best thing he had ever made. The hand that made them was healed, a little shiny, a little tight, a faint pink scar across the palm.

"Hold them up," said Astrid Lindqvist, thirty-two, nurse, with the sea wind blowing her hair across her face, "and I will tell you whether you have done them right."

He held them up. She examined them, professionally, with the face of a woman who checks a wound.

"There is a bubble," she said.

"It is a flaw."

"It is a *pulse,*" said Astrid. "I left it in. I told you, in the summer. I left it in on purpose, in the stem of mine. It is a heart, Erik. The hand that burned remembers." She looked at him with those brown, steady, impertinent eyes. "I said I have not forgotten your pulse. This is it. This is the sound of it, in glass."

Behind them, Sofia Holm, twenty-eight, a tea-seller from the island and a woman of ferocious romance, was heard to sniff, and to murmur to nobody that she had never been so happy at a funeral, and when corrected, that it was perfectly the same.

They drank from the glasses. The sea went out and came back. And on the rock, among the thirty guests, there was a hush as of something very large and very tender being passed from one pair of hands to another.

### SCENE VI
*Ibadan. A Thursday, at the end of a long red afternoon. The field day. Four hundred farmers on benches under a tin shade, a microphone with an opinion, and a hen.*

At the end of the field day, in front of four hundred farmers, thirty women's co-operatives, a delegation from the Ministry and a young man from the radio, the director of the research station stood up, in a tie that did not fit, to announce the release of the new cassava.

"It is high-yielding," said Professor Segun Afolabi, to the benches. "It is resistant to disease. It cuts the cost of feeding the poultry of this country by one third. It has been bred by a man of exceptional patience, who has been up since four. It has a code. It has a number. It has had, until this morning, no name."

A murmur ran along the benches.

"I have here," said the Professor, holding up a form, "the registration. The name has been entered in a hand I recognise, at nine minutes to six this morning, by a very frightened young man, who then ran away and has been hiding behind the tractor. I am going to read it to you."

He cleared his throat. At the back of the tent, behind the last row, a thin, anxious, extremely unhappy man in a lab coat put his face in his hands.

"The name," said the Professor, with the air of a man reading the verdict of a very friendly court, "is *Funmi.*"

In the third row, between a farmer from Ogbomoso and a woman selling roasted corn, a young woman in a yellow head-tie had stopped smiling.

*Funmi.* A common word. A name. In the old tongue of that country, a thing that means, in plain English, *give me*, and in the longer form, *give me joy.* She had been told it as a child; she had been laughed at for it; she had grown up with a name that was a request.

"It is a very good variety," said Professor Afolabi, to the benches, "and I have a rule that a name should be a thing you can call across a field. I have tried. It carries."

And across the field, from behind the tractor, in an unsteady voice, like a man calling a bird that has flown over a wall, Victor Adeyemi said it again, not to the Professor, not to the benches, but to a woman in the third row in a yellow head-tie, with her hands over her mouth and the loan of six million naira falling from her shoulders like a winter coat:

"*Funmi.*"

She stood up. She walked, with great composure, down the length of the tent, past four hundred farmers who had forgotten to breathe. She stopped in front of the tractor.

"You named a *root vegetable,*" said Funmi Balogun, "after me."

"I named a *line.*"

"It is going to be in every farm in Nigeria. People will say my name in markets. They will say, *Is there any Funmi? Is it the good Funmi? Funmi is yielding.*" She was crying and laughing, in the helpless way of the very proud, and her yellow head-tie had come undone. "You cannot grow a way to say my name, you wrote."

"I could not."

"You *did.*" She took his clipboard out of his hands, quite gently, and held it against her heart, a plain wooden board with a form on it and eleven months of fear. "Come to my farm tomorrow. Bring the stems. I will feed them to the hens. And when the bank man comes on Friday, we will show him a *harvest.*"

"And on Saturday?"

"On Saturday," said Funmi, "you can call me whatever you like."
