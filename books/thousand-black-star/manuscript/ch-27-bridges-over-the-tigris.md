## Chapter 27
### Bridges over the Tigris

> *"I am building a bridge so you will have somewhere to come back to."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0076

---

*Enter the Chorus, between two rivers, one brown and slow, one that exists only in the rains.*

> Two roads are laid tonight by two proud hands:
> One crosses water; one outruns the sun.
> The first is built that someone may return;
> The second, that a woman may be gone.
> And both, my friends, are only ways of saying
> What neither builder dares to say aloud.
> Now mark: the one who makes the bridge is shy,
> The one who makes the road has given her knee.
> Walk softly. Spans are strong, but they have limits,
> And knees, and hearts, and mornings, have them too.

---

**Baghdad, the Karkh bank of the Tigris, five o'clock on a Thursday in spring.**

The bridge had no name yet. The city council had held three meetings on the subject, in the course of which a retired electrician named Huda Al-Tamimi, seventy-three, had proposed that it be called *The Bridge That Was Finished Before the Meetings*, and had been thanked for her contribution and asked to leave.

It was a footbridge, two hundred and forty metres from bank to bank, slung low over the river in a long white curve like the stroke of a calligrapher's pen. On the Rusafa side it came down at the foot of the old street of the booksellers, where on Fridays the pavement vanished beneath a tide of paper. On the Karkh side it came down in a garden of young palms, beside a café where the tea was served in small waisted glasses and the owner had never in his life been in a hurry.

It was nearly done. The deck was laid. The cables sang, very faintly, in the evening wind. At the centre, one segment of the walkway, three metres long, lay on the barge below, wrapped in tarpaulin, to be lifted into place at dawn. Until then there was a gap. Between the two halves of the bridge, over the brown water, nothing at all.

Mustafa Karim stood on the western half and looked at the nothing.

He was thirty-four, a civil engineer, and a man who believed in numbers because numbers, unlike people, could be checked. He had a pencil behind his ear, a hard hat in his hand, and a small, patient, stubborn face that had spent twelve years learning to look as though it had no feelings and had never once managed it. He had been awake since four. He had a spreadsheet in his head that told him the exact weight, in kilonewtons, that each cable would carry, and another, which he did not consult, that told him how many days it was until the woman who had drawn the bridge went away.

"You are staring," said Shaimaa Al-Obaidi.

She was the stonemason, twenty-eight, broad of shoulder and dry of tongue, in a head-scarf grey with dust and a leather apron that had seen the better part of a quarry. She was on her knees at the foot of the western pylon, tapping with a small chisel at the lettering of a stone plaque. She had been given the text by a committee. She had been tapping at it for a week.

"I am inspecting," said Mustafa.

"You are staring at a hole with your heart showing."

"Shaimaa."

"I carve stone, Engineer. I am not obliged to be tactful. It is the privilege of the trade. The stone cannot hear me and the public does not read the small print." She blew the dust from a letter. "Do you want to know what the committee wants me to write?"

"I have read the draft."

"*To the glory of the city and the unity of its people, this bridge was opened in the year such-and-such.* And then fourteen names. Fourteen! Including a deputy assistant of a department that did not, I think, lift a stone." She sat back. "Your name is ninth. Hers is eleventh. She drew the whole thing, and she is eleventh. I told them it was an affront to the alphabet."

"The order is by seniority."

"And the *bridge,* is it by seniority?"

He did not answer. Below them, on the bank, the schoolchildren of the district were already gathering for the evening's tour, in blue pinafores and white shirts, shepherded by two harassed teachers and a sheep that someone had brought and nobody would admit to. A ten-year-old with ink on his chin, named Ahmed, stood apart from the rest, counting the bolts in the abutment aloud.

"Four hundred and twelve," he announced. "Four hundred and thirteen. Engineer! There is one that is a different colour."

"That one," said Mustafa, "was a mistake."

"Does it hold?"

"It holds."

"Then it is not a mistake," said Ahmed, with the severity of the very young. "It is just a different *opinion.*"

Shaimaa laughed out loud. Mustafa, who was trying not to, took a long time to find his pencil.

---

She came at sunset, as she always came. He would never have admitted that he knew the hour.

Noor Hashim was thirty, an architect, and she walked like a woman who has been told, since the age of six, that she is going to be remarkable, and had made the best of it. She wore a long coat the colour of wet sand and carried a roll of drawings under one arm and, in her satchel, where it had lain for eleven days, an envelope with a foreign stamp.

She had drawn the bridge. Not the cables and the weight; that was his. She had drawn the *way* of it: the long curve, the benches in the shade of the pylons, the lamps along the rail, each set in a small pierced shade of brass so that at night the light fell on the deck in the patterns of old Abbasid windows, stars within stars. A hundred and twelve lamps. She had drawn every one of them by hand.

"You are early," said Mustafa.

"You are on the wrong side of the gap," said Noor.

"I am on the side that has the tea."

She had brought, as she always did, two glasses and a flask. She set them on an upturned crate by the pylon and poured; cardamom, sugar, and a thread of steam, the whole of Baghdad in an inch of amber glass. They drank together, on the bridge to nowhere, looking at the place where the bridge was not.

"Tomorrow," said Noor.

"Tomorrow at six. The crane comes at half past five."

"And then it is a bridge."

"And then it is a bridge," Mustafa agreed, "and I am a man who has nothing to do."

"You will have the next one."

"I do not want the next one." He said it to the water. "I want to stand at this end of it, at nine in the morning, on a Friday, and watch a boy with a bad shirt cross to buy a book he cannot afford. I want to watch a girl run across it, late for school. I want to watch an old man stop in the middle, as old men do, and be annoyed that there is no bench. There *is* a bench." He rubbed his eyes. "Do you see? I am not building a bridge. I am building a place for people to come back to."

He had said more than he had intended. In the tea-glass his reflection trembled.

"For whom?" said Noor.

"For the city."

"And for—"

"For *the city,* Noor."

She turned the glass in her fingers. In her satchel the envelope lay against her hip, like a small stone in a shoe.

"I have a letter," she said.

"I know."

"You *know?*"

"You touch your bag every four minutes," said Mustafa. "It is a professional tell. I once watched a contractor do the same with a counterfeit cheque." He did not look at her. "It is from abroad. It is thick. It is a good letter."

"It is a scholarship," said Noor. "In Copenhagen. Two years, for the study of rivers in cities: how a city should live beside its water. They have chosen me out of four hundred." Her voice had gone very quiet, and very exact. "I have until the seventh to say yes."

"The seventh is Saturday week."

"I can count, Mustafa."

"You should go," he said.

"You say that very fast."

"I have had eleven days to practise."

She laughed, not happily; she set down her glass. "You are *impossible.* You will not ask. You will not say stay. You will not even say that you will mind."

"If I said that I minded," said Mustafa, "you would stay. Out of kindness. And in a year you would be a woman who had stayed out of kindness, and you would look at this bridge, and you would not see a bridge. You would see a *wall.*" He turned to her at last, and his face, in the going light, was as plain and as gentle as a brick. "I would rather be a man you left than a man you were *kept* by."

"And what if I come back?"

"Then I shall be here."

"At the bridge."

"It will be here. I will be somewhere near it." He tried a smile, and nearly managed. "I am an engineer. I only promise things that have been load-tested."

She stood up. She walked to the very edge of the western span, where the white deck stopped, and stood there in her sand-coloured coat with the wind pulling at the roll of drawings, and looked across the gap to the other side, where the bookshops were putting out their lamps. A single boy was crossing the old stone arch at the end of the street, with a sack on his shoulder. He was very small at that distance. He looked, she thought, like a comma.

"I will tell you what I will do," said Noor. "I will not answer tonight. And I will not answer tomorrow. I will answer *after.* After the bridge is opened. After I have walked across it. I want to know what it feels like, to cross a thing I have made, before I decide to cross a thing I have not."

"That is reasonable."

"It is the least reasonable thing I have ever said." She did not turn round. "Will you walk with me?"

"First?"

"At the opening there will be a ribbon, and fourteen names, and a deputy of something. I mean *before.* At dawn. When the crane has gone and the last piece is in, and it is only us and the river."

"The council will be furious."

"The council," said Noor, "can have the sheep."

He stood beside her on the edge. Neither said anything more; for what is there to say on a bridge that does not yet touch the other bank? Below them, the Tigris went about its ancient business, carrying the sky to the sea. And Mustafa Karim, who did not believe in anything he could not check, took the pencil from behind his ear and, without a word, drew on the white edge of the deck, in a small, firm hand, where no inspector would ever look, a line.

It was a plain line, a hand's breadth long. Beside it he wrote a single number.

She read it, and did not ask what it meant. It was the span of her stride.

**Addis Ababa, Meskel Square, 6:10 on a Sunday morning.**

(*The altitude, two thousand three hundred and fifty-five metres. The air thin and cold, and bright as a struck bell. The sun not yet over the Entoto hills, but making up its mind.*)

The women's field had been called to the line, and Selam Tesfaye was kneeling on the tarmac with her left leg stretched before her like an unpaid bill.

"Do not look at it," said Abel Haile, the nurse who had been her friend for eleven years and her physiotherapist for the last four, and who was, at that moment, taping her knee with the grim concentration of a man defusing a clock. "It is looking at *you*, and it is not impressed."

"It has been looking at me for nine months."

"It has been making a very *clear* case. Have you heard it?"

"I have heard it."

"And?"

"I am not a tribunal, Abel. I do not rule on knees." Selam was thirty-three. She was lean and brown and fierce, with a long jaw and long thighs and a stillness at the centre of her that unsettled strangers; she had run for her country at three Games, and tomorrow, in all probability, would be able to run for her own convenience, and only that. "Four more hours. Then it may be as unhappy as it likes."

"And the injection?" said Abel, with the air of a man asking a thing he already knows the answer to.

"No."

At the barrier, a small, grey, exquisitely dressed man of sixty-two, with a spray of red roses held against his chest like a rifle, was watching her with an expression of deep suspicion. Yonas Mekonnen had been a florist for forty years and her coach for nineteen, and distrusted all things that could not be pruned.

"Take it," he said, pushing a rose through the barrier. "A rose in the pocket is a good-luck thing."

"It will be crushed."

"That is not a comfort, Yonas."

"I did not say it was a comfort. I said it was a rose."

Beside him, in a white netela shawl and a grey-brown headscarf, her prayer-cord wound twice round her fingers, stood the oldest and most terrible of her supporters: her mother, Tigist Girma, seventy-six, retired fishmonger, who had never been fooled by a daughter in her life and knew perfectly well that the knee was not the only thing the girl had decided not to mention. Tigist said nothing. She raised her cross, and kissed it, and held it for a moment above her daughter's head; then she lowered it, and put into Selam's hands a small twist of cloth in which were three roasted coffee beans.

"Chew them at the thirty-kilometre mark," said Tigist. "The beans know you."

"Yes, Mama."

"And Selam."

"Yes?"

"Run like a woman who has *finished,*" said her mother, "not like a woman who is *finishing.* There is a difference. The first has already won."

"I look forward to it," said Selam gravely.

"You will not even *be* here!"

"No," said Selam, and there was in it the whole of a long season. "I think that I won't."

At the very back of the crowd, behind the barrier, stood the man Selam did not look for.

Samuel Bekele was thirty-six, a coffee exporter of Merkato, steady, proud, and extremely tired. His brother, who had died in October, had left him eleven thousand sacks of green Yirgacheffe, a warehouse with a mortgage, and an unbroken chain of polite and utterly merciless creditors. He had a meeting at ten o'clock with the landlady of the warehouse, a blunt woman of fifty-five named Rahel Girma, who had told him that he might have until noon and not an hour after. He had a buyer in Hamburg who would sign for a price that was a kind of robbery, by the same hour. He had a telephone in his pocket, which had been ringing since five. He had not slept. He was wearing the wrong shoes. He had come to see her run.

**The Gulele Road, kilometre thirty-eight. Two hours and fifty-one minutes after the start.**

(*Dawit Tadesse, fifty-nine, librarian of Bahir Dar and a man who had never been to the sea, was sitting in a café on the corner with a transistor radio. He was a little vain. He had been given leave to narrate.*)

"And now, ladies and gentlemen," said Dawit, to a room of eleven men with glasses of macchiato and one cat, "I will tell you the manner of this race, for it is a thing of Homer. The leaders are three. They go, as the poet says, like three lions pursuing a fourth, who is an inferior lion and has already gone to lunch. Tesfaye, Selam, of the national club, is in second place. She has the knee of an old oak. She has the heart of a young one. She has, if I may say so, a great deal of timber."

"*Sit down,*" said the café-owner.

"I will not sit down. I am *speaking.* I speak for the nation. Behold, she approaches the corner of the Gulele Road, with a rose in her pocket—"

"How do you know there's a rose in her pocket?"

"I have *imagination,*" said Dawit, with great dignity. "It is the chief function of the librarian."

In truth, a mile and a half to the north, the race was no longer a matter for epic. At thirty-eight kilometres, a runner is not a hero; a runner is a small, hot, rhythmic debt, owing the road everything. Selam ran. Her breath came in two beats and went out in two. The sun was well up now, and the hills had turned the colour of honey; the crowds at the roadside had thinned, and then grown, and then become a single sound. Her left knee was no longer a joint. It was an argument, delivered at intervals, in the voice of a hot nail.

She chewed her three beans.

> *I have run twenty years to reach this road,
> And every step was borrowed from the next.
> I gave my childhood to the hill; my youth
> To the long dark of mornings; and today
> I give the knee. It is a fair exchange.
> I do not ask what I shall have for it.
> I ask the road to be a little shorter.
> The road is not a thing that hears. But look:
> The hill is gold. The air is thin. I breathe.
> Somewhere a woman says that I have finished.
> I'll run as though she were quite right.*

At the thirty-ninth kilometre, on the corner by the old petrol station, there was a thermos held up over the barrier.

It was a plain steel thermos with a dent in it. The hand that held it was a man's, not young, tired, and quite steady. It held it out for the length of two strides, and she could have taken it, and did not; for to take it she would have had to look. She looked.

Samuel Bekele was not saying anything. He had no breath to spare. He had run, she saw, along the barrier from the three-kilometre point, in the wrong shoes, in a jacket and a good tie, with a telephone buzzing like a trapped wasp in his pocket. His face was grey. He had never in his life looked less like a coffee merchant, and never more like a man.

"It is *buna,*" he gasped. "Black. No sugar. Mama said the beans know you, but the *coffee* knows me."

She laughed. It cost her a breath, and she paid it.

"You have a meeting," said Selam.

"Yes."

"At ten."

"It is nine-forty."

"You will be late."

"I will be very late," said Samuel, and kept pace with her along the barrier, three steps, four, five, the thermos held up like a lamp, "and the warehouse will be a day closer to the creditors, and I will, I am afraid, be in a great deal of trouble. But I thought—" He paused to breathe. "I thought it was a more interesting trouble than the other."

"Samuel."

"What?"

"*Go.*"

"I am going," said Samuel. And he kept running.

He ran with her to the corner of the square. He ran when the barrier ended and the road narrowed, along the footpath, among the crowds. He ran the last four hundred metres on the pavement, a man in a tie, weeping and laughing and out of breath, while on the road beside him the woman he had not yet said a single word to about love gave her last whole self to the line. A hundred metres out her knee went, as a branch goes in a storm, and she did not stop. She did not stop; she ran on it, the leg dragging like a flag, and the crowd, who knew what it was seeing, rose up in one body and made a noise that Dawit, in his café, described as *the sound of Mount Entoto clearing its throat.*

She crossed the line in third place, in two hours and forty-one minutes, and sat down on the road.

Abel reached her first. Then Yonas, with a rose. Then her mother, who did not run, but arrived, with her cross, as a ship arrives in harbour, having never doubted the harbour. Selam sat with her knee in both her hands, and the sun on her face, and the whole bright, thin, gold-and-blue morning above her, and said, to no one:

"It has finished."

"The race?" said Abel.

"The knee."

"And you?"

She looked up. At the barrier, a little way off, a man in a good tie stood quite still, with a dented thermos in his hand, and the telephone in his pocket ringing, and ringing, and ringing, and not one hand moved to answer it.

"I do not know," said Selam, honestly. "I have not been this far before."

The pigeons rose from the square. Somewhere a church bell was striking the hour. Whether she would run again, the knee did not say; whether the warehouse would stand, the telephone did not say; whether two people who had not said a word of love would say one, the page, with rare restraint, declines to forecast.

But Samuel went through the barrier, at last, and gave her the coffee.
