## Chapter 23
### Needle and Thread

> *"I am sewing your wedding dress for another man's bride."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0730

---

### SCENE I
*Kano, Nigeria. The tailors' lane behind Kurmi Market, ten in the morning, in the season of the harmattan. The wind has come down from the Sahara with a load of fine red dust, and the sun hangs in the sky like a coin at the bottom of a pond.*

In the lane behind Kurmi Market the treadle machines clatter in their hundreds, like a town of nervous sparrows, and on a harmattan morning the dust lies on the cloth, on the counters, on the cats, and on the eyelashes of the goldsmith, who was Aminu Yakubu, fifty, the owner of the loudest laugh in Kano and the shop next door to a tailor.

"*Yusuf!*" he roared, through the wall. "There is a very old woman in your doorway, and she is holding a fan like a sword!"

"I know," said Yusuf Abdullahi, from the dark of his shop. "I am hiding."

He was thirty-three, a tailor, devout and modest and the best in the lane, which he would have denied under oath. He was a narrow man with a clean-shaven, serious face, and hands like a pianist's, long and exact, that moved on a seam as if it were a sleeping child. He had a Singer treadle machine older than himself, a tape measure around his neck like a stole, and a prayer-cap that sat very straight. He had never said an unnecessary word, and had accordingly said, in thirty-three years, hardly any at all.

"Malam Yusuf," said Hajiya Zainab Bello, sweeping in. She was sixty-nine, a retired pharmacist and, in the opinion of Kano, the finest matchmaker north of the Niger. She had arranged four hundred and six marriages and been wrong, by her own count, in two. She sat on the stool in the corner and arranged her *zani* about her like a general disposing her flag. "I have come to commission a dress."

"For whom, Hajiya?"

"For a bride."

"I have measured many brides."

"Not this one." She opened her fan with a snap. "Swiss lace. White, but not the hospital white; the milk white. Sleeves to the wrist. A hem that falls as water falls. And embroidery. I have heard that you can do embroidery that cannot be seen except by the light."

"It can be done."

"It is for the wedding of Aminu Danjuma, the baker of Zaria, forty-three years old, a most honourable man, who has never said a kind word aloud but has done a great many in dough. His bride—" the fan paused "—is the midwife. Hauwa Garba."

There was a very small sound. It was the sound of a tape measure being dropped.

*A man of Yusuf Abdullahi's character does not faint. He merely sat down, with great deliberation, on the nearest bolt of cloth, and looked at a small lizard on the opposite wall as if it had said something of the highest importance.*

"Hauwa," said Yusuf.

"She is thirty years old. It is not too soon. Her father, Malam Sani, who is a retired boat mender and has had, in a city with no sea, nothing to mend but his opinions, is very much in favour. And the baker is a very solid man. His bread is the best in the north." The old woman fixed him with her hawk's eye. "You do not have an objection, I hope?"

"No, Hajiya."

"Because I have been a matchmaker for forty years, and I have a nose for an objection."

"I have no objection," said Yusuf. He spoke into the lizard. "It is a good match. I will make the dress."

"You will make it as for your own sister?"

"I will make it," said Yusuf Abdullahi, "as it should be made."

"Good. You will not be able to measure her, of course. It is not done. Her mother will bring you a dress of hers to copy." Hajiya Zainab rose, with a rustle. In the doorway she turned, and for a moment the hawk was gone from her face, and what looked out was only an old woman who had seen a great deal of weather. "Malam," she said quite gently, "you are a good tailor. I should hate to think I had commissioned the wrong man."

"You have not," said Yusuf.

When she had gone, the goldsmith put his head round the wall with a plate of groundnuts and a face of rare tact.

"Yusuf."

"Do not."

"I am only going to say—"

"Aminu."

"—that I have sold gold to four hundred grooms," said Aminu Yakubu, "and I have never once seen one look as you looked just now. And you are not the groom." He placed the plate carefully on the counter. "I have a wife. I know nothing. Eat the nuts."

---

The dress of Hauwa Garba came on Thursday, folded in a clean cloth, in the arms of her mother.

It was a plain green cotton dress, worn at the elbows, with a pocket on one side where a pencil had been carried, and ink on the right cuff, a small blue-black stain. She wrote the register of births. It was the stain of four hundred children's names. He turned it on the table beneath the lamp, and measured, as he had been told, the shoulder, the sleeve, the fall, and in the measuring he read her, as a man reads a page in a language he has studied for years: the way she carried her left arm a little crooked, from lifting; the narrowness of the waist, from forgetting to eat; the fold at the elbow where a woman has leaned on a doorframe through a long night, waiting for a head to crown.

He did not say her name. He had a rule.

That night, with the light gone, as it goes every night in Kano (it is the custom in Nigeria to say of electricity that *NEPA has taken the light,* as one speaks of a lodger who has run off), he lit a kerosene lamp, and set out the lace, and cut. It took him four hours. It was the best cutting of his life. When it was done he sat before the pieces, in the gold light, with the wind throwing handfuls of dust against the shutter, and he threaded a needle with black thread, and spoke. He spoke, not to God, who knew it already, but to the cloth:

> *I measure you by the cuff, and by the elbow,
> By ink that has forgotten it was ink
> And is a name. I measure you by the pocket
> Where you have carried pencils for the births
> Of other people's children. I have known
> The width of you for three years and a half,
> Since you were kind about my mother's cough.
> I have no right. I own a thread, a lamp,
> A Singer and a name too poor to put
> Beside a baker's. Therefore I will make
> The dress. I'll make it better than a king's,
> And sew in secret, in the final seam,
> One star in black. No bride will see it. No
> Groom turn the hem. It is the one thing I
> Am keeping. Let it be the dark in the white,
> The place I hid, that does not need to be seen.*

He sewed a single star, five points, in black, in the lowest seam, on the inside, where the lace would fold and nobody look. It was no bigger than his thumbnail. It took eleven minutes. Then he put the dress away under a clean cloth, and put out the lamp, and sat in the dark, with the dust against the shutter and his hands in his lap, until the dawn call came from the mosque at Kofar Mata and the whole city rose up in the grey to pray.

---

At twenty minutes to five that same morning, a woman in a green hijab came down the tailors' lane with a bag in her hand and her feet aching.

Hauwa Garba had been awake since the previous dawn. She had delivered a child in a compound in Fagge at two o'clock, a girl, eight pounds, protesting, and had washed her and wrapped her and put her into her mother's arms, and said as she always said, *Masha Allah, she has a good voice,* and then sat for an hour in the courtyard in the cold, drinking the tea they brought her, because the one thing nobody had taught her about midwifery was what to do with the hands afterward.

She was thirty, tall, quiet, with a long, still face and brown eyes that missed nothing. She had delivered four hundred and twelve children. She had learned to wait. It was a skill, she told the young nurses, and it was not the same as having time.

She was to be married in May. She knew the baker, who was honourable, and silent, and had once walked past her clinic door a hundred times without going in. She knew her father's heart, which was set on it, and Hajiya Zainab's, which was set on everything. She had said nothing. A woman of thirty in Kano has a great many ways of saying it.

In the lane, in the grey, one light was burning. It was a little yellow flame behind a slit in a shutter, in a shop the width of a coffin; and through the slit, because she was a midwife and had learned to see in the dark, she saw a man with his head down, quite still, with a thread in his teeth, and on his table a long cloth the colour of milk.

She did not stop. It would not have been proper. But she slowed, a very little, as one slows beside a sleeping house; and from behind the shutter, in the thin cold of the dawn, there came the last two lines of something spoken aloud, in a low voice, to nobody:

*"—the place I hid, that does not need to be seen."*

Hauwa Garba stood quite still in the red dust, with her bag in her hand.

She had heard, in the small hours, a great many things said by people who thought themselves unheard. She knew how a person speaks when he has stopped believing in an audience.

She knew whose voice it was. She had known it, in her own way, since a morning three and a half years before, when she had brought a pot of medicine to a tailor's mother and been given, in return, a cup of water in a clean glass by a man who could not meet her eye.

*I know whose dress it is,* thought Hauwa, *and I am waiting.*

She did not say it aloud. She said, instead, very softly, to the lamp, in the way one blesses a threshold: *Allah ya kiyaye.* May God keep you. And she went on down the lane, under the fading stars, with her aching feet, towards her bed, and the call to prayer rose over the city like a long gold thread being drawn through cloth.

### SCENE II
*Lagos. The third floor of Adeniran Court, a Wednesday in January. The harmattan has laid its fine red dust on every windowsill in the city, and on the nine desks of a company that no longer exists.*

The company had died on a Thursday and been buried on a Friday, in the form of a letter from a lawyer in Ikoyi; and the nine members of its staff, having read it aloud to one another in the toilet, had agreed to take it as a suggestion.

So they had stayed. Emeka Okafor, twenty-eight, founder of nothing, sat among them with a calculator and the expression of a man who has been forgiven by a congregation and cannot decide what to do with it. Tobi, twenty-four, had built a spreadsheet of Chidinma Eze's order book so beautiful that it was shown to visitors. Ngozi Afolabi, her oldest friend, a mechanic, had taken the big Juki sewing machine apart on the floor, in her coverall, and was speaking to it quietly, as one talks to a horse before an operation.

"A machine that has been set to one stitch," said Ngozi, "hates to be asked for another. That is not a fault. That is *character.*"

"Is that a remark about me?" said Chidinma.

"It is about the Juki," said Ngozi. "But I am glad you took it personally."

The visitor arrived at eleven. She was Madam Bimpe Adewale, buyer for the Ikeja boutiques, fifty, in a *gele* tied like the prow of a ship, and she walked the length of the room trailing a scent of expensive oranges and looked at the rack of Chidinma's armoured coats with the expression of one who has been shown the bill.

"Two hundred pieces," said Madam Bimpe. "I will take them for the Easter season. Fourteen million naira. But *softer,* my dear. Shoulders down. Brighter. A little more *welcome.* And this one—" she touched the indigo jacket, the sharp-shouldered one, the one that had walked down a runway in the dark "—this one is very strong. It is frightening. Women in Lagos do not wish to be armoured. They wish to be *admired.*"

The room went quiet. Nine people who were not employed by anybody stopped typing.

Emeka looked at the calculator. It said fourteen million. It said nine weeks of rent. He had, in the death of a company, learned the single lesson of his life, which was that a practical suggestion is a kindness and an opinion is a weapon; and he said, therefore, nothing whatever, and was a little astonished by how much it cost.

"I will make the soft ones," said Chidinma. "In the colours. I like them. They are good."

"Wonderful."

"Not the jacket."

"Darling—"

"The jacket is not softer, Madam. It is not for admiring. It is the one thing I have made that does not say *sorry.*" Her hands were trembling; she put them behind her back. "You may have two hundred pieces and I will be glad. I will not sell you a woman who is smaller than she is."

Madam Bimpe regarded her for some time. Then she laughed, a rich, low, startled laugh, and took out a pen the size of a baton.

"I have been buying clothes for thirty years," she said, "and no one has ever refused me a jacket. I find that it is making me hungry. Two hundred soft pieces, and the jacket in a window of its own, with a card that says *Not for admiring.* Is that acceptable?"

A cheer went up from the nine desks, which was a little ragged, because Tobi had been holding his breath.

"It is *excellent* arithmetic," said Emeka, to the calculator, with his ears red. "I have never before disliked a sum so little when it came out in my favour."

In the stairwell, as the buyer descended in a cloud of orange, a very old woman in a purple wrapper put out a hand like a bird's and gripped her sleeve. "I saw it in a dream, my daughter," said Mama Ngozi, who was fifty-nine, and a chapel leader of true faith and no sense of direction. "A woman in this building would be asked to make herself smaller. And she would say *no.* I did not know it was a *Wednesday.*"

"What do you want?" said Madam Bimpe.

"Only to bless you. Also, I do not have bus fare."

That night, when the nine desks were dark and the dust ticked against the glass, Chidinma went home to a room in Yaba and sat down on the edge of her bed, in front of a trunk. It was a plain wooden trunk, with a brass lock. She had a key to it on a string round her neck. In it, folded in tissue, lay a length of gold *aso-oke,* woven by a cooperative in Iseyin, which she had been cutting and stitching one inch a night since she was nineteen, for a woman of forty who had never in her life apologised to a room. She was twenty-seven. She had told no one. She had not told Emeka, who had carried boxes down three flights in his shirt-sleeves in a month of Saturdays and had not once asked what was in the trunk.

She put her hand on the lid.

"Not yet," said Chidinma Eze, aloud, to the gold. "I am not that tall yet. But I said *no* to the oranges." And she laughed, alone, in the dust, for the first time that week, and left the key where it was.

### SCENE III
*Karachi. Eleven at night. The newspaper office of the Sahil Daily, on the third floor of an old building in Saddar. Outside the sea wind is rattling a hoarding that says* DRAIN YOUR WORRIES: WE FIX NALLAHS.

At eleven at night the *Sahil Daily* smelled of ink, cold tea, and the particular sweat of the truth.

Sana Ahmed, twenty-seven, stood at her desk with her dupatta thrown over her shoulder and a motorcycle helmet in her hand. She was packing her notebooks. There were seventeen, each with a name on the spine, each with the dates and the amounts and the signatures that she had been collecting for four months, all of them concerning a creek, a contract, and a flood. The creek had been dredged, said the ledgers, at a cost of two hundred and ninety million rupees. The creek had not been dredged. This was the whole of her article, and the rest was footnotes.

"You will be careful," said her editor, who was sixty and had the face of a basset hound that has read the Constitution.

"I am always careful."

"You are always *loud.* It is not the same."

"Sir. I am going home."

"Take the main road."

"I take the main road every night."

"Then take it," said the editor, "*faster.*"

She went down the stair, and out into the warm night, which smelled of petrol and the sea. The street was dark and nearly empty; a chai-boy was stacking glasses; the old hoarding rattled and creaked. Her motorcycle stood where she had left it, under the single lamp, a small red Honda with a cracked mirror.

On the seat was a paper boat.

It was folded neatly, as a child folds one, with a crisp bow and stern. It had been made from a page of notebook paper. She knew the ruling. She knew the faint blue lines. She knew, with a cold, clear, slow dropping of the stomach, the handwriting that ran across the sail, because it was her own.

*Name. Contractor. Amount.* It was a page from the third notebook, from the bottom of her bag, in the office, in the locked drawer.

On the other side, in a different hand, a hand that had been taught to write by a clerk, a single line:

*The creek is dry. Let it stay dry.*

Sana Ahmed stood in the lamplight for a long time.

She did not look round. She did not need to. She had interviewed enough frightened men to know exactly how it felt when someone was watching from a car with the lights off, and she would not give him the pleasure. She turned the boat over once in her palm. It weighed nothing. It weighed about as much as a sentence.

Then she did the bravest thing she knew. She folded it back along its creases, and put it into the pocket of her bag, and put on her helmet, and she started the engine, and she rode the same way home.

*Reader, I will not tell you that she was not afraid. The Keeper has read her letter; it says plainly: "A man has threatened me and I pretend I am not afraid." Pretending is not the absence of fear. It is a form of rowing.*

---

At six the next morning, at the gate of the Salt Creek Cricket Academy, a coach in a white shirt too large for him was bowling an imaginary ball at a boy in a cap that said FEAR NOBODY.

"Sana-baji," called the boy, "will you see my wrist?"

"I will see it."

"Hamza-sir says it is better."

"It is much better," said Hamza Qureshi, who was twenty-nine, and had looked at her face for exactly one second, and had stopped smiling by exactly half. He did not ask. He took a cup of tea from the kerosene stove and put it into her hand, and said in a light voice, so that the boys should hear: "Madam Journalist. You are late for the nets."

"I am not in the team."

"Everyone is in the team who is early," said Hamza. "It is a new rule." And then, under the noise, in Urdu, so that only she could hear, with his smile still on for the boys and nothing at all in his eyes: "Show me."

She took out the paper boat. He held it for a long time, in the pale morning, with the sea wind lifting the corner of its sail.

"I will not tell you to stop," said Hamza. "You would not hear it. I will say only: on this field, you will always have eighteen people with brooms."

"It is not enough."

"No," said Hamza Qureshi, and put the boat, with great gentleness, into his own breast pocket. "But it is a very *large* number of brooms."
