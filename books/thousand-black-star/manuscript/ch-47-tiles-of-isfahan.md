## Chapter 47
### Tiles of Isfahan

> *"A pattern that never repeats is the closest thing to you."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0228

---

### SCENE I
*Isfahan, Iran. A ruined madrasa behind the great square, in the last gold hour of an August day. Scaffolding, a bucket of lime, a dome the colour of a turquoise someone has dropped into the sky, and a wall that is missing one corner of its own face.*

There is a legend in Isfahan, told by every tea-seller to every tourist, that the old masters made one mistake on purpose in every panel they ever laid. *Only God is perfect,* they said, and set one tile crooked, so as not to be presumptuous.

Ali Rezaei, thirty-six, tile master, did not believe the legend. He believed in measurement. But he had been kneeling since noon in front of a panel a thousand years too old for its own good, with a bucket of lime at one elbow and a stub of pencil behind one ear, and he was beginning to suspect that God had taken the legend personally.

"It is not a repeat," said a voice behind him.

"I know it is not a repeat."

"I mean that I have checked every unit in the field. It *looks* like a repeat. It looks like the five-fold stars that the masters used everywhere. But you set one star beside the next and the next one is turned a hair, and the one after, a hair more. The whole panel is made of a rule that does not close." Parisa Karimi, thirty-one, architect and restorer, had come up the scaffold with a clipboard held as carefully as a baby and the expression of a woman who has found, in a ruin, a handwriting she cannot copy. "I do not know how to restore a thing that has no template. Every other panel I have mended, I took one tile, I made forty."

"Then do not make forty."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean," said Ali, sitting back on his heels, "that you make *one.* And then you look at it, and you listen, and you make the next."

Parisa lowered the clipboard. "That is the worst building method I have ever heard."

"It is the only method that is true to the panel."

"It is *slow.*"

"It is the only speed I have." He took a glass of tea from the ledge beside him, because she had brought it, and had not said so, and he had not said thank you, which in their long habit of exactness was a form of understanding. He bit the sugar-cube between his front teeth and drank through it, the old way, and for a while the two of them looked at the wall.

"A man from Shiraz is selling a carpet in the lane," said Ali, "to a woman who does not want it. I can hear him from here."

"He has been selling it since morning."

"He will go on until she buys it, or one of them dies."

This was Reza Jafari, fifty-seven, trader, a man of such profound stubbornness that he had once sold a Shirazi rug to the husband of a woman who had come in to return the same rug. He had been conducting his business in the lane below the madrasa for three days, and had acquired an audience of tourists, pigeons, and two municipal inspectors. Now he stood at the foot of the scaffold, shouting up with great politeness.

"Master Ali! Madam Architect! I have heard everything!"

"You have heard *nothing,* Reza," said Parisa.

"I have heard that the pattern does not repeat, and the man must listen to each tile before he lays the next. I am a trader. Do you know what that is called in trade? It is called a *relationship.* And may I recommend, for the sake of your own *lives—*"

"Reza," said Parisa, going the colour of a pomegranate.

"—that a pattern which never repeats is the best a man can sit down to, because it is never boring, and never finished, and nobody can say you have done it *wrong.*" Reza bowed with great dignity, tripped over the carpet, and sat down in the lime.

Ali did not laugh. He turned very slowly towards her. He had not planned it; he had planned, for eight months, to say it on some day in the autumn, with proper notice and a proper bunch of flowers. But the light was going gold, and the dome behind her was the colour of a thing that does not last, and a man will be forgiven for the one sentence he has never been able to carve.

> *I have laid the same star ten thousand times,
> And every star was perfect, and was dead.
> The masters knew: one crooked tile, no more,
> Lets in the breath of someone who is living.
> You are the tile that will not lie like others.
> You turn a hair. You turn again. You fail
> To close the pattern. And I find, to my
> Considerable alarm, I would not have it
> Closed. I would rather listen all my life.*

Parisa Karimi, who restored ruins because she did not know how to restore herself, said nothing for so long that the muezzin, in some tower beyond the square, began the evening prayer. Then she took off her glove.

"My mother is in Shiraz," she said. "She prefers that a man's uncle call. With flowers. And sweets. It is a very old, very silly custom."

"I have an uncle."

"The tailor?"

"The tailor. He has been waiting for the call since I was nineteen. He has kept a suit."

"Tell him Friday," said Parisa. "Tell him to bring the *gaz.* Not the sugar one. The one with the pistachios."

"I will tell him."

"And Ali."

"Yes?"

"Do not make forty."

Below them, in the lime, Reza Jafari wept softly, and said it was the dust.

### SCENE II
*Baghdad. The new footbridge over the Tigris. Evening of the same week. A flock of pigeons, a ribbon, and a plaque.*

The new bridge was a thing of steel and glass and cable, and people had been walking on it since noon, to be sure it would hold.

It had been built in two halves, from either bank, as the best bridges are, so that it should meet in the middle, in the middle of the river, in the middle of the sky. The engineers had reckoned a tolerance of fifteen millimetres. When the two halves came together on the thirty-first of May, they had met with a gap of three.

"Three millimetres," said Mustafa Karim, thirty-four, civil engineer, to a crowd of two hundred and a television camera. "In a river that is a kilometre wide. I would like it noted that nobody has ever done better, and that I am not at all pleased."

There was applause. In the third row, a young woman in a long grey coat and a green scarf did not applaud. Noor Hashim, thirty, architect, ambitious, eloquent, stood with her arms folded and her eyes on the cables. She had not said a word to him in three weeks. She had also not left his side at any public function, and the two phenomena were beginning to confuse the neighbours.

The ribbon was cut. A poet named Huda Al-Saadi, twenty-five, devout and best friend of the engineer, climbed the little podium and began an ode of two hundred and eleven lines, each ending in the same rhyme, which was *Tigris.* The crowd, which had its principles and its supper, began to drift down the ramps. On the parapet a young stonemason named Shaimaa Al-Obaidi, twenty-eight, who had spent a month carving the plaque in the dry heat and was the wryest woman in Baghdad, stood with her chisel in her belt and her face turned carefully to the river.

"Shaimaa," said Mustafa. "The plaque says *Mustafa Kareem.* My name is spelled *Karim.*"

"I know how your name is spelled." Shaimaa did not turn. "I carved it correctly. Then a flake of limestone came off the *i,* and in fixing it I made it, in every sense but spelling, a *better* name. You may be wrong in the particular, Mustafa, but you are right in spirit."

"Nobody who builds a bridge," she added with great dignity, as he opened his mouth, "is *entirely* himself."

By the time the ode had reached its hundred-and-fortieth line, the footbridge was nearly empty. The lights came on along the cables, white and blue, and on the water below the long, slow lanterns of the fish-grillers began to glow along the bank, with the smell of *masgouf* and woodsmoke rising up to the rail. The heat that had stood on the city since June had finally drawn a little back; a hot wind from the desert went down the river like a hand smoothing a cloth.

In the middle of the bridge, where the two halves had met, a seam of three millimetres ran across the walkway like a very small, very polished scar. Noor was standing on it.

"You are standing on my error," said Mustafa, coming up.

"I wanted to see it."

"It is not a big error."

"It is the only thing on the whole bridge that is *honest.*" She looked at him, and then at the river. "Mustafa. I answered the university."

"I know."

"You know?"

"Huda told me. She was drunk on her own ode." He had put his hands behind his back, which is a thing an engineer does when he does not trust them. "You go in three weeks. Two years. Copenhagen. A studio on a canal. They want you to design a thing that has not been built."

"Yes."

"I am glad."

"You are not," said Noor, "glad."

"I am extremely glad, and also not glad, and I would like the two to stand on the bridge together without a fight." He drew a breath. "Noor, I wrote once that I was building this for you to come back to. I wrote it in the dark, and I should like to correct it. A bridge you can only *come back* over is not a bridge. It is a leash. I built it to go both ways. That is the engineering of it. A bridge lets you go, and lets you come, and does not mind which."

She was silent a long time. The call to prayer from a nearby mosque drew itself out over the water, and from the Rusafa bank a late firework spilled a thin red thread across the dark.

"I do not know if I will come back," said Noor, "to the city. Or to *you.* I want to be honest."

"I know that also."

"And you are not going to ask me to say."

"I am going to ask you," said Mustafa Karim, slowly, "to walk with me to the east end of the bridge, and back to the west, and then, if there is time before your tea gets cold, to the east again. And we will see how far that is. And I will not say another word about it."

Noor took her hands out of her coat pockets. She looked down at the seam. Then, carefully, she stepped across it, off the error, into the sky on the other side; and he stepped across beside her, a respectful two feet away; and they walked, without touching, the length of a bridge that did not need to be anyone's leash, and she did not say *yes,* and he did not say *wait.* It was the open sort of ending, and the Keeper, who disliked a closed door, let it be.

### SCENE III
*Mirpur, Dhaka. A Friday in the high monsoon. The lane under water to the ankle; the sky the colour of wet slate. A third-floor flat, a tea stall, and a parcel in brown paper tied with a string that has been to Rotterdam.*

It had arrived on a Thursday in the arms of a courier who stood at the foot of the stairs in the rain and refused to go up.

"One thousand eight hundred taka," said the courier. "Customs. For *cloth.*"

"It is a gift," said Fayaz.

"Gifts are taxed at the rate of generosity."

"That is not a rate."

"It is in the *regulations,*" said the courier, who had never read them. Fayaz Rahman, twenty-six, had in his pocket two hundred taka, and in his head the grey arithmetic of nine lakh still owing to a man his father had trusted. The Singapore contract worked him from midnight until eight, and was kind, but not that kind.

"Bhaiya," said a voice from the doorway of the tea stall, "I will lend it."

Bunny had come across the flooded lane with his trousers rolled to the knee and a tin in his hand. In the tin was the stall's takings of three days. He put it into the courier's hand without being asked. "It is a *loan,*" said Bunny, with the gravity of a banker. "At two spoons of sugar, one of condensed milk, repayable at tea."

"I cannot take your money."

"It is not *my* money. It is the *stall's* money. I am only its guardian." He looked at the parcel, and at the brown paper, and at the Dutch stamp, and at the neat handwriting, and made a judgement on it that would have done credit to a high court. "A parcel that comes from so far *behind* you, Bhaiya, should be paid for in advance."

Upstairs, in the third-floor flat, they opened it at the table. Tithi cut the string. Their mother sat with her hands in her lap, breathing carefully, on a cushion that Tithi had put there, and their father, home since June from the hospital, thinner, slower, and unusually quiet, sat in his chair by the window with a stick across his knees and watched without saying a word. On the table under the lamp lay a letter in a Dutch envelope, which Fayaz kept inside *Thinking in Systems,* and had read eleven times, and had never shown anyone.

The paper came away. Inside was a smell: wax, and woodsmoke, and indigo. Folded in the middle lay a note that said, in careful Latin letters, *Do not iron. — E.* And on the corner of the wrapping, chewed, was a single green feather.

"She says," said Tithi, looking at the note, "that you must *not iron* it."

"A cloth that cannot be ironed," said Bunny from the door, dripping, "is a cloth with a plan."

Fayaz unfolded it.

It was a great length of indigo, as deep as the river at midnight, and across the whole of it, in rows, were dots. They were black. They were so black that at first the eye took them for nothing, a shadow on the weave, a stain of some darker blue. Only when he turned the cloth to the lamp, and the light came across it at a slant, did they rise up out of the dye, one by one by one, each with five small points, each a star, each bright with the dull gloss of wax-resisted ink, like a thing you could see only at the edge of your attention. They were *bintang.* They were black. They went on to the end of the cloth.

"It is *beautiful,*" breathed Tithi.

"It is a *star-field,*" said their mother.

Their father got up. It took him a long time. He crossed the room with his stick and put out one hand and touched the cloth, as a man touches a thing he has no business touching, and held it to the window, and looked at the weave against the grey light.

"The dye is true," he said. "It does not run." And then, after a pause, to no one: "Tell her thank you."

Fayaz could not speak. Their mother, who had coughed twice and recovered, looked at him with eyes bright from the effort of looking, and asked, in the voice of a woman who has been waiting a year to ask: "This girl. The Java one. Is she kind?"

"She is," said Fayaz, "extremely punctual."

His mother laughed. It cost her a minute's breath. It was worth it.

---

At ten that night, which in Rotterdam was six, he propped the laptop on two textbooks, lit a candle in case, and pressed the green button. Behind the screen the room came into being: a long bare studio with steel windows, a harbour under cloud, a tall drying-rack hung with a dozen unfinished cloths, and a young woman with her hair pinned up by a pencil.

"You are four hours *behind* me," said Fayaz.

"I know. It is a demotion." Ermis Larasati put her chin on her fist. Her face was thinner, a little, in the Dutch light, and the tin roof of her laugh was exactly the same. "I have been looking at the clock since April. I have been awake in your yesterday for four months. It is *very* strange, Fayaz. I used to know what would happen to you. Now I must wait."

"You were never in my future. You were only an hour early."

"It is the same thing."

"It is not." He lifted a corner of the cloth to the camera. In the dark, the black stars did not show. He tilted it. They rose, one by one. "I have counted them."

"Of course you have."

"There are nine hundred and ninety-nine."

She did not answer. In the studio behind her a kettle, which was a Dutch one, began to whistle, and went on whistling, and she did not get up.

"The last is bare," said Fayaz. "In the corner. A star the shape of the others, but not dyed. Just the cloth. It is the only one you can see without the light."

"Yes."

"Is it a mistake?"

"I do not make mistakes in wax," said Ermis. "I make decisions."

"What is it for?"

"It is for when the sky is clear," she said. "I do not know what for. I only know that it is for *then.*"

For some time neither of them spoke. A tin roof, in the monsoon, in Mirpur, beat out the long drum of the rain. In Rotterdam the kettle shrieked and, at last, stopped, as a thing stops when somebody turns it off, because nobody else will.

"Fayaz," said Ermis.

"Yes."

"Do not say it."

"I was not going to."

"You were going to say that you would tell me everything, if you were free to."

"I was going to say," said Fayaz, "that you were kind."

It was not what she had expected. He saw it in her face. She had been braced, as people are, for the thing that has no name. And what he had given her was the other thing: the sentence she had once handed to him on the other end of a candle, and which he had been carrying since, folded very small, inside a book.

"I have seen it," he said. "I am four hours ahead of you tonight, and in that time I have already lived this Friday, and I can report that it was *very* good, and that you were kind in it. The studio, the harbour, the kettle. You were kind." He stopped, because his voice had done the thing it did when she laughed, only the other way. "I thought you ought to hear it from the future."

Ermis said nothing at all. She put her hand over her mouth. On the screen, the pencil in her hair slid, and her hair came down, and behind her, in the grey Dutch window, a gull went by like a thrown stone. At last she took her hand away and said, in a small and entirely ordinary voice: "It is the first thing anyone has told me from the future that I wanted to hear."

"Then I will tell you more next week."

"Fridays."

"Fridays," said Fayaz. "Ten, mine. Six, yours."

"I am *not* going to say *eleven,*" said Ermis, and wept, and was laughing in the same breath, so that the whole feeble connection shook.

---

When she had gone he did not sleep. The power failed at midnight, and he lit the candle, and laid the cloth across his knees; and being a man who could not leave a question alone, he began, with a pencil and the back of an old bill from the electricity board, to measure it.

It took him till two. The stars were in rows; he had known that. But the rows were not level. Each lay, by the smallest degree, at a different angle from the row above, so that the whole cloth, from first row to last, was twisted by one steady turn. He drew it, and drew it again, and worked out the angle. It came to fifteen degrees, to the width of a thread.

He sat very still with the pencil in his hand.

Fifteen degrees. It is what the sky turns, in one hour.

Outside, the rain was slackening. Somewhere in the lane a boy was singing a song from a film, with the wrong words. The candle burned straight. And in a rented flat on the third floor, a man with an ironed shirt and nine lakh of debt held a cloth made of nine hundred and ninety-nine black stars and one clear space, and thought, as a man does who has been given a very long sentence in a very small hand, that it would take him a lifetime to finish reading it, and that he had, he found, no objection at all.
