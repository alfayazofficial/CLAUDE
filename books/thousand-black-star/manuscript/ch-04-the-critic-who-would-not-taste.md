## Chapter 4
### The Critic Who Would Not Taste

> *"I cook one bowl each night for the woman who never returned."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0510

---

The people of Osaka are said to eat until they fall down. The expression is *kuidaore*, and the city is proud of it, as other cities are proud of their cathedrals.

On the night of the new moon, in the narrow alley behind the Tenma canal where the lanterns hang so close together that they gossip, there was a shop with eight stools, one counter, a curtain the colour of old tea, and a single man. The curtain bore, in letters faded by many winters, the words **TANABE-YA**. The man bore a white towel about his head, a pair of forearms scarred with small honest burns, and a silence so complete that his regulars had been known to lose money betting on it.

"Seven words," said Daichi Ito, setting his phone upon the counter. "My calculation. Since opening at six. *Irasshai. Hai. Mō ippai? Arigatō.* That is four. And a *nn*, which I count as a half, and a cough, which I do not."

"Cough counts," said Rin Hayashi, from the third stool.

"A cough is not a *word.*"

"It is when *he* does it."

Daichi, thirty-four, a historian from Kobe who was said to be writing a history of the noodle and was believed by his friends to have been writing it since the Meiji era, folded his hands. He was a superstitious man. He would not sit at a table of four, nor whistle at night, nor begin a chapter on a Friday, and for the past fourteen months he had also declined, with a small shudder, to look at stool number eight.

For stool number eight was empty. It was always empty. Though the shop was full, though salarymen stood in the alley in the November chill with their collars up and their breath standing before them, stool eight stayed bare, and at the end of the counter, in front of it, a place was laid: lacquered chopsticks upon a rest, a glass of water, and a small blue towel, folded in thirds.

At eleven-forty, Hiroshi Tanabe would make one bowl.

He made it exactly as he made all the others: the clear brown broth, fourteen hours in the pot, kombu and pork bone and dried bonito and a hundred small secrets; the noodles, thin and straight and springy, lowered into the water for exactly the time that it takes to say a short prayer; the pale slice of pork, the half-egg gone soft as a thought, the green onion cut so fine that it looked like a rumour. He set it upon the counter before the empty stool. He stood back, and bowed to the air.

And for twenty minutes, while the room went quiet, the bowl sat, and steamed, and cooled.

"It is a *ghost,*" whispered Daichi. "I have told you. It is a ghost, and it eats."

"It does not eat," said Rin. "I have watched."

"That is *because* it is a ghost."

"Daichi. The man gives it a bowl. At midnight he pours it into the sink."

"Into the *sink?*"

"And then he washes the bowl, by hand, and dries it, and puts it on the shelf with the others, and does not use it again." Rin looked at the broad, silent back at the stove. "Four hundred and twenty-six nights."

"He is counting?"

"He is a man who has counted since he was a boy. I think he counts the way other men pray."

At the stove, without turning round, Hiroshi lifted one finger from the ladle, lightly, as a conductor does. It meant: *you are speaking of what is mine.*

Daichi shrank. Rin laughed. In the corner a very old woman in a quilted jacket, who had been shelling pickles for an hour into a steel bowl, said briskly, in the high dry voice of Kyoto, "*Honma, you boys are noisy. My son hears everything. He has been hearing everything since he was four years old. He just does not say.*"

"Obā-san," said Rin, with deep respect. "You know the story?"

Nanami Shimizu, seventy, retired welder, gossip, and Hiroshi's mother, placed a pickle in her mouth, looked at the ceiling, and said, with a profound air of withholding:

"I know *some* of it."

---

Across the canal, five floors up, in an apartment of white walls and exactly three plants, a woman sat in the dark with her phone, and did not weep. She had learned not to. Weeping, since the illness, had a flavour of nothing, which is the worst flavour of all.

Her name was Mei Sato. She was thirty-three, and for six years she had been the most feared and most loved restaurant critic in Osaka: author of the Sunday column *A Hundred Bowls,* in the *Naniwa Shimbun,* wherein she had once reduced a three-star kaiseki master to tears with the single sentence *"The soup is afraid of its own salt."* People said that Mei Sato could taste the sorrow in a sauce. They said she could name the hand that held the ladle. They said, in the tone of men who fear for their trade, that if you wished to be remembered, you should be eaten by Mei Sato.

Fourteen months ago she had had a fever, a common one, of the sort that arrives on a Tuesday and is forgotten by Friday. On Saturday she had sat down before a bowl of her favourite *tamagoyaki,* and had tasted nothing.

Not weak. *Nothing.* A warm softness, as of wet cloth. Salt, a ghost of salt, there and then not. She had put down her chopsticks and had looked at the egg, which was yellow, and glistening, and perfectly made, and had understood that she was standing at the edge of a country from which she would not be permitted to return.

"I'm *telling* you," said Nanami Yamamoto, from the sofa, with the severity of a woman who works the Tempozan ferry in all weathers and does not take back a word. "You are going to the doctor."

"I have been to six doctors."

"Go to a seventh."

"They say it is the nerve. They say it may return. They say it may not." Mei's voice was flat. "They say, *Miss Sato, you are very lucky; you can still smell lemon.* I can smell lemon, Nanami. I cannot taste a lemon. A lemon is a shape and a cold."

"So what do you do? With the *column?*"

There it was. The question that her friend had waited fourteen months to ask, and had asked only on the night when the whole country was reading its own heart in a stranger's ledger.

Mei said nothing for a long while.

"I write from memory," she said at last. "Every Sunday, I go to a shop. I sit. I order. I watch the faces of the people eating. I hear the sound the noodles make, I feel the heat, I watch the way a man closes his eyes when the broth goes down. And I write what I *remember* it would taste like, if it were good. Or bad." She put down the phone. "I have been right, Nanami. Mostly I have been right. That is the shame of it. I am a counterfeit whose forgeries are better than the originals."

"Your editor knows?"

"Mr. Kato suspects. He has begun sending me to places that cannot lie. Tofu shops. Plain noodles." She smiled. It was a dreadful smile. "And Sakura at the news-stand, who thinks she can do my job better than I, has begun to read my column aloud with a very peculiar voice."

"Sakura Takahashi is a *snake.*"

"She is a newsagent. It is the same thing, with more weather."

Nanami laughed, in spite of herself. Then she took the phone from the table, thumbed it alight, and without speaking held the screen out, as a nurse holds out a cup.

*ENTRY 0510 — Osaka, Japan.*
*"I cook one bowl each night for the woman who never returned."*

Mei looked at it.

She looked at it for a time that was a good deal longer than it takes to read eleven words.

*Entry 0810 — Osaka, Japan. "I can no longer taste a thing, and I am ashamed."* That one was hers; she had written it three nights after the fever, to a woman in England who would never tell. It stood two columns to the right. They did not touch. They did not need to.

"You know him," said Nanami.

"I have not seen him in fourteen months."

"But you know who he is."

"He is the best cook in Osaka," said Mei Sato, in a voice she did not recognise as her own. "He was my Thursday for two years. Seat eight. He never asked me what I thought. He did not need to. I would eat, and I would put down my chopsticks, and he would look at the bowl, and I would nod, and that was the whole of the review. And then the fever came. And I could not go back to the one man who would know, in a *glance,* that the nod was a lie."

"So you did not go."

"So I did not go."

"For fourteen months."

"I am a very *disciplined* coward," said Mei.

She rose and went to the window. Below, in the long black ribbon of the canal, the lights of the city lay broken and trembling. A boat went by, with a small red lantern at its prow, and a man singing.

It was then, as she stood against the glass, that she spoke, not to Nanami, and not to the night, but to the thing in between that is called the heart's ear, and she did not do so in prose:

> *They say the tongue remembers what it loved.
> They lie. The tongue forgets. It is the mind
> That holds the sweetness, and the mind grows thin.
> I have a hundred bowls inside my head,
> And every one is cold. I taste them still
> The way a blind man tastes the sun: by heat,
> By the soft weight of light upon the skin.
> He cooked for me four hundred nights and six,
> And I was not there to be disappointed.
> Oh, cruel kindness, to forgive so hard
> A woman who has nothing left to judge!
> I will go down. I will not eat. I will sit
> And let him see me not taste, and be shamed,
> And then at last be free.*

"That," said Nanami Yamamoto, wiping her eyes, "was extremely *bad* for a woman with no sense of taste."

"I had a metaphor."

"You had a *sword.* Go. Go before you think of a better one."

---

It is a fact little known to those who have not suffered it that the world, when stripped of flavour, grows louder.

Mei Sato walked the six minutes to the alley and was astonished by it. She could not smell the sweet-sour breath of the *takoyaki* stall, with its octopus and its bonito flakes dancing in the steam. She could not smell the grilled eel, nor the fried batter, nor the sea-reek of the canal. But she could *hear* it all: the clack of a tin spatula; the hiss of oil; the laughter, the high laughter, of three girls in rain-capes; a vendor calling "*Atsui, atsui!*" in the cracked tenor of a man who has sold hot things for forty years. She could *see* it. She could feel the warmth of every shop-front as she passed, on one cheek and then the other, like a series of small hands.

She came to the curtain at eleven-thirty-eight.

She did not go in. She stood at the gap between the cloth and the post, and looked.

He was there. Of course he was there. He was in the white towel, with his back to the room and his hands upon the pot, a heavy, still, unhurried man, and she saw the shoulders that she had watched for two years, and a thing happened inside her that she had not been prepared for; a feeling like homesickness for a house she was standing in front of.

The regulars were silent. The old woman at the end had stopped shelling pickles. The historian, with the wide eyes of a man who has seen the dead, was holding his phone aloft as though to capture a miracle.

And then, at the exact stroke of eleven-forty, Hiroshi Tanabe lowered noodles into the water.

She watched him make it. She had always wished to watch him make it, and had never done so, for a critic does not stare at the cook; it is a breach of the contract. But now she was no critic. She was a woman in a doorway. She saw him lift the noodles with the long chopsticks in one smooth turn of the wrist and shake them, twice, with a motion like a bell-ringer's. She saw the broth go in, the colour of dark amber. She saw the egg laid down, the onion scattered, the pale pork fanned like a hand of cards.

She saw him set the bowl before the empty stool, and stand, and bow.

And then she did the one thing she had not told Nanami she would do.

She pushed aside the curtain.

---

There is a particular noise, in a small room full of people, when a sound that has been expected for fourteen months finally arrives. It is not a gasp. It is much quieter than that. It is the sound of eight strangers deciding, simultaneously, to hold their breath.

"*Irasshai,*" said Hiroshi Tanabe.

He had not turned. He had known her footstep, as he might have known the footstep of weather.

"I am sorry," said Mei. "I am late."

He said nothing. His mother, at the far end, said "*Ara,*" very softly, and put down a pickle.

Mei crossed the floor. It was a short floor, four steps, and she took each of them as one crosses ice. She sat on stool number eight. It was warm. It was warmer than the room. And it took her a moment to understand why, and when she did, she nearly stood up again: it was *kept* warm. He had placed upon it, earlier that evening, a small heated cushion, such as the old use in winter, and had plugged it in at the wall, and had done so for four hundred and twenty-six nights.

She put her hands in her lap.

"It is still hot," said Hiroshi.

It was the first thing he had said, in her hearing, in three years that was not *irasshai* or *arigatō.* His voice was low, and very slow, like a bell struck in a distant temple. "It has been on the counter two minutes. The broth holds."

"Hiroshi-san," said Mei.

"Eat."

"I *cannot.*"

She had meant to say it better. She had meant to say it with the whole of her critic's armoury of syntax and irony. What came instead was five words, flat on the counter like a dropped plate, and she heard them, and did not take them back.

"I cannot taste," said Mei Sato. "Since last September. A fever. It has not come back. I am a woman who writes about food and cannot taste it, and I am so ashamed that I have not been able to walk down your street." Her eyes were stinging, which she found, in an odd detached way, to be very interesting. "I wrote a column every Sunday from *memory.* I am a fraud. I do not deserve a bowl. I do not deserve a *stool.* You should pour it down the sink."

She waited for the blow.

In the shop, you could have heard a noodle fall.

"I know," said Hiroshi.

"You—?"

"Your column." He wiped his hands upon the towel, slowly, one finger at a time. "Eleven weeks ago. The shop at Namba. You wrote that the broth was *a clear autumn pond.* Their broth is thick. It is a pig's bath. A person who had *tasted* it would not say *pond.*" He paused. "You do not make mistakes by being wrong. You make them by being *beautiful.* I knew."

"And you cooked for me *anyway?*"

"I did not cook for the critic," said Hiroshi Tanabe. "I cooked for the woman who ate my noodles with her eyes closed."

He set down the chopsticks on their rest. Then, because he was a man for whom great events could only be expressed in small adjustments, he turned the bowl, a quarter-turn, so that the handle of the spoon lay toward her right hand.

"Eat," he said. "Not to taste. To be *fed.*"

---

The old woman at the end of the counter had her hand upon her mouth. The historian had lowered the phone. Rin Hayashi, who had been a lifeguard for eight years and had pulled four swimmers from the Osaka surf, now found himself with a lump in the throat, and addressed it to the ceiling.

Mei took up the chopsticks. They were light, and made of some pale wood, and they were warm from his hand.

She lifted the noodles. She was astonished to find that she knew them; that her whole arm remembered the weight, the long smooth resistance, the little spring of them against the lips. They went into her mouth, and she tasted nothing, a hot softness, an oil, a coolness of onion that was only cold. She drank the broth. It was heat, and the memory of heat, and a faint richness upon the lip.

She closed her eyes.

She was a critic. She had been trained for a decade to name what she ate. And so she sat in the dark of her own eyelids, with the steam against her chin, and listened to a room full of people not speaking, and attempted for the first time in fourteen months to say not what was *in* the bowl, but what the bowl was *for.*

"It is warm," said Mei.

"Yes."

"It is the warmest thing I have been given in a year."

He did not answer. She heard the faint scrape of a ladle. When she opened her eyes, he was standing over her with the pot, and was, with a gentle, almost shy movement, filling the bowl again to the brim, as a man tops up the lamp of a vigil he has kept too long.

"Kaedama," said Hiroshi. "Second noodles. Free."

"Hiroshi."

"I have been told," said the cook, "I am too quiet."

He bowed. It was the smallest bow she had ever seen, no more than a lowering of the eyes, but the whole shop saw it, and the old woman at the end rose, and so did Rin, and so did the salarymen from the alley in their damp coats, and the girls in rain-capes, and Daichi Ito, historian, who had begun to cry, and who had to be given a pickle.

Behind them all, from the corner, a high excited voice said, quite clearly: "*It worked! Look! Look at the picture! The ghost bowl! Eight thousand likes!*"

This was Daichi's young cousin, aged sixteen, who had been sitting very still beside the pickle-jar for an hour with her phone held steady, and who had, in the confusion of the great moment, taken an excellent photograph of an empty stool, a perfect bowl, and, in the foreground, out of focus and unmistakable, the back of a woman's head.

Nobody had heard her. Nobody ever does, at the beginning.

"Hush," said Hiroshi's mother, taking the phone away and putting it among the pickles. "Let her eat."
