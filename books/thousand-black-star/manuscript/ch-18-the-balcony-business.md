## Chapter 18
### The Balcony Business

> *"I write the replies to the letters left on the wall."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0516

---

### SCENE I
*Verona. The courtyard on Via Cappello, at seven on a February morning, before the tourists. Fog the colour of milk. A bronze statue with one shining breast, rubbed bright by a million hopeful hands. A wall papered thick with letters, chewing-gum, and small folded prayers.*

The balcony, if one is honest, is not very large.

This is the first thing Giulia Rossi tells the groups, in five languages, at ten o'clock, in the careful flat voice of a woman who has said it four thousand times. *It is a small stone balcony, added to a medieval house in the nineteen-thirties to improve the story. No lovers ever stood upon it. It is a very good piece of marketing.* She says this in English to the Americans, and in Italian to the Italians, who are offended. Then she lets them take photographs. Then, because she is not a monster, she shows them the wall.

At seven, however, there were no groups. There was only fog, and a coffee, and a basket on the wall beneath the arch, where the night's letters had been left by the faithful, the desperate and the drunk. They came from Tokyo, Lagos, Ohio, Lima, on hotel stationery, on napkins, on the backs of boarding passes, addressed to a girl who had never lived, and every one of them was answered.

"Forty-one," said Giulia, to the coffee. She was thirty-one, a small, sharp woman with ink on the heel of her hand and the expression of a person who has seen the worst of the human heart and put it in alphabetical order. "Forty-one overnight. It is the *fog.* Fog makes people honest. By March it will be seventy."

"You say that every year," said a voice from the little office behind the arch.

"It is true every year, Elena."

"Truth is a *habit,* child. It does not make it less so."

Elena Gallo, seventy-four, retired newsagent from Mantua and, for forty years, the senior secretary of the Letters, sat in a cardigan the colour of a tobacco field, with a pair of glasses on a chain and a brass kettle that she did not trust anyone else to fill. She had taught Giulia how to read a sentence for the thing beneath it, how to answer in a voice that is neither pity nor sermon, and how to say *I do not know,* which is the hardest sentence in any language. She had also, by never minding her own business, introduced eleven couples, of whom three had stayed together, which she considered a creditable average.

"Who is writing in tongues today?" said Elena.

"The cat lady."

"*Ah.*"

The cat lady was Francesca Costa, fifty, weaver, of Mantua, an anxious woman who drank tea at night and wrote every Tuesday. Her letters were seven pages long. They concerned her husband, her husband's cat, her husband's mother's cat, and the fundamental question of whether a woman could leave a man and keep the cats, or vice versa. She signed herself *Desperate in Mantua,* and had done so for six years, and had never once left.

*Dear Desperate,* Giulia wrote that morning, with a nib that scratched like a beetle, *You have asked me nine times whether you should leave your husband. You have asked me once whether you should leave his cat. I notice that you only seem alarmed about one of these. Sign the cat over to your sister. Then we shall discuss the husband. In the meantime—drink less tea after nine.*

"That is harsh," said Elena.

"It is the only medicine she has not tried."

She opened the next letter. It was on thick cream paper, expensive, and it smelled, faintly and unmistakably, of bay rum and hair pomade. The hand was large, looping, theatrical, a hand that had been taught as a child to sign autographs. It said:

> *To the Lady of the Balcony,*
>
> *I am a singer. I am loved by a thousand people on any given night, and I am afraid of every one of them. Each time I stand in the wings, my knees turn to ricotta. I have a great many scarves. I have a reputation. I am very handsome (I do not say this to boast; it is a medical fact, like a limp). I have sung for nine years in the finest houses in the north of Italy, and I have never once, in all that time, walked onto a stage without wishing to be sick.*
>
> *I am not what the posters say. How does one go on?*
>
> *— a Man Who Is Not What the Posters Say*

Giulia read it twice. She read everything twice; it was what she was for. And then she did a thing she had not done in six years of answering the wall: she took off her glasses, and held them in her hand, and looked at the fog.

"Elena."

"Mm."

"If a man writes that he is not what the posters say..."

"Then he is probably *exactly* what the posters say," said Elena Gallo, "and is afraid of it. It is the commonest thing in the world. I have a nephew like that. He is a Carabiniere."

"That is not an answer."

"That is a *diagnosis.* The answer is for you to write." She put the kettle on, and a small, grandmotherly, wicked light came into her face. "Write it well. The famous are the ones who most need to be told the truth, and are least likely to get it."

Giulia bent over the paper. She wrote for a long time. When she had finished, she read it over, and amended one line, and signed it with the only signature the Letters had ever had: *Juliet's Secretary.* Then she did something else. She added, below, in a smaller hand, four lines of verse, the first she had written since the age of nineteen, and which she would have denied under oath:

> *Take off your shoes. The floor is cold and real.
> Find one face in the third row. Sing to her.
> The thousand are a rumour; she is not.
> And be afraid. It is how true things feel.*

She sealed it. She put it in the post-basket. The envelope the letter had come in bore no name, only a printed flap that read *Posta Artisti, Teatro Filarmonico.* She looked at it only once, and then very hard, and then she poured the cold coffee out into the drain.

*Mark this, reader. She had addressed it to a man she had never met, and she had told him the truth, and the only person in all of Verona who did not know yet that she was falling for him was Giulia.*

### SCENE II
*The Teatro Filarmonico, Verona. A dressing-room with a mirror ringed in forty bulbs. Eleven scarves. A tin of throat lozenges. A man in a silk dressing-gown, declaiming to a reflection.*

Marco Bellini, thirty-four, the first tenor of the north of Italy, did not believe in superstitions, but believed in lucky socks, a lucky lozenge, a lucky mirror, the left-hand dressing-room and the colour yellow, and had once refused a role because it required a green cloak.

"I am *dying,*" he told his reflection.

"You are *shaving,*" said Enzo Marino, twenty-four, his dresser, a cousin from Salerno who had come north for the season with a suitcase, a guitar, and the ambition to be discovered as a baritone. "There is a difference."

"Not at my level. I am a man on the edge of a precipice with a razor. Look at this face. This is a *national asset.* And tonight it will be seen by eleven hundred people, and one critic, and a *woman in the third row with a hat like a cabbage* who has sat in that seat for thirty seasons and tuts."

"So don't look at the third row."

"I always look at the third row! The first two are for the donors, who smile with their teeth. The third row is where the real ones sit." He put down the razor. His voice, which a moment before had been a thing of golden bravura, had dropped suddenly to the register of a child. "Enzo."

"Mm?"

"My knees have gone."

"They have been gone all week."

"They have gone to *ricotta.*" He sat down heavily upon a velvet stool. In the mirror, forty bulbs lit a perfect, famous, slightly sweating face. "I wrote to the wall, Enzo. To the *letters.* Like a schoolgirl. I thought, *If anyone in Verona could tell me what to do, it would be a person who spends her life answering the unanswerable.* And I signed it with no name. It has been four days. I am a fool."

There was a knock at the door. A boy of fourteen, in the usher's red jacket, held out a small cream envelope with the pride of a messenger from the gods. "From the Via Cappello, Maestro. A courier brought it."

"A *courier*?"

"A woman on a bicycle."

Marco took the envelope. He held it quite still, at arm's length, as one holds a thing that may be alive. Then he tore it open with a thumb that, to his great surprise, was trembling, and read.

Enzo went to the far end of the dressing-room and began, tactfully, to rearrange the scarves.

The note was a page long. It said that fear was not a flaw in a performer but the receipt for having cared; that the thousand were an idea and the one was a person; that the only cure for ricotta in the knees was to take off one's shoes in the wings and stand for thirty seconds on the cold floor, like a human being, and then to walk on, and find a face, and sing to it. It said that *the vain are only the shy with good lighting.* It said, at the end, in four lines of verse, the thing that made Marco Bellini put the letter down on the dressing table and cover his mouth with his hand.

"Enzo. Who is she? *Juliet's Secretary.* Is she a nun? Is she eighty?" He seized his cousin by both shoulders. "I have never in my life been so *perceived.* I am going to marry this woman. I do not even know whether she has teeth."

"She might be a man."

"She is *not* a man. No man has ever written 'it is how true things feel.'"

"Forty minutes, Maestro," said a voice at the door.

He stood. He tied the silk tie. He put on the yellow scarf, and the lucky socks, and a look on his face that transformed him by degrees from a frightened boy into a prince, and at the stage-left curtain, in the dark, in the smell of dust and ropes and old paint, with the orchestra's tuning-A rising like a drawn breath, he did a thing which had never happened in the entire history of the Teatro Filarmonico.

He took off his shoes.

> *The floor is cold. It is a floor. I am
> A man who stands upon it. Eleven hundred
> Are out there, and are only a rumour.
> There is one face. The cabbage. Let it be the cabbage.
> God keep me from the donors and the knees.*

He walked on, in his socks, in the full gold light, and there was a murmur from the house that was the sound of eleven hundred people noticing his feet. And he looked to the third row, and found, under a vast felt hat in the shape and colour of a savoy cabbage, the face of a stout woman in her sixties, who held her programme as one holds a rifle, and who had come, he afterward learned, because her sister's ticket had gone to waste.

He sang *Che gelida manina* to the cabbage.

It is a famous aria. It is sung by every tenor in the world and murdered by half. He did not sing it as it was written, for it had never before been sung by a man who had just been told the truth. He sang it quietly, and plainly, with no scarf in the voice, to one astonished woman in a hat, and there is a thing that happens in an opera house on certain nights when the eleven hundred stop being a crowd and become one person, listening. By the end of the first verse the cabbage was weeping unreservedly into her programme. By the second, the whole of the third row was. By the last note, held a beat longer than any tenor has dared since the war, there was not a dry eye from the gallery to the pit, except the critic's, whose eyes were never dry, being a man of principle.

In the interval, the usher boy found Marco in the wings, still in his socks, with his hands over his face.

"Maestro," he said. "The lady in the cabbage hat says you are the best she has heard. She says it was not a *performance.* She says, 'He sang as though I were the only one.'"

"She *was,*" said Marco Bellini, and began, quietly and helplessly, to laugh.

That night, at a table in a café in Via Mazzini, with Enzo asleep across three chairs and the street shining with fog, he wrote a letter on a napkin. It said: *I did as you said. I sang to a lady in a cabbage. It was the best night of my life. Who ARE you?*

He sent it to the wall at dawn.

### SCENE III
*Mexico City. Colonia Doctores, at midday, in the dry bright cold of a February noon. A wall, and under the wall, in the shadow of a lorry, a young woman with a map.*

There were ninety-nine of them. She had counted.

Camila Reyes, twenty-five, law student, a person who made flashcards of her feelings, had pinned every one to a map of the city that covered her bedroom wall: ninety-nine murals, from Coyoacán to Iztapalapa, on bakeries and school-yards and the sides of tire shops, in a style she could have picked out blindfold. The colours were burning: marigold, cobalt, a deep Mexican pink. The subject never changed. It was a woman's face. She was laughing, or reading, or sleeping on a bus with her head against the glass; she was caught in the rain, with a hand lifted against it; she was thirty feet high, and she was always, unmistakably, the same woman, with a short chin and a slightly crooked nose and an eyebrow that rose on its own, like a question.

It was Camila's face. It had been her face for eight months.

"Señorita," said a voice.

She started. She was standing, with her camera, on the wrong side of a chain. Behind the chain stood a wall; on the wall, in fresh paint still tacky from the night, was number ninety-nine. The woman was holding a violin.

"This is private property," said the officer. He was a large, mustachioed man in a blue uniform, with a gold tooth and a bearing of the most dreadful self-satisfaction. "I am obliged to inform you that you are *loitering.*"

"I am an observer."

"You are loitering in an *artistic manner.*"

"It is a public mural. Article Six of the Constitution protects—"

"Alejandro Hernández," said the officer, drawing himself up, and then, with a flicker of apprehension, "Officer. Of the Colonia Doctores. Twenty-two years of service. I have never in my life *lost* an argument." He said this with such finality that it was clear he had never won one either. "I am arresting you. In a *friendly* way."

"For what?"

"I will think of something in the car."

---

It was, as Camila said later, the first time she had been arrested for her own face.

He drove her, in the patrol pickup, ten blocks, humming a bolero in a baritone of enormous sweetness; and in the course of them he struck a traffic cone, a tamale cart, and an unoccupied taxi, and apologised to none. The words *I am sorry* were as foreign to him as Finnish. He said, in each case, "That cone was *in the road,*" and drove on.

"You know who painted it," said Camila at last. It was not a question.

"Everyone knows. The boy with the paint." He stroked his moustache. "I do not, strictly, *give out* the address of a citizen. But I may *drive past* it. By accident. Slowly."

He drove past it slowly. It was an old tortillería in a back street behind a tire shop, with a roller shutter and a hand-painted sign that said *TORTILLAS — SE VENDE,* the last word crossed out, and above it, in a fresh, enormous, unfinished hand, a single marigold the size of a cartwheel. A woman with a hennaed bun sat in the doorway shelling beans into a bowl. This was Marisol García, forty-five, painter, who sold him his lime and half his colours and the whole of his gossip.

"*Ay, mija,*" said Marisol, looking up. She looked at Camila's face for a long, deliberate moment. Then she looked at the cartwheel marigold, and at the wall behind her, and back again. "*Ay, Dios.* Alejandro, you have *outdone* yourself."

"I have done nothing," said the officer, with deep dignity. "I merely drove."

"He's gone for white paint. He'll be an hour." Marisol put down her beans. "You are the law student? He told me. He said, 'There's a girl who has been to every wall. She stands for ten minutes. She goes away.' He saw you at the eighth." She smiled, the broad, wicked smile of an old friend who has been waiting for this since October. "Come in. Do not touch anything."

Inside, there was light.

It fell through high windows onto a floor littered with buckets, rags, a ladder, a great copper comal still black with ancient masa, and a tortilla press that now held, in its iron jaws, a bouquet of brushes. And on the walls—

She stood in the doorway and did not breathe.

On every inch of whitewashed brick, from the floor to the rafters, pinned, taped, drawn in charcoal and chalk and wax crayon, in pencil on butcher's paper and ink on the backs of receipts, was a woman's face. Hers. There were sketches of her on a bus; of her asleep at a library table with her chin on a law book; and one, in a corner, small and exact, of a girl of nineteen at the Metro, in the rain, with her umbrella blown inside out and an expression of the purest and most justified indignation. She had forgotten that day. He had not.

And beside each, in a small, quick, impatient hand, there was a note. *Her mouth when she is reading something she disagrees with. The way she holds her bag like a shield. The eyebrow.*

"He said it was for the *walls,*" said Marisol, softly, behind her. "He said a man has to paint something. He did not say it was for *her.* He is a very stupid boy."

Camila said nothing. She walked slowly down the long aisle between the studies, as one walks through a church, with her hands at her sides. She had made flashcards of her emotions for seven years, and she stood now in the middle of the old tortillería, in a cathedral of herself, and could not remember a single card.

"I walked to every wall," she said, to nobody. Her voice was barely a whisper. "I wanted to find the hand."

"And now you have found the whole *arm,*" said Marisol. "And a studio. And a hundred of the same nose."

"I am a law student," said Camila Reyes. A small, wet, magnificent smile had begun at the corner of her mouth. "I have been grievously *wronged.* Where is a pen?"

She took a legal pad from her bag. It was her best, the yellow one, with the margin ruled in red. She wrote on it for a long time in a firm, fast, upright hand, and when she had finished she tore it off and pinned it, with a dressmaker's pin from the tortilla press, in the very centre of the biggest wall.

*NOTICE OF CLAIM*
*Re: Unauthorised Use of a Likeness (×100, approx.)*
*Respondent: R. Ortega, painter, of no fixed sense of proportion.*
*The Claimant, C. Reyes, has examined the evidence and finds as follows: that the respondent has painted her without consent, in public, at scale, in marigold; that the likeness is, on the whole, flattering; that the nose is too crooked and she has never held a bag in that manner.*
*The Respondent is hereby cited to appear in person at the Café de Tacuba, Calle Tacuba 28, on Sunday at four in the afternoon, to answer the charge of having painted her better than she looks.*
*Failure to appear will be taken as an admission.*
*C. Reyes. (Unlicensed. Fierce.)*

"Is it a *binding* summons?" said Alejandro, from the doorway.

"It is *extremely* binding."

"Then I will serve it," said the officer, "should he *resist.*"

When Rafael Ortega came in, at ten past one, with a can of white on each hip and a streak of cobalt across his forehead, he found the studio in perfect order and a woman's yellow paper pinned in the middle of the wall. He was twenty-six, loud, impulsive and romantic, a man who had painted a face thirty feet tall on ninety-nine walls without once working out what he would say to it if it spoke. He read the notice from the top. Then he read it again. Then he read it a ninth time, with his lips moving, and put both cans very carefully down on the floor.

"Marisol," said Rafael, in a very small voice. "Who was here?"

"The law."

"The *law*?"

"A woman with a crooked nose," said Marisol García, shelling her beans with the dreadful serenity of one who has seen the end of a comedy before its second act, "and a *very* good handwriting."

He sat down on a bucket of white. He looked at the hundred faces on the wall, and at the one piece of yellow paper, and for the first time in his life, he could not think of what to paint.

*Sunday at four.* It was Thursday. He had never been so unprepared for a Sunday.
