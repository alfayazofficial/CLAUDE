## Chapter 31
### The Pizzaiolo's Duel

> *"I wrote that recipe for you to find."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0486

---

*Reader, you want the dots.*

You are not alone. The Keeper, who has the whole ledger open on her knees and the lamp turned low, has looked at the little pulsing grey ellipsis under the name of Ermis Larasati a good many times, and has never once been permitted to turn it into a sentence. What she can report is only this: that on the Saturday morning after that Friday, at a tea-stall in Mirpur, a boy of thirteen poured two spoons of sugar and one of condensed milk into a glass for a customer who had not, in six whole minutes, said a word, and who was whistling.

"Bhaiya," said Bunny, with the air of a physician arriving at a diagnosis, "that is not a Friday tune."

"It is a tune."

"It is a tune from the *future,*" said Bunny darkly, and went to see about the kettle.

And now the spring moves on, as springs do, with its hands full of other people's business. Let us go where it is warmest, and loudest, and where somebody is about to have the whole of Naples as an audience.

**Bucharest. A Thursday night in late April, 8:15.**

The number 783 bus had been rerouted. This was announced by its conductor, Ionuț Constantin, in a voice of such profound sorrow that eleven passengers got off at Piața Romană out of sympathy.

"A burst main," he said. "On the Calea Victoriei. We must go by way of Lipscani, and I regret to say that the only stops on that route are, one, a hospital, two, a church, and three, a restaurant. I would advise the restaurant."

"I live on the Calea Victoriei," said a man in a hat.

"Then you are in grave danger, sir. Out you get."

When the bus was empty save for one acrobat in a good shirt and one nurse in a green coat, and when the conductor, in his green scarf, had made a great business of counting the tickets that no one had bought, Andrei Popescu leaned across the aisle and said, in a low voice, "Is there a burst main?"

"No," said Ioana Dumitrescu.

"Do you know that?"

"I know a good deal about pipes. I am a nurse. If there were a burst main the whole of Calea Victoriei would be in the news and the men's shoes would be wet." She folded her hands on her handbag. "It is Roxana Ionescu, the librarian. She rang my father. My father rang the tailor's shop, the tailor's shop rang the circus, the circus rang Mihai, and Mihai rang Mr. Constantin. It has taken them eleven days to arrange a dinner for two people who have been walking in the same corridor for six weeks."

"It is very efficient."

"It is *Romania,*" said Ioana, with a dry smile, "where we arrange everything except the roads."

He laughed, and winced, and put a hand to his lower back. She noticed it. She said nothing. A good nurse has a second set of eyes in the back of her tact.

The restaurant was a *crama*, a cellar in the old merchants' quarter, down eleven worn steps from the cobbled lane: all vaults and whitewash and strings of dried peppers, with candles in wine bottles and a proprietress who was, by her own account, the third cousin of everyone present. On the table: a plate of *zacuscă* with black bread, a carafe of plum brandy that Andrei was forbidden to touch and touched, a bowl of tripe soup sharp with garlic and vinegar, and, to come, a platter of stuffed cabbage rolls the size of a baby's fist, with polenta and sour cream.

It was an odd dinner, in its way. Neither had ever been taught how to have one. She had spent six years on night shifts, learning to eat standing; he had spent twelve on a diet of bananas and fear. They looked at each other over the zacuscă for a long time, with the awkwardness of two people who have been formal with each other in a hospital corridor and must now be formal in front of a candle.

"I should say," said Andrei, "that I have been discharged with orders. No wire for six weeks. No lifting. No trampolines. A physiotherapist named Mrs. Radu has told me that if I so much as sneeze with enthusiasm, she will personally *return me to the ward.*"

"Mrs. Radu is formidable."

"She says that my core is a *rumour.*"

"It is a very rare thing for her to be wrong." Ioana tore a piece of bread. "What do you see, from up there?"

"From the wire?"

"Yes. I have wondered. I stand at the bottom of ladders at work, and I look up at the lamp. I have never been the one on the ladder."

He considered it. The candle shook in its bottle. Down at the end of the cellar, a very old man with an accordion had begun to play something slow and sad and Romanian that sounded like a fiddle remembering a better year.

"The tops of people's heads," said Andrei. "That is the first thing. You would be astonished what a head says when it does not know it is being watched. A man who has just been given bad news bows his neck, just so. A woman in love tilts. A child, always, looks straight up, with its mouth open, as if it were being offered something." He smiled. "And then there is the wire. It is not what you think. It is not fear. It is the only place in the world where I am not *explaining* myself. On the ground I am always saying, *I am fine, I am well, I can lift that.* On the wire, there is no room for the sentence."

"And when you fall?"

"There is a very short silence. And then, to my shame, a thought: *Ah. So this is the answer.*" He turned the glass in his fingers. "I do not think I am afraid to die, Nurse Dumitrescu. I think I am afraid of the *ground.* It is such a small place to be put."

She did not say a word for a moment.

"I am afraid of the opposite," said Ioana. "I am afraid that I will be good at the ground. That I will be so very reliable at it, and so useful, and so thorough, that I will not notice, until I am sixty, that I have never once gone up."

"Up where?"

"I do not know. Anywhere that was not a ward."

"You are in a cellar," said Andrei gravely. "It is a very good start. It is eleven steps *down,* but one must begin somewhere."

She laughed, at last; the plait came undone a little; the old man with the accordion, who had been listening shamelessly, struck up a waltz.

It was an excellent dinner. They ate everything. They said nothing of importance at all, which is, as the experienced know, the most important thing two people can do at a first dinner. At the end of it, she took his wrist, across the table, with the casual authority of one who has done it ten thousand times to ten thousand strangers, and held her two fingers on the pulse. He let her.

"Well?" said Andrei.

"It is arguing."

"With what?"

"With itself. It cannot decide whether to be frightened or glad." She released him. "A strong argument. I would not worry."

"Nurse Dumitrescu."

"Yes?"

"On the fourteenth of June there is a gala. At the Aurora. It is the end of the season. Mihai has made me a *low* wire; six feet from the ground, a sixteen-foot span, with a net, and a mat, and a man called Gigi to stand underneath with his arms out for no reason except the principle. It is not the wire. It is the idea of the wire. A very small, very polite wire." He swallowed. "I would like to walk it. Once. For anyone. I would like you to be there."

She looked at him for a long time.

"I will bring my bag," said Ioana.

**Johannesburg. A weeknight in Braamfontein, eleven minutes past one in the morning.**

(*A city of gold and glass and shadow, in the pitch dark. Load-shedding, Stage Six. A thousand windows out. In the sky, quite clear, a great many stars, which had not been seen over Johannesburg in years and seemed a little embarrassed by the attention.*)

On the ninth floor of an old tailoring factory that had been turned into a radio station, with a wind-up lamp on the desk and a battery pack in the mixing-board and a window full of stars, Naledi Khumalo, twenty-nine, host of *Late Light* on Nightjar FM, did the thing that no host has ever been trained to do. She put down her script.

"Good morning, Johannesburg," she said. "I should say good *night,* but I have been awake since yesterday. If you are listening to this, you are either a nurse, a night-watchman, a taxi driver, a very small baby, or a man in a car who is lost. I love you all. I will not be able to play you any music tonight, because the building has no power but the radio has a very brave little battery, and I am told that if I play a record, we shall die."

Behind the glass of the studio, in the half-dark, Mama Palesa Zulu, forty-four, goldsmith, who had made her fortune in gold and her station in love of noise, put down her cup of rooibos and said to her engineer: "She is going off the page."

"Yes, Mama."

"I told you she would. Do not stop her."

"I have not touched anything, Mama."

"*Good,*" said Palesa. "A woman who is going off the page needs only a microphone and a friend."

"Naledi means *star,*" said the voice on the radio, steady in the dark. "Did you know that? In my mother's language. And the funny thing is that I have never, in all my years, been permitted to see one. Tonight, for the first time, I can. I am looking at the Southern Cross right now over the roof of the Standard Bank. It is quite small. It is quite nice. I am going to tell you something I have never told you, and then I will go back to being professional.

"For fourteen months I have dedicated one song every night to a man I have never met."

In a flat above a dry-cleaner's on the other side of town, in the dark, sitting on the edge of his bed with a saxophone across his knees, Thabo Dlamini, thirty-one, went entirely still.

"I do not know his name," the voice went on, from a transistor radio on the sill. "That is not strictly true. I know it. I know it from the number he rings. But I have never *said* it, and I do not think I should, since he has never said mine. He is a saxophonist. Each night, at ten minutes to two, when the show ends, he plays me something down a telephone line, from wherever he is, for four minutes, and I put it on the air. I do not know what he looks like. I know how he breathes. I know the sound of a man who stops, just before the last note, to be afraid of it." Her voice dropped. "Tonight the line is down. The exchange is without power. So I thought I would tell you, tonight, what he does, and let you hear what it is to be given a song every night by a stranger who does not ask for anything. That is all. That is the show. Now I will tell you what time it is."

Thabo stood up.

There was no logic in it. He was a shy man. He was, as a matter of professional record, a very good saxophonist whose whole life had been arranged so as to avoid being looked at; he played in jazz clubs from the back of the stage, with his eyes closed, so that he need not meet anyone's; he had refused three offers of a recording because they involved a cover. He had a flat with one chair. He had not, in fourteen months, taken the number-nine taxi to the building in Braamfontein, or gone up to the ninth floor, or asked to see her, on a principle which he had not examined and which he believed to be *respect.*

He took the saxophone in its case. He did not take his coat.

"*Hawu,*" said Jabu Mokoena, twenty-nine, absent-minded shopkeeper and his oldest friend, in the stairwell, in a pyjama top, with his keys in his hand. "Where are you going? It is one in the morning, there are no robots, the traffic lights are out. I will drive."

"It is not necessary."

"I know it is not necessary. I will drive *anyway.* I have been waiting fourteen months for you to do something stupid, and I do not intend to miss it."

They crossed the dark city in a rattling white bakkie, through junctions where no light worked and the minibus taxis moved by pure telepathy; past a vendor at a corner who had set up a gas stove and a pot of mealie-meal for the night shift, and who waved; past the great dark towers, in which a few candles, one to a window, burned like the spaces between stars. Thabo sat with the case in his lap, and his heart in his teeth. He did not speak. He had never, in all his years, been so afraid.

At the door of the factory, with a paraffin lamp and a trestle table and a sizzling pan, a woman in a blue doek was frying vetkoek.

"Good morning," said Thandi Zulu, thirty, street vendor, who sold hot fat cakes to the night staff of the building and had done so, in all weathers, since she was nineteen. "A man with a saxophone at one in the morning. Either you are a musician or it is a very elaborate robbery, and the banks are all *closed.*"

"I am going up to the radio," said Thabo.

"Of course you are. It is a day for it." She tilted her head and looked at him, very hard, with the lamp under her chin, and her whole broad face changed. "Wait. *Wait.* I know you."

"I do not think so."

"Two winters ago. Winter. A Tuesday. I was selling in Eloff Street and I had not sold a thing and I had no bread at home, and a man came by in a long coat, with that very *case,* and bought a vetkoek, and gave me a hundred, and said *keep the change,* and I said *it is only eight rand,* and he said *then keep the bread.* And I did not thank him. I have been looking for you ever since."

"I do not remember."

"*Of course not,*" said Thandi, who had begun, without being asked, to wrap a vetkoek in newspaper. "You do not remember it because it was nothing. That is the thing about you people who give. It is nothing. I will remember it until I die." She pressed the hot parcel into his hands. "Now. Ngiyabonga. Thank you. There is polony in it. Go up and be a hero."

It was nine floors. There was no lift. He climbed them in the dark by the light of a phone and a small stub of vetkoek, with the saxophone case knocking at his hip, and Jabu panting behind him, saying, "Eish, I am a *shopkeeper.* My heart is for sitting." On the sixth, a guard with a torch said *where do you think you are going?* and Mama Palesa's voice, from the stairs above, said *Let him up. He is expected.* On the eighth, Thabo stopped, with his hand against the wall, and heard it.

Her voice. Not on a phone line, not through a transistor. Through a door, in the actual air. She was speaking quite low, to nobody, in the way people speak when they have forgotten they are on the radio.

"…and I think the sad thing about the night, listeners, is not that it is dark. It is that we all pretend, in the daytime, that the dark is not ours."

He went in.

---

It was a small glass room with a mixing-desk and a wind-up lamp and a high window full of southern stars. A woman in a grey jersey sat at the microphone with her chin on her hand and a face that he did not, for a moment, recognise, because he had imagined it so many times and wrongly. She was small. She was brown. She had a large mouth, quick eyes, and a scar through one eyebrow that no voice could have told him. She looked up, and she did not say anything at all.

Behind the glass, Mama Palesa put a hand over her own mouth.

"Is that the line?" said Naledi to the engineer, without turning her eyes.

"No, Naledi."

"It is not the *phone.*"

"No."

"Then who—" And she stopped. For he had opened the case.

He did not say a word. He had not planned anything; had he planned, he would have said something, and ruined it. He took out the old tenor saxophone, with its dented bell and its worn gold keys, and fitted the reed, and put the strap round his neck. The engineer, a young man of great presence of mind, put a live microphone in front of it. Naledi, with her hand on the fader, nodded. A red light, on the battery pack, came on.

He played.

It was an old tune, a slow tune, a tune from the township that his mother had hummed when she washed the floor. It was not a thing to show off. It was a breath, shaped. He played it in the dark with his eyes closed, as he always did, so that he need not see her; and the sound went out of the ninth floor into the transmitter, and from the transmitter over the dark roofs of Johannesburg: into the cab of a taxi stuck at a dead traffic light, where the driver stopped the engine; into a night nurse's kitchen in Hillbrow, where she turned off the stove; into a car park where a man sat crying at the wheel; into a shebeen in Soweto, where a hundred people stopped talking in the middle of an argument. It was only a man and a horn and four minutes. And when he came to the last note, the place where he always stopped, and was afraid, and went on, he opened his eyes.

She was standing quite close. She had taken off the headphones. She was crying, openly, as a child cries, with no regard for the microphone.

At that moment, with no warning, the city's power returned.

It came on all at once. The lights of the factory, the lights of the street, the windows of ten thousand rooms across the whole bright, wounded, magnificent city, the traffic robots flickering green, from the Brixton tower to the Carlton Centre, and a great enormous sound, rising off the roofs, from the people at their windows and in their cars and on the pavements, who had heard the last note in the dark and had, with the lights, begun to clap.

"You are taller than the radio," said Naledi.

"You are louder than the telephone," said Thabo.

"That is not what I—" She wiped her face with her sleeve. "That is *exactly* what you should have said."

"I did not plan it."

"I know." She smiled, very wide, and the scar through her eyebrow crinkled. "That is why it was the best song."

On the stairs behind him, Jabu Mokoena sat down on the top step with his hand on his chest, and said in a voice of pure content, to a vetkoek: "I have waited fourteen months. It was worth it. I might die. Let me die."

**Naples. A Sunday in the Piazza Bellini, three in the afternoon.**

(*The smell of woodsmoke, hot oil, orange blossom, and a thousand years of argument. Laundry hung across the lane like bunting. Above the rooftops, the flat lilac shoulder of Vesuvius, asleep, in the manner of a large dog that has been listening.*)

The feud between the pizzeria of Alessandro Greco and the pasticceria of Chiara Esposito was in its fourth year, and had, by this time, a constitution.

They stood at either end of a narrow lane off the Via dei Tribunali, in two shops facing each other like two old actresses on opposite sides of a stage. On the left, with a red-brick wood-oven and a smoking black chimney and a hand-painted sign that said **DA SASÀ — PIZZA DAL 1961,** was Alessandro: thirty-three, broad, handsome, a man who shouted everything, including *good morning,* and who made the best margherita in the quarter by common consent and by his own. On the right, with a window full of golden sfogliatelle and a pink neon sign in the form of a spoon, was Chiara: thirty-one, small, quick, a pastry chef with a mouth like a drawn knife and hands like a bird's, who could fold two hundred layers of dough in a minute, and who sold her babà in little boxes with the instruction, in capitals, **NO REFUNDS.**

The cause was famous. Everyone in the lane could recite it.

Two Easters ago, at the Festival of the Quarters, Alessandro Greco had won the Golden Peel, the highest honour in Neapolitan pizza, with a creation called *Pizza Immacolata:* a pizza whose crust, to the astonishment of all the judges, was crisp as a biscuit, buttery as a pastry, and scented with orange-flower and a trace of Sorrento lemon. It was unlike anything in the history of the city. It had been *tasted.* It had been *photographed.* And it had been, as every old woman in the lane would tell you, *Chiara Esposito's grandmother's pastiera dough,* which was a secret, which had never been written down, and which he had somehow obtained from a card in a bag of flour.

He had never said so. She had never said so. They had stopped speaking on the Monday. In four years, they had exchanged no words that were not insults, called through the open doors at six in the morning across forty feet of cobbles, in the grand old tradition of the town.

"*Pasta frolla,*" bellowed Alessandro, as he rolled out a dough, "*is for people who do not believe in fire!*"

"*Fire,*" replied Chiara, not looking up from a tray of sfogliatelle, "*is for people who cannot be patient!*"

"I am patient!"

"You were patient *once.* You waited three weeks to steal a recipe."

There was a gasp from the lane. A man selling cornetto charms stopped selling. A balcony across the street opened; an old woman with a bowl of peas leaned out; and a thin gossipy man named Paolo Ricci, thirty, choir director and the self-appointed town crier of the quarter, who had been waiting for this very moment with an accordion at his hip and a trumpet in his bag, stepped into the middle of the cobbles and raised both hands.

"Ladies and gentlemen!" cried Paolo. "Citizens of the lane! I have a proposal!"

"Go home, Paolo."

"It is Easter week! It is the Festival of the Quarters! And it has come to my attention, as a man of the arts, that there are in this lane two persons who have been shouting at each other for four years about a recipe. I say: let it be *settled.* A duel! Pizza against pastry! Sasà against the Spoon! Each shall make a single dish, in public, in the piazza, today, and it shall be judged, not by a jury of foreigners with clipboards, but by *the people.*"

"By whom?" said Alessandro.

"By *us,*" said the whole lane.

"Done!" said Alessandro, who shouted it.

"Done," said Chiara, who said it very quietly, and who went, for no reason that anyone could see, quite white.

---

By three o'clock the Piazza Bellini was a theatre. There were two long tables, two portable ovens, two tubs of dough, a rickety stage with a banner, and, in the middle, a referee: Matteo Conti, fifty-three, police officer, in his blue-and-white cap, holding a pizza cutter as a sceptre and a whistle as a threat.

"The rules," said Matteo, who was gossipy, and loved rules above all things because they gave him something to tell his wife. "One dish each. Forty minutes. No sabotage. No spitting. The judges are the nine persons whose names I draw from this hat: *Anna Bruno, seventy-eight, retired rickshaw-puller; Lucia Bruno, nine, schoolchild; Anna Costa, thirty-eight, street vendor; Paola Fontana, twenty-five, lifeguard…* Does anybody here know why there is a lifeguard?"

"We live by the sea," said Paola Fontana, with dignity.

"Then you are qualified. Begin!"

Alessandro worked like a thunderstorm. Dough flew. Flames roared. He slapped, stretched, spun; he cried out to the crowd; he dropped a ladleful of San Marzano onto a bare disc, and swept it into a spiral, and put a handful of fior di latte on it, and a fistful of basil, and slid it into the oven with a long peel and a cry like a man launching a ship. His brother-in-law, the bellows-man, sweated. Matteo Ricci, twenty-six, a welder and the brother of the pastry-chef, who had been drafted to check that the oven door did not fall off, stood with a wrench like a man in a church.

Chiara, at the other table, worked in silence.

It was the silence of a surgeon. She folded; she rolled; she brushed with butter; she dusted with sugar; and she made, to the bafflement of the crowd, not a babà, not a pastiera, not a sfogliatella, but a flat white disc of dough, and put it into the oven, and took it out, and covered it with something, and covered that with something else, and finally, with trembling fingers, laid across the top, in a pattern, a circle of Sorrento lemon peel, thin as a thread.

She carried it across the cobbles herself.

"Here," she said.

"What is this?"

"It is a pizza," said Chiara Esposito. "I made it with your dough."

There was a murmur in the crowd. It rose, and fell. Alessandro looked at it. It was flat, round, golden-brown, with a faintly blistered rim, smelling of orange-flower and wood-smoke and the very particular char that can only come from an oven at four hundred and eighty-five degrees; and on it, in place of tomato, a thin layer of sweet ricotta, scattered with candied orange, and a small bright field of lemon peel. It was perfect. It was a margherita in a dream.

"It is *my* dough," said Alessandro. "How did you know my—"

"I have been buying it from you under another name for three years," said Chiara. "The boy who comes to the back door on Thursdays. That is my cousin. I have studied your crust like a scripture." She lifted her chin. "I cannot stand you, Sasà. You are the loudest man in Naples. But you are the only person in the city who knows how much to *leave out.*"

He took a bite.

For a moment he forgot to shout. It was the first time in thirty-three years.

"Chiara," he said, hoarsely.

"Eat it."

"I made you wait four years."

"I know. I have counted every day." She lifted her eyes. They were very bright. "Now it is your turn."

He put down the pizza. He took off his apron. It was a big heavy white apron, with the name of his grandfather's pizzeria stitched on the bib, and he folded it with great care and laid it on the table, as one lays down a sword.

"I will not make a dish," said Alessandro Greco, and, to the stupefaction of the whole square, he said it softly. "I will lose the duel. It is not necessary to cook. I stole a recipe from you two Easters ago. It came in a bag of flour, with a card, in a hand that I knew, and I read it, and I thought: *this is the best thing I have ever seen.* And I did not send it back. I used it. I won a prize with it. And I have been ashamed, every day since, to the bottom of my boots. I am sorry." He swallowed. "I stole your recipe, and I am sorry."

There was an enormous silence. Even the pigeons stopped.

"You did not steal it," said Chiara.

"I did! It was in the flour!"

"It was in the flour," said Chiara, "because I put it there."

"By *mistake—*"

"On *purpose.*" Her voice cracked, and she let it. "It was a card, Sasà. It was a long card, and a very good recipe. Did you read it to the end?"

"I—" His face went slowly crimson. "I read the *dough.* I did not read the end."

"Of course you did not. Nobody ever does." She took from her pocket a small, soft, flour-dusted card, creased and worn with four years of carrying, and handed it to him. "Read it now."

He read it. His lips moved; they always did when he read. At the bottom, below the quantities, below the oven temperature, below *knead for eleven minutes until the dough remembers you,* in small, neat, rather angry pastry-chef's handwriting, was a final line.

*Serve to the one who finds this, and tell him the lemon should be from Sorrento. And tell him I have been waiting for him to ask.*

The whole piazza was quiet enough to hear a spoon fall.

"I wrote that recipe for you to find," said Chiara Esposito. "I did not know how else to say it. You do not listen when I *talk.* You only shout."

"I always shout," said Alessandro, in a very small voice.

"I know. It is the thing about you."

"I have been shouting for four years in the wrong direction."

"It was a little further to the left."

He laughed. It was a great helpless broken laugh, and it came out of him like water out of a cracked pipe, and the whole square roared with it, and Paolo Ricci, who had been gripping his accordion to his chest like a drowning man, began to play, with a tear running down his nose, something sentimental and sixty years out of date. In the third row, Anna Bruno, seventy-eight, retired rickshaw-puller and the keeper of every secret in the quarter, who had seen the card go into the flour and had watched it for four years like a woman watching a pot that would not boil, nodded once, very slowly, and said to the child beside her: "That is the first honest dough I have eaten since the war."

"Which war?" said Lucia Bruno, nine, severe.

"Any of them," said her great-aunt.

The vote was, by general agreement and without the necessity of a count, declared a draw. The referee blew his whistle, and wept into his cap; and Matteo Ricci, the welder, handed the oven door to his sister's rival, and said, with the gravity of a man giving away a bride: "You will not break it, I hope. It is a very good door."

"I have never broken anything of hers," said Alessandro.

"You stole a recipe."

"I did not steal it," said Alessandro, looking at her. "It was a gift."

"At last," said Chiara, "he has read to the end."

> *O love, you came to me in flour and fire,
> Disguised as theft, so I might dare to take you.
> I read you wrong for four whole years, and burned
> A thousand loaves of pride to learn your name.
> Now let the lane say what it likes; I'll shout it:
> There is no crust so sweet as what is given.
> There is no oven hot enough to match
> The sudden warmth of being, at last, forgiven.*

(This verse was recited in the Piazza Bellini by Paolo Ricci, with accordion, without permission, and to such general applause that he repeated it eleven times, and had to be removed from the stage by the police.)
