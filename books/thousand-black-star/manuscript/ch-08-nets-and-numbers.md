## Chapter 8
### Nets and Numbers

> *"My boat is failing and so am I."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0443

---

### SCENE I
*Bergen harbour, a Tuesday in late November, before dawn.*

In Bergen it rains two hundred and forty days of the year, and on the remaining one hundred and twenty it is merely considering it.

The Bergensers do not complain of this. To complain would be to admit that the weather had an opinion, and they prefer to think of it as a lodger: noisy, always behind with the rent, and, in the end, part of the household.

On a Tuesday in the last week of November, at ten minutes past seven, with the sun still some hours from its brief and grudging appearance, a man named Lars Eriksen stood on the deck of a boat and spoke to it as one speaks to an aged and expensive relation.

"You will start," said Lars.

The boat, whose name was *Ragnhild*, did not reply. She was eleven metres of tarred oak and old paint, built in the year his grandfather had been young and his grandfather's opinions had been newer. She had a wheelhouse the size of a confessional, a winch that groaned like a bad conscience, and a diesel engine that, in the spring, had begun to cough as a smoker coughs, politely at first and then without apology.

"I have been patient," said Lars.

The engine considered this, and coughed.

Lars Eriksen was thirty years old. He was tall and square and weathered beyond his years, with hands like folded oars and eyes the grey of the fjord at its least forthcoming. He was the third of his name to fish out of Bergen, and he would, it was generally agreed, be the last, since the *Ragnhild* owed the bank four hundred and twelve thousand kroner, the quota for his class of vessel had been trimmed twice in three years, and the sea, which had once been a sort of savings account, had become a sort of overdraft.

He was a man of very few words. These he spent as a poor man spends his coins, one at a time, with a look of faint regret afterward.

"Ikke bra," said a voice from the quay.

---

It was the voice of Kristian Strand, who was seventy-eight, and who had been for fifty-one years a waiter at the finest restaurant on the Bryggen wharf, and who had never, in his retirement, put down his napkin. It hung over his left forearm even now, in the rain, in the dark, at a fishing dock. In his right hand he carried two paper cups of coffee on an imaginary silver tray.

He had married Lars's mother late in both their lives, and had buried her six winters since, and in the grief of it had simply gone on being a father, in the way a clock goes on ticking after the room has emptied. Lars called him *Far*, which is father. Kristian called Lars *the young master*, which was a joke that had stopped being a joke in about the year two thousand and twelve.

"That engine," said Kristian, passing down a cup, "sounds like a man trying to remember a name."

"She will start."

"Of course she will start. Everything starts. It is the question of what happens next." He examined the *Ragnhild* with the cold eye of one who has seen ten thousand dishes sent back to the kitchen.

"You are carrying passengers today."

"The institute. The woman with the nets."

"The biologist." Kristian's eyebrows rose by about one millimetre, which in a waiter of his school was the equivalent of a flourish of trumpets. "The one who talks."

"She is not so bad."

"Lars Eriksen," said the old man. "That is the longest sentence you have said in praise of any human being since your confirmation."

"It was *four words*."

"In this house, young master," said Kristian, "four words is a sonnet."

### SCENE II
*The Fish Market, Bergen. Eleven o'clock. The light has arrived, in the manner of an inspector: late, grey, and with no intention of staying.*

There is a woman at the corner of the Fisketorget who sells umbrellas, and she is not popular.

Her name was Solveig Dahl. She was thirty-six, an umbrella maker from Ålesund, who had come south to Bergen on the theory that if one has to sell umbrellas it is wise to sell them in the one city on earth that requires them. Her stall carried a hand-lettered sign that said, in two languages, *UNBREAKABLE*.

"Excuse me," said a tourist, a large and aggrieved gentleman from Ohio, holding up a skeletal wreck that had, a few moments before, been an umbrella. "Your sign says unbreakable."

"Yes."

"It broke."

"It did not break," said Solveig. "The *wind* broke."

"The wind broke my umbrella."

"The wind," said Solveig, with the patience of a great teacher, "was not covered by the guarantee."

"Ma'am, I'd like a refund."

"I do not give refunds. I give *advice*. The advice is: do not stand in the wind."

The gentleman went away muttering. Solveig watched him go with a very faint and secret satisfaction, and turned to find that a tall young woman in a yellow oilskin had stopped at her stall, and was examining the stock with the frown of one who solves problems for a living.

This was Sigrid Haugen.

She was twenty-nine, a marine biologist at the Institute of Marine Research on the Nordnes peninsula, with the look of one who has had a great many ideas in the last ten minutes and is trying to finish all of them at once. Her hair, the colour of dark honey, was escaping from three separate clips. When she stood, she bounced very slightly, as though the ground were a little too slow for her.

"Is it really unbreakable?" said Sigrid.

"No."

"Then why does the sign—"

"A sign is not a *promise*," said Solveig. "A sign is a hope with a frame around it." She looked Sigrid up and down. "You are restless."

"I am *not*."

"Your left foot is going. You have been tapping it since you arrived." Solveig produced a small umbrella the colour of a ripe plum and held it out like a gift. "Take it. You cannot go through the world with a hood. A hood makes you look at your feet. An umbrella makes you look *up*, to see what is the matter."

"What would I owe you?"

"Two hundred and ten kroner."

"That is a great deal for a thing that is not unbreakable."

"I am saving money," said Solveig Dahl, and for a moment something very small and naked looked out of her face, and then went back inside, "to disappear."

Sigrid blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"I said, to *dis*count it. Two hundred, then." Solveig looked away at the grey water. "Take the umbrella."

Sigrid took the umbrella. She paid the two hundred. It was not until she was halfway back to the institute, and the rain had begun in earnest, that she opened it and found, stitched into the lining in tiny, furious, red thread, a line that Solveig Dahl had embroidered for no customer in particular, and perhaps for herself:

*Look up. It is only weather.*

Which is, as the reader may have noticed, the whole philosophy of the Norwegian people in six words, and the reason that they are, on the whole, rather cheerful.

---

At the institute, a thing was waiting on Sigrid's desk, and it was not an email.

The foundations that give money to scientists send their good news by electronic mail and their bad news in the post. A thick cream envelope, with a window and a crest, is the equivalent of a surgeon's face when he steps out of the theatre.

Sigrid sat down on her chair without taking off her oilskin. She did not tear the envelope; she was a scientist; she slit it with the tip of a pipette, as though the letter might have fingerprints.

*Dear Dr. Haugen,*

*We are delighted to confirm that the Foundation will continue to support work in your field. We regret, however, that the present grant for the coastal cod study cannot be renewed beyond the thirty-first of March. Funding for the successor programme has been allocated to the Arctic station at Ny-Ålesund, Svalbard, where a post of fourteen months is offered to the present holder of the grant, subject to acceptance by the fifteenth of January…*

She read the paragraph twice, as one reads a map one hopes to be wrong.

*Fourteen months.* In Ny-Ålesund, in the high Arctic, where the sun does not rise for four months of the year, and the nearest city is a thousand kilometres of black water away. A post of a lifetime. Everything she had ever wished for; everything she had spent her twenties writing grant applications to be offered; and, laid on a desk in the rain in a cream envelope, it had the exact weight of a stone.

*And if I take it,* thought Sigrid, who could do such sums in her head faster than she could control her feelings, *I will not be back for a year and a quarter. And if the programme grows, as such programmes do, I may not be back at all.*

"Sigrid."

She turned. A man stood in the door of her office, a slender, dry, dark-haired man in a surgeon's green scrubs with a raincoat over them, holding a sheet of paper in one hand and a coffee in the other. He did not enter. He did not speak again. He held up the paper.

The paper said, in black marker: **HOW ARE YOU?**

"Henrik," said Sigrid. "You are standing in my doorway with a sign."

He shrugged and held up a second sheet, which he had evidently prepared for the occasion: **I TRIED TO TELEPHONE. I CANNOT. I CAME IN PERSON.**

"It is three hundred metres from the hospital."

**IT IS A LONG WAY WHEN YOU ARE AFRAID.**

Henrik Strand was thirty-one, a surgeon of real skill, who had held human hearts in his hands and could not bring himself to lift a telephone to his ear and hear a voice come out of it. He had been her best friend since the age of nine. He wrote everything down.

She handed him the letter.

He read it standing, which is how a surgeon reads bad news. Then he took a third sheet from his pocket, uncapped his marker, and wrote, with his usual neat and merciless hand:

**YOU ARE GOING TO TAKE IT.**

"I have not decided."

**YOU ARE GOING TO TAKE IT. AND YOU ARE GOING TO WISH SOMEONE WOULD ASK YOU NOT TO.**

"Henrik—"

**WHO WOULD YOU LIKE TO ASK YOU?**

She opened her mouth. She closed it. A small unbidden image rose before her: grey eyes under a wet cap, a square jaw, a man who had said, in six months of weekly voyages, not four hundred words in all.

"No one," said Sigrid.

Henrik regarded her for a long moment. Then he wrote one more sheet, and put it carefully on her desk, and went away down the corridor, a figure in green, with his coffee going cold.

The sheet said: **YOUR LEFT FOOT IS GOING.**

### SCENE III
*The Byfjorden. Two in the afternoon. The sun, having made its inspection, has begun to leave. A fog is coming down the water like a slow white curtain at the end of a very long play.*

"One more station," said Sigrid. "Just one more. I need the dip at ninety fathoms, and then I swear on my mother's good name that we go home."

"Your mother's good name is not at risk," said Lars.

"That was a joke."

"I know. I am being *supportive.*"

She laughed. It was a surprised, delighted, startled laugh, the laugh of a person who has been told a joke by a wall. She sat on an upturned crate on the foredeck, in her yellow oilskin, with her plum umbrella furled between her knees (it was not raining, and she held it nonetheless, as a man will hold his hat in church), and wrote numbers in a little notebook with a pencil on a string.

They had done eleven stations. They had netted, tagged, measured, and returned to the sea some forty cod, each of them looking at her, as cod do, with an expression of profound moral surprise. They had spoken, between them, perhaps sixty words, of which Sigrid had supplied fifty-four.

It was the best day of her week. She knew it; she was a scientist, and had at last learned to count the things that were not on her list.

And then the engine coughed.

It coughed once, with a sound like an old man being asked to climb a stair. Lars, at the wheel, did not move. Sigrid, who was watching him, saw that his shoulders had gone very still, in the way a horse's will when it hears something the rider does not.

It coughed again.

And then the *Ragnhild* made a sound that was not a cough, but a long, descending, apologetic sigh, like a great beast lying down, and the engine, which had borne them across forty thousand miles of grey water, went silent.

The silence that followed was enormous. The water lapped. A gull remarked upon the situation and flew away. Somewhere behind them the fog slid over the city, which went out like a candle.

"Lars," said Sigrid.

"I know."

"Is it—"

"It is nothing." He turned the key. Nothing. He turned it again. A small, wretched click. "It is flooded. It will clear."

He went below.

For eleven minutes she listened to the sounds of a proud man talking to a machine: a clank, a murmur, a muttered Norwegian syllable of a kind not found in the hymnbook. She did not go down. She did not offer to help, which was the kindest thing she could have done, for a man who is ashamed will bear almost any burden but a witness.

*Reader, let us go below, since it is permitted, and since the lady cannot.*

The engine room of the *Ragnhild* was the size of a coffin and about as cheerful. Lars Eriksen knelt before the engine with a wrench in his hand and a lamp in his teeth. He took the lamp out. He spoke, and because he thought himself alone, he spoke in the only form a taciturn man will trust: in measure, slow and low, with the pauses where a man swallows.

> *Old girl, I have nursed you since the spring,
> And bought you parts I could not well afford,
> And lied to the bank, and lied to my own father,
> And told myself the sea would give me back
> The thing I put in. And she does not give.
> She takes. She is the one great creditor
> Who does not send a letter. Nor will I.
> I have not told a living soul how far
> Behind I am. I have not told the woman
> Who sits above me with her careful numbers,
> And laughs, and does not know she is the light
> Upon this deck. I would not tell her. No.
> What could a man like me say? That I fish
> For cod that are not there? That I am tired?
> That I would sooner drown than ask for help,
> And that the drowning is the cheaper course?
> Start, damn you. Start. If you are all I have,
> At least be something. Be a boat. Be warm.
> Be a thing that does what it was built to do,
> And I will be a man. We'll go together.*

The engine, which had been listening with every appearance of sympathy, did nothing at all.

Lars put his forehead against its cold green flank. He stayed there some time. Then he took a breath, and straightened his shoulders, and returned the lamp to his teeth.

*Mark this, reader.* He did not weep. There is a species of courage that consists wholly in carrying on with a wrench. It is not much admired in poems. It should be.

---

By the time he came up, the dark had come down.

The dark comes early in the north: a short grey afternoon, a long blue dusk, and then the black, with only the lights of the far shore laid out along the water like beads on a thread. They drifted in a pocket of clear air, with the whole city shining three miles off, and seven hundred feet of water beneath them.

Sigrid had turned on the cabin lamp. She had put the kettle on the little stove. She had found, in a locker, a bag of *skolebrød*, the sweet custard buns of Bergen, rather stale, and set two of them on a saucer, with that particular dignity people give to a meal when everything else has gone wrong.

"We could call," she said.

"No."

"The rescue boats are volunteers. They would come in half an hour. It is what they are *for*."

"We are not in danger."

"We are adrift in a fjord in the dark, Lars."

"We are in a *fjord*," he said. "That is not a danger. That is an address."

She looked at him. He sat on the locker opposite, in the glow of the lamp, with engine-grease on his cheek, holding a stale bun as though it were a document he had been asked to sign. He did not look at her. He looked, with great concentration, at the bun.

"It is the money," said Sigrid.

He said nothing.

"You do not want to call because the rescue society asks a donation, and the tow, and the engineer, and you do not have—" She stopped. "I am sorry. That was not mine to say."

"No," said Lars. "It was not."

"But it is true."

He was silent for a long time. The kettle began to mutter. Out on the black water something moved, a seal, perhaps, or the tide.

"Four hundred and twelve thousand," he said at last, to the bun. "It is what I owe. It is not so very much. My father had more, once. I think about the number at night, you understand. It has a shape."

"What shape?"

"A *hook*." He very nearly smiled. "It is a stupid thing to say."

"It is not."

"I have not said it to anyone."

"Then," said Sigrid, softly, "I will not either."

It was the most they had said to one another in six months. The kettle sang. She poured two cups of tea, and handed one across; and for a moment their fingers touched on the warm china, and withdrew, each with the air of a party that has been formally introduced.

And Sigrid Haugen, who could not keep a foot still, who had never in her life kept a secret for longer than a week, felt the cream envelope lie in her pocket like a sleeping coal. *Fourteen months. By the fifteenth of January.* She opened her mouth to tell him. She had always told everyone everything. It was a fault.

"Lars—"

"Yes?"

*Say it,* thought Sigrid. *Say: I have been offered the sun at the top of the world, and I do not want it, and I cannot say why.*

"Your tea is going cold," said Sigrid.

*Ah, reader.* Two people on a small boat in a large dark, each with a letter against the breast, and both of them, in the great Norwegian manner, discussing the temperature of tea. There is an art to missing the moment by exactly the width of a cup. The whole nation practises it daily.

### SCENE IV
*Half past nine. The lights of the rescue boat come over the water like two kind and slightly officious stars.*

They were found by a boat from the Sea Rescue, crewed by six volunteers in orange suits, among whom was a retired poet.

His name was Espen Lund, he was forty-nine, he wrote the weather-poems that were chalked each morning on the board outside the harbour café ("Today: wet, with rising hope"), and he served as a rescue volunteer for the excellent reason that it gave him something to rhyme. He leaned from the bow and caught the line Lars threw.

"Eriksen!" he called. "I have written a poem about you."

"I am not yet dead."

"It is a *preventive* poem. It is hard to rhyme with 'diesel'."

"Then throw the rope."

On the quay a small crowd had assembled, for in Bergen a breakdown is a public entertainment. Marit Strand was there, Kristian's daughter and Lars's friend since the cradle, a train guard of thirty-eight in a very good coat, who arrived in the middle of a sentence she had evidently been conducting alone for some time.

"—and I said to the stationmaster, a woman of my position cannot be told that the eight-fifteen is *philosophically late*. Lars! You have grease on your face, it is very *attractive*—who is this? Oh, you are the *biologist.* Lars says nothing but I can read it in his eyebrows. Is that a plum umbrella? How *sensible*—"

"Marit," said Lars.

"Yes?"

"Breathe."

"I *am* breathing, I am breathing *through* it, it is an art—"

And the young woman in the yellow oilskin, who had a letter in her pocket and a stale bun in her satchel and a left foot which, she noticed with surprise, had at last stopped tapping, looked once across the black water at a tall, silent man being hugged by a talking train guard, and thought, with a clarity that astonished her:

*I would like to be on that boat again on Tuesday.*

And then, a second later, with the dread of the scientist at the moment the data refuse to fit:

*And I have until the fifteenth of January.*
