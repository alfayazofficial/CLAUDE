## Chapter 52
### Dancer of Ubud

> *"I dance for the gods and I think of you."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0445

---

### SCENE I
*Ubud, Bali. The hour before dawn, on the last day of a long engagement. A temple courtyard, a split gate, a smell of frangipani and wet stone.*

There is a kind of dancer who dances for the audience, and a kind who dances for the gods, and it is the great secret of the island of Bali that the second kind is the more exacting critic and is never once impressed.

Putu Wirawan, twenty-eight, had been dancing for the second kind since he was six. At that hour, with the stars still out and the roosters beginning their wars across the rice terraces, he stood alone in the courtyard of the family temple in his practice sarong and a plain white headcloth, and he danced the warrior's dance, the *baris,* to no music at all but the dripping of the eaves.

It is hard dancing, reader. The knees are bent and the back is straight, the fingers quiver like a leaf in a draught, and the eyes, which you would think were for seeing, are thrown wide, flashing, with a ferocity that must be put on and taken off like a coat. He had practised it every dawn for twenty-two years. He had never yet been satisfied.

"You are late for the gods," said a voice behind him.

"The gods," said Putu, without moving his feet, "have been told I would be late. I sent a banten."

"They do not take bribes of rice."

"They have taken worse."

Dewi Anggraini, twenty-six, weaver, stood in the gate with a tray of morning offerings: little palm-leaf boats of flower, rice and incense, each no larger than a hand, laid with the habit of a lifetime. She had been awake since three. Her fingers were stained at the tips with the red-brown of the *mengkudu* root, and she had a loose thread of cotton in her hair that she had not noticed.

"Tomorrow," said Putu, "there will be three hundred people watching us be married."

"Today there are three. You, me, and that gecko."

"I am not afraid of the gecko."

"No," said Dewi, laying a small boat of flowers upon the temple step, "you are afraid of the three hundred. You, who dance in front of the god of the mountain and the god of the sea and every tourist from Düsseldorf. Putu." She straightened, and her calm face, which was famous in the village for being impossible to ruffle, went a little soft at the edges. "Do you remember what I wrote?"

"*My cousin has taught me patience; I am still learning.*"

"And what did you write?"

"I wrote that I danced for the gods and thought of you." He lowered his arms. "I should have written *only* of you. The gods had their turn."

"They are very patient."

"They have had to be," said Putu. "I took four years to ask."

Behind them, in the dark of the lane, something shuffled, and sneezed, and said in a loud, grave, wholly foreign voice, in the Javanese of Yogyakarta: "*Is this the temple? I was told the temple would have a sign.*"

"That," said Dewi, closing her eyes, "will be the delegation from my mother's side."

---

It was. It consisted of Bayu Saputra, fifty-three, a scribe of Yogyakarta, who wrote letters for those who could not, and who had been drafted by Dewi's grandmother as an escort on the grounds that he owned a good umbrella; of Dewi's grandmother herself, Eyang, eighty, upright as a lamp-post and about as likely to be moved; and of a large cage covered in a batik sarong, from which came, at intervals, a muffled sound of grievance.

"We took the night bus," said Bayu. "Then the ferry. Then another bus. Then a driver who said he knew the way, which is the most dangerous sentence in any language. Eyang has not slept. Neither has the bird."

"*TOO LATE!*" said the sarong.

"She is nervous," said Eyang.

"She is *always* nervous."

"She has a right. It is her first Hindu wedding." Eyang took Dewi's face in two dry hands and looked at it for a long time, as one looks at a finished cloth. "You are thin. You have been weaving all night."

"Eyang, I have to finish the wedding cloth."

"Then finish it." The old woman let go. "And Ermis lands this morning. She comes from the airport at noon, in a taxi, with a suitcase of Dutch cheese. She does not know what a wedding costs. She has been in the cold."

"*BEAUTIFUL!*" said Ayu, from the dark.

"She says it to everyone," said Bayu Saputra, "but a little more *firmly* at weddings. It is a thing I have observed. A wedding, I find, is only a long tea with a great many chairs."

---

Ermis Larasati, twenty-four, designer, resident for six months of the city of Rotterdam, came out of the arrivals hall at Ngurah Rai in a coat that was far too heavy, and was struck at once by the island: the wet flowered air, the smoke of incense, the sky the colour of a pearl, and the roar of ten thousand motorbikes.

She stood a moment with her suitcase and her heart. She had not expected it to feel so much like putting down a stone.

*In Rotterdam,* she thought, *I am four hours behind him. I have eaten my dinner at the hour of his tea, and called at the hour when my mind is only starting to work, and have never once complained, because there is no one in Rotterdam to hear.*

*And here,* thought Ermis, *I am two hours ahead.*

It was a very small thing. She smiled at it, standing in the exhaust, and was unreasonably happy for the length of a minute.

### SCENE II
*The same night. The family compound, a veranda, a screen, and a very long distance. Friday. Midnight, Bali; ten o'clock, Dhaka.*

The compound was at rest, as much as a Balinese compound before a wedding is ever at rest. Bamboo *penjor,* tall and bent like fishing rods against the stars, leaned over the lane; the offerings stood in rows on the wall; and from the pavilion at the back the muffled noise of a gamelan orchestra rehearsing its one piece, again, again, again, with the patience of the dead.

On the veranda, beneath a lamp haunted by moths, Ermis opened the laptop.

The window blinked. A flat in Mirpur came into being. There was a candle in it, as there so often was, though this time it was a mere ornament, for the electric light was on; there was a wall behind him with a cloth hung on it, indigo, patterned with a field of black dots.

"You are early," said Fayaz. "By nine seconds."

"I am *not* early. I am *ahead.*" She pulled the lamp nearer. "I am two hours ahead of you tonight. Do you see how I have grown?"

"You are two hours *taller*, at least. Where are you?"

"Bali. At my cousin's. There is a wedding. Do you hear that noise? That is a gong being hit in a pattern. It has been hit in the pattern since seven. I think it is going to be hit in the pattern at my funeral."

"It sounds beautiful."

"It is *very* beautiful," said Ermis, "at three in the afternoon, from a distance of one mile."

He laughed. There was a sound, behind him, of a cough through a wall, and a lighter sound of a girl asking in Bengali whether she might *please* have the room for physics, and he turned his head and said something short and kind and returned.

"Tithi?" said Ermis.

"Tithi. She has an exam on the fourth. She is going to come first in the district, and it will be unbearable."

"And your mother?"

"The new inhaler is better." He said it lightly; he had said it lightly for a long time; but there was a place behind his voice where, for the first time, it did not feel like a lid. "She walked to the end of the lane on Tuesday. She stopped four times. She told the neighbours it was to inspect the drains."

"And the other thing?"

"The other thing is a number." He looked away, at the wall. "It is a smaller number than it was. Not by a great deal. But it has begun, how shall I say, to *consider* going down."

Ermis looked at him through the small glass, from the middle of an island, from a great black distance, and was silent a moment.

"Your cloth," she said. "You hung it."

"It is the only thing on the wall that is not a certificate." He glanced behind him. "It has been there since the day it came. People ask what it is. Bunny says it is a map of all the houses in Bangladesh that have no electricity."

"Is that what you say?"

"I say it is a field of stars that cannot be seen," said Fayaz, "and that someone I know made it, and that I am being very careful about the nail."

There was a little pause. The gamelan in the back began its piece again, from the beginning.

"Fayaz."

"Yes."

"In Rotterdam," said Ermis, "I am four hours behind you. I did not tell you. I did not like it at all. It is as though I have been left a long way back in the corridor of the day, and you are at the other end of it, waving."

"I know. I have been doing the sums."

"And now I am ahead. And I wished to say it while I was." She turned her head, a little, so the lamp took her cheek. "It is a very small thing. But for tonight, I have a view of your tomorrow again. I have a view of it. It looks all right."

"Does it?"

"It looks like a Saturday," said Ermis. "With tea."

He did not speak for a moment. Then he said, very gravely, "I will see that it does."

At that instant a sound, great and sudden and not to be borne, issued from the other end of the veranda, as a tall, sleepless Javanese parrot, who had in all her days been permitted a view of no cloth but the one that covered her, tore a corner of her sarong aside and shouted, directly into the lens, from a distance of eighteen inches:

"***TOO LATE! BEAUTIFUL! TOO LATE!***"

"Ayu," said Fayaz, with deep respect, "good evening."

"She is *delighted,*" said Ermis. "She never talks to men. I have not heard her address a man since my uncle."

"What did your uncle do?"

"He lost an argument," said Ermis. "She learned it from him. All three sentences. It is the whole of her education."

### SCENE III
*Saturday. Four weddings, on the same day, in four countries. Watch the clocks: that is the whole of the joke.*

**Kochi, 9:00 a.m.**

Meera Menon, twenty-six, schoolteacher, stood in a cream-and-gold *kasavu* sari at the door of a small temple beside the backwaters, holding in her left hand a book, which she had been told, by three aunts and a priest, she was not to bring to her own wedding.

It was a small, brown, sea-stained volume of poems. It was the book she had bought every year for six years, and in which she had found, every year, on the same page, in the same careful hand, a poem that was for her.

And beside her, in a white *mundu* with a gold border, a bookseller of twenty-seven who had not stopped trembling since Tuesday, stood Arjun Nair, with the look of a man who has seen the hour.

"You brought the book," said Arjun.

"I did."

"They said not to."

"I am a schoolteacher. When a person says *not to*, I put it on the board for the whole class." She held it out. "Read it. The one you hid. Now. In front of everybody."

"Meera—"

"*Now,* Arjun."

So the shy bookseller of Kochi, in the presence of two hundred relatives, a brass lamp, a drummer, a nadaswaram player and his own father (who was seventy-six and had not been told), opened the book and read the poem aloud, in a voice that began as a whisper and rose, line by line, like water in a lock, till it reached the beams. Vishnu Pillai, her father, a retired train guard of theatrical disposition, had begun to weep before the second verse and had borrowed the handkerchief of a waiter, Lakshmi Panicker, who stood at his elbow with a stack of banana-leaf plates, trying to look like furniture.

"I did not want her to win," whispered Rajesh Kurup, the temple singer, a rival of other years, to the person next to him. "I thought it was a trick of the book. I was wrong. I will sing the *vazhi* myself."

And he did.

---

**Lusaka, 10:00 a.m.**

In the yard behind a house in the Kabwata quarter of Lusaka, beneath a mango tree, a schoolmaster of thirty-eight in his one good suit and a maize-seller of thirty-five in a blue *chitenge* of such magnificence that three women had to be seated to look at it were being introduced to each other, for the fifth time, by their families.

It is the Zambian custom, which is wise: the families must meet before the people do, as the river must meet the sea before the fish. It is called the *lobola,* and in its way it is a duel of courtesy. Joseph Tembo's uncles sat in a row on one side, with a basket of gifts, speaking with great solemnity. Grace Banda's aunts sat in a row on the other, with arms crossed, listening with a gravity so profound it was clearly enjoyment.

"The groom," said the senior aunt, "is a good man. He is a teacher. He has two children. He is a widower. We have enquired. His late wife's mother has said that he was kind to her. This is rare in a man, and rarer in a son-in-law."

"The bride," said Joseph's uncle, "is a woman of sense. She sells maize. She has a daughter. She is a widow. We have enquired. Her late husband's friends say that she gave him a good funeral. We have never heard so much said about so much grief, so quietly."

"We have a fair price in mind," said the aunt.

"We have a fair price in mind," said the uncle.

"It is *very* fair," said Loveness Zulu, thirty-eight, baker, who had brought the cake and could not be silenced, "so we should *all* eat."

Joseph, at the end of the row, sat very straight, with his hands on his knees and the two children, Chisomo and Mapalo, on either side, scrubbed and furious with importance. Across the yard sat Grace, beside her friend Thandiwe Mumba, fifty-one, a railway ticket-inspector and a woman of no sentiment whatever, who had once told Grace that romance was a thing invented by people who sold greetings cards.

"I am still not sure I believe in it," murmured Thandiwe now, into Grace's ear.

"I know."

"But I believe in *him.*" She nodded across the yard, to where the schoolmaster, with immense solemnity, had begun to straighten a child's collar. "I have watched him with that boy for two years. He is the kind of man who would apologise to a door. Marry him, you fool, before somebody sensible does."

Grace smiled. The aunts, at that moment, announced that the price had been agreed, and nobody present could say afterward, with any exactness, what it had been, except that it included maize.

---

**Naples, 12:00 noon.**

It cannot be told in the time. There were three hundred guests, eleven priests, a pizza oven carried on a cart into the cathedral square by the groom's cousins, and a tower of *sfogliatelle* so tall that the bride's cousins had to hold the corners.

It was an hour of pure Neapolitan insanity. The groom wept. The bride, who is the sort that does not, wept, to her enormous annoyance, into the first pizza of the wedding, which she bit into in the doorway of the church, in her veil, to find that it was her own recipe, to the grain, with a tiny cross of basil in the middle.

"You stole it," said Chiara.

"I *borrowed* it," said Alessandro, and the entire square, which knew the story, said it in chorus.

His sister, Anna Gallo, translator, who had married into Mantua and come down in a hat of tragic proportions, stood up at the long table to make a toast, and confessed to the assembled company that eleven months earlier, in the dead of night, she had found a letter on the shelf above the oven, an unsigned confession in her brother's hand, which he had meant to burn. "I did not let the fire have it," said Anna. "I put it in an envelope, and I posted it to an old woman in Cornwall who kept the secrets of strangers. I have been *waiting* to confess that. Alessandro, you owe me a finder's fee, in pizza."

"I *knew* it!" cried Chiara, to nobody.

---

**Ubud, 5:00 p.m.**

And at five o'clock, on an island at the far end of the clocks, the gamelan struck, and three hundred people fell silent, and the wedding began.

Mark this, reader: a Balinese wedding is not a ceremony but a landscape; one walks through it. The bridal pair were led out beneath a shade of gold, he in a dark *saput* and crown of frangipani, she in a gold-threaded *songket* that she had woven herself, with a pattern on the hem in black and white squares: the *poleng,* the cloth of the balance, which tells the god and the stranger that all things in the world come in two halves, and that neither is the enemy of the other.

Ermis stood at the back with her grandmother and a caged parrot and was weeping in a manner she had arranged to look like hay fever.

They went round the fire. They touched the coconuts. The priest, a very old man with a voice like a drum, sprinkled holy water and chanted in an ancient tongue that not one in ten present could construe, and everyone present knew perfectly what it meant. And then, when the blessings were done, and the ritual had ended, and the guests had risen to eat, the bridegroom, who had never in his life been persuaded to dance for himself, stepped to the centre of the courtyard in his gold and his white and stood before his wife, and spoke, in the plain Balinese of the kitchen, the only vow he had written:

> *Four years I carried you in my two hands,
> As men carry water, not daring to spill.
> The gods have had my mornings. They have had
> My knees, my breath, my best and truest fear.
> Tonight I give the gods a holiday.
> The dancing now is yours, and so am I.*

And then, with no music, he began to dance.

It was not the warrior's dance. It was another, one that he had not shown to anyone, a slow circling with the hands, in which the fingers open like petals and the eyes, which in the *baris* are weapons, are for once simply lowered.

It was a dance for a woman, and he danced it to the gods, and the gods, for the first time in twenty-two years, were pleased.

Dewi stood quite still. She was watching his hands.

"That," said Eyang, in a whisper that could be heard in the next village, "is the first thing I have seen in Bali I understand."

"*BEAUTIFUL!*" said Ayu, with a clarity that cut through the gamelan like a blade through silk. "*TOO LATE!*"

"It is not too late," said Ermis, wiping her face with the back of her hand. "It is exactly on time."

She did not say what she meant. She was looking at her own phone, which lay dark in her palm, and at a field of black stars on a wall, in a flat, in a distant city, two hours behind her, in a Saturday with tea.
