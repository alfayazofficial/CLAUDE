## Chapter 42
### The Throat-Singer's Winter

> *"I leave for Europe in spring. I have not told the horses."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0551

---

*Enter the Chorus, in a fur hat, breathing smoke.*

> Beneath the Sky that has no roof but Blue,
> Where winter shuts the grass beneath its glass,
> A man must choose what horse to save; and who
> Must choose between the hoofbeat and the pass.
> Draw close the felt. Put on the stove its dung.
> The tea is salt. The night is forty cold.
> And here a woman, with two notes, one tongue,
> Prepares to sing a story yet untold.

---

### SCENE I
*Ulaanbaatar. A concert hall behind the Square, in the last week of the Month of the Tiger, or the Rabbit, or whichever animal the weather had decided to be. A rehearsal. The radiators are working, which is a miracle, and the miracle is being resented.*

In a land where a person may live a lifetime without seeing a ferry, it is a matter of some wonder to find a woman who works as a ferry hand.

Munkhzul Bold, twenty-five, of Erdenet, was exactly this. In the summers she worked the little white boat that crossed Lake Khövsgöl with tourists in life jackets. In the winters the lake froze to a depth of four feet, the boat was put up on blocks like an old bull, and Munkhzul, having nothing to ferry, found herself ferrying everything else: instruments, rumours, other people's lunch, the long grievances of the Eternal Blue Sky Ensemble, whose assistant, driver, porter, tea-maker and general bearer of burdens she had become by a process nobody could reconstruct.

She had wanted to be a singer. She could sing exactly one note. It was an A, it could strip paint, and she held it every morning in the shower to the despair of the building.

"Oyun-*egch,*" she said now, lugging a horsehead fiddle in its felt case across the stage, "the Europe men have phoned. Again. They want the shoe sizes."

"Shoe sizes?"

"For the *costumes.* For the *photographs.* They say it is a *concept.*" She dumped the case on a chair. "Do you know what a concept is? It is when a European wants to see your feet before he hears your voice."

"Munkhzul."

"I am merely observing." She sniffed. "Twelve cities. Utrecht, Lyon, Ljubljana. I looked them up. They have fountains. A person can stand and *watch* water run for no reason. It is called 'cultural heritage.' Here we call it a leak."

At the centre of the stage a young woman sat with her eyes closed.

Oyun Dorj was twenty-seven, and she looked like her own instrument: long, dark, a little weathered, with a deep stillness in the neck. She was the finest *khöömei* singer of her generation, which is to say that she could do what the old herders call the *impossible* and the physicists call the *harmonic*: sing two notes at once, one drone from the floor of the belly, low as a quarry blasted in the next valley, and over it, impossibly, a high clear whistle, thin as a spider's thread, which the old people say is the voice of the wind itself, borrowed for the occasion.

She opened her eyes.

"Munkhzul. How many days till the plane?"

"Seventy-one."

"And how far is it to the Munkh camp, in Töv?"

"One day, in a jeep, in this weather. Two if Batmunkh drives." Munkhzul narrowed her eyes. "Why? Oh. *Oh.* You have not told him."

"I have not told the horses."

"Oyun, horses do not *read* the Register."

"No." Oyun's voice was very level. "They read the throat. If I go to them with Europe in my chest, they will hear it before I have drawn breath. They hear all of it. A horse is a very honest critic."

"Then sing something else."

"You cannot sing *something else* to an animal that can hear you thinking."

Munkhzul considered this, a rare gravity settling on her round face, and then said what is said by every fool of every age at the one moment when it is least expected:

"A horse does not need to be told that a person is leaving. It only needs to be told that the person is *coming back.* The first it can work out. The second it cannot. So tell it that, and be sure it is true."

She picked up the fiddle again and carried it away, humming her A.

Oyun sat quite still for a long time, and then she took out her phone and ordered a seat on the morning jeep.

---

### SCENE II
*Töv province. A white valley. Forty below by the thermometer on the jeep, which had been broken since November and gave this figure only to be polite.*

The winter had begun, as these winters do, with a lie.

It was a mild October. It was a mild November. In the first weeks of December the herders of the steppe began to say, *It will be a good year,* and by the end of that month the snow had come, and thawed a little, and frozen again, in a crust as thick and hard as iron, so that the grass lay under it like a drowned thing under a lake. The horses, who can paw through snow but not through ice, stood in the white field and starved, with the whole green summer buried one hand's breadth beneath their hooves.

They call it *dzud.* It is the old word for the winter that kills. It had killed, in this valley, ninety horses since the new year. The Munkh family had once had three hundred and forty.

Oyun saw the first carcass from the road. It lay in the snow twenty paces off, a bay mare, quite whole, frozen into the posture of a gallop, with her neck outstretched and her legs flung wide. It looked as though she had been running toward something so hard that the cold had decided to let her keep it.

"Do not look," said the driver, Batmunkh.

"I will look," said Oyun. "Someone must."

There were fourteen more before the ger.

Baatar Munkh was standing in the yard, in a heavy *deel* of faded blue, with his hands behind him and a face like a closed door in a good house. He was twenty-nine. He was a horse trainer, as his father and grandfather had been, with the best racing colts in the province, and a patient manner with animals and a very impatient one, so far, with himself. He saw the jeep and did not move. A man does not run toward what he has trained himself for three winters not to need.

"Oyun," he said.

"Baatar."

"You have come a long way."

"It is a short way," she said, "when one knows where it ends."

He blushed. She saw it, even in that light, even under the beaten bronze of his cheeks, and she thought, with that clear mystical directness which had made her at once famous and unmarried: *There it is. The thing the horses will hear.*

"Come in," said Baatar. "Mother is boiling milk. Father is praying. It is the same thing, in this house, in a winter like this."

---

### SCENE III
*The ger. Felt walls, a painted ring of roof-poles, the stove glowing with the dried-dung fire that makes the whole of Mongolia smell, to a native, like home. In the north, the family altar: a photograph of a lama, a small brass lamp, a dried white cheese on a dish. The old man by the door, turning a prayer wheel with fierce, steady devotion.*

They gave her tea, salted and milky, in a bowl so large she held it with two hands, and dried curds hard as bone, and a bowl of mutton broth that tasted of smoke and thyme and the far side of the world. Old Erdene Munkh blessed her in the old way, with a small, solemn bow, and, being seventy-three and the sort of man who has stopped being shy because it saves time, said, "You have come to take my son's heart to Europe."

"Father," said Baatar.

"I merely observe. She is a singer. A singer takes the heart and gives back the music. I have heard the music. It is a fair exchange."

"Father."

"I will go and feed the sheep," said Erdene, with great dignity, and did, though the sheep had been fed an hour ago and were eating in the manner of beasts who had learned the habits of the very poor.

They sat alone in the warm. The wind sang through the roof-ring on one long ghostly note, a perfect fifth, and Oyun, who could not help hearing it, listened to it for a while.

"Seventy horses left," said Baatar quietly. "Perhaps sixty by the thaw. I had a colt who would have won at Naadam this summer. A dark bay. I called him Night. He died last week, in my arms. I took him into this ger and kept him by the stove, and he lay down on the felt, and—" He stopped. "It does not matter."

"It matters."

"I am a horse trainer with no horses." His mouth did a small, bitter, ruined thing. "A poor man. Poorer by spring. I have a ger, a name, and a rope. That is what I have. And you have Utrecht."

"I have a *plane.*"

"It is the same thing."

"Baatar—"

"I have read your letter in the Register," said Baatar, to the stove. "I did not mean to. A friend sent it. *I leave for Europe in spring. I have not told the horses.*" He lifted his head. "I wrote one too. Do you know what it said?"

"I know," said Oyun.

"You read it."

"I read it the night they published. Number one hundred and thirty-nine. *Our herd is dying; I cannot ask you to stay.*" She set down the bowl. "That is a very stupid sentence, Baatar."

"I thought it a very honourable one."

"It is the same thing," said Oyun Dorj, "and I would rather you were stupid at me than honourable at a distance."

For the first time that night, he laughed: a short startled sound, the sound of a man to whom it had not occurred that the weather might change inside the house.

"Sing for me," he said. "Before the horses. Before the winter takes any more. I would like to hear it in this room, where it is warm, and where—" he glanced at the altar—"where there is someone to hear it who has been listening a long time."

So she sang.

She sang low first, the drone, that great dark shaping thing from the floor of the body, the sound of a mountain breathing. And out of it, as a star comes out of the black, rose the second voice, a thin bright whistle, unbelievably pure, which wandered high and came down and rose again, like a bird making up its mind about the sky. The old man at the door stopped turning his prayer wheel. The stove ticked. In the roof-ring the wind, which had been playing a fifth all night, was quite suddenly *silent,* as one stops talking when a better speaker comes in.

Then she sang words, in the old tongue, and I give them here as best the language can be given, which is badly:

> *Over the world is the Blue that has no end,
> And over the Blue, a golden peg is driven,
> And to the peg the whole bright sky is tied,
> The seven elders, and the herds of stars,
> Wheeling for ever round their one fixed post,
> As colts around the post their master set.
> And I am but a colt upon that line,
> And what I wander from I wander toward.
> The rope is long. It is not made to break.
> It only means: go far, and come to the post.*

When she stopped, nobody spoke. It was Erdene who broke it, from the doorway, in a thick voice.

"The Golden Peg," he said. "The North Star. Every child in this country knows it. All the stars go round it, and it does not move." He wiped his eyes with his sleeve. "A man could do worse, my son, than to be a peg."

"I am a peg," said Baatar hoarsely. "I am the one that cannot go."

"No," said Oyun. "You are the one to whom it is *tied.*"

The two of them looked at one another across the stove. A log fell. Somewhere in the dark yard a horse coughed, and another answered.

"Come," said Baatar, rising. "Come and meet the herd, before the night closes. They should know your voice. It will be, in their ears, the most beautiful thing of the winter."

### SCENE IV
*The horse line. Midnight, or near it. No moon: a new moon, the dark of it, which is the time of the year when the sky shows what is truly there. The stars, enormous, close, colder than a blade. Snow squeaks under the boot like a mouse.*

*Mark this, reader: breathe gently. We are going quietly.*

They stood at the long rope where the weakest of the animals were tethered close to the ger: thirty horses, in the starlight, shaggy and gaunt, their breath rising in plumes that hung in the cold like questions nobody has dared to answer. At the end of the line, loose, because he would not stay tied, stood the herd's old stallion, a grey with a white blaze and eyes like polished obsidian, whom Baatar had trained himself and who had never in nine years allowed any other human to touch him.

"That is Altai," said Baatar. "He leads them. If he is quiet, they are quiet."

Oyun walked up to him in the snow, unhurried. She did not hold out a hand. She stood a pace off, and breathed with him, long and slow, until their breath was one cloud.

Then she began to sing.

It was not a song for a concert hall. It was the oldest kind, the kind a woman sings to a mare who will not take her foal: low, nearly without words, a long, warm, patient drone with a very thin thread of whistle above it. The horses lifted their heads. One by one, along the whole line, thirty great dark shapes grew still and attentive, like a congregation recognising the hymn.

She sang to them of the pasture under the ice. She sang of the thaw. She sang that the grass was only sleeping and that there were roads under the snow, and that a person might go away and come back, as the stars go round the peg, and that nobody here was being abandoned.

Altai stepped forward and laid his great head against her shoulder.

Baatar stood in the snow with his hat in his hands. He had stopped feeling the cold. It may have been the only time in his life, he thought, that he had seen a thing and been entirely unafraid of it.

"Oyun," he said, when the song ended, and she turned. "Listen. Whatever the horses do. Whatever comes. I did not ask you to stay, because I am a poor man with a dying herd and nothing to offer you but a rope and a name; and because the sky is wide and you are a voice that belongs to all of it, and I would not shut it in a ger. But I want you to know, before the spring, before the plane, before any of it, that if you asked me whether I would follow you down every one of those twelve cities, to every fountain and every concert hall and every cold hotel room in Europe, I would—"

Behind them, up on the ridge, something moved.

It was not the wind. The wind had gone still hours ago. It was a long low dark shape against the stars, and then another, and then a sound came down the valley that every herder knows in his bones before his ears have named it: a high, thin, trembling note, like the whistle of her own voice turned inside out.

The old grey stallion lifted his head from her shoulder. His ears went flat.

"Wolves," said Baatar, very softly. "The iron winter brings them down."

The grey mare at the head of the line screamed.

It was a sound he had heard only twice in his life, and both times he had afterwards wished he were deaf. The thirty on the rope threw up their heads together and, like one animal with thirty throats, hauled back against the line; and the line, old and frozen and strained by a winter's tugging, snapped with a crack like a plucked string; and from the dark below the ridge the whole great herd, the sixty, the seventy, the last of the Munkh horses that had stood in the snow of the open valley all winter, came sweeping down toward them out of the night in a long dark river of hooves and steam and flying manes, and behind the ger the door burst open and the lamplight fell out across the snow in a yellow tongue, and in it the horses poured past, so close that Oyun could feel the heat of them on her face; and Altai gathered under her hand like a wave drawing back before it breaks; and Baatar caught her wrist and shouted her name, and she turned, and he said, in a voice that she could barely hear over the thunder of the herd:

"Oyun, I have been trying to say it since the autumn, and if the horses go, and if I never see them again, and if you go, and if—I would sell the last of them, and the ger, and the saddle off my own back, and my father's lamp, and I would walk behind you to Utrecht on my own two feet, if you would only let me tell you that I—"

*Here the page is torn.*

### SCENE V
*Ulaanbaatar. The concert hall behind the Square. Six days later. The radiators working, the miracle unresented, for there was nobody to resent it.*

I will not tell you what became of them. I do not know. Do not look at me so, reader. I have turned over every page I own.

I can tell you only what the hall looked like on the Thursday after.

The rehearsal room was empty. Twelve folding chairs stood in a ring round the stage, and a horsehead fiddle lay in its felt case where somebody had set it down, and on the central chair sat Munkhzul Bold, in her padded coat, with a thermos of tea gone cold at her feet and her telephone in both hands. It had not rung for six days. She looked at it as one looks at a sleeping animal that one has been told not to wake.

The ensemble's manager came in, grey with worry, and said what he had said every morning since Friday.

"Anything?"

"No."

"The radio says there were wolves in Töv. A great stampede. Three herds lost."

"I know."

"The jeep has not been heard from."

"I *know,* Mr. Ganbold."

"What shall we tell the Europeans?"

Munkhzul looked up from the telephone. She looked at the chairs. She looked at the fiddle. She was twenty-five, she could sing one note, she was a ferry hand in a country with no sea, and her round face, in that moment, had the terrible untroubled clarity of the simple.

"Tell them," she said, "the horses know the way home. It is only people who need roads."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only one in this building." She stood up, and put the telephone in her pocket, and picked up the fiddle, and held it, for a moment, against her chest, like a child. "I will go to Töv in the morning. I am used to carrying things. Somebody should carry *this.*"

She went to the window. Outside the Square lay white and still under a sky the colour of old iron, and in the north, hidden by day but never absent, the Golden Peg held the whole turning sky on its long rope, as it had for ten thousand winters, and did not tell.
