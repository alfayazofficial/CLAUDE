## Chapter 5
### The Pianist's Silence

> *"I wrote a waltz for you and never asked you to dance."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0234

---

In St Petersburg the first snow does not fall. It is *issued,* like an order, from a sky the colour of an old spoon, and the whole city, which has been waiting for it with the stoicism of a very long marriage, pulls up its collar and carries on.

It was issued that Thursday at four in the afternoon, upon the Fontanka, upon the bronze horses, upon the green roof of the Nevsky Chamber Theatre, which on this night, of all nights in its hundred and twenty years, was giving a gala.

"A *gala,*" said Sergei Orlov, with the reverent contempt of one who has to sew it. "Eleven hundred sequins on the soprano alone. She has asked for a train. I said, Madam, there is no room on the stage for a train; she said, then we shall move the stage."

"And have you?" said Dmitri Volkov, from the piano.

"I have moved the *soprano.*"

The theatre at that hour was a cavern of dust and half-light, full of ladders and the smell of size and varnish and hot lamps. In the pit, at a black Bechstein with a cracked lid and a candle-stub on the music-desk (against all regulations, and by common consent of the fire-marshal, who was a patron), sat the pianist, in the stiff dark jacket he had worn to every rehearsal for sixteen years, and he was running through the programme, which he had printed on a single sheet and pinned to the wall like a bill of indictment.

He was forty. He had a long grave face with a good deal of forehead in it, a pair of hands that looked as though they had been borrowed from a much taller man, and a manner of courtesy so rigid that it could have been used for scaffolding. He rose when women entered rooms. He apologised to doors. He had accompanied, in his time, four hundred performances of the repertory and one funeral, and had never once been heard to say what he thought of any of them.

"Number nine," said Sergei, reading the sheet over his shoulder, for he read everything over everybody's shoulder; it was a vice, and a devout one, for he said that God had given him two eyes so that one might guard the other. "*Waltz.* Composer: *Anonymous.* Dmitri Andreyevich, you cannot do this. A gala is for the *famous* dead. Nobody will know whose it is."

"That is the idea."

"It is a very strange idea."

"It is a very *short* waltz," said Dmitri, "and I should like it to be heard without being explained."

"And who is the dedicatee?"

"There is no dedicatee."

"I did not say *dedicatee,*" said Sergei Orlov, who was fifty-seven, a dressmaker, a deacon's cousin and a man of subtle instruments. "I said *who is it for?* A tune like that is never for *no one.* I have heard you at night, Dmitri. Every night when the house is dark you play it, twenty-four bars, very soft, as if you were arguing with a ghost. The whole fourth floor knows it. The boy who sweeps the stairs whistles it."

"Sergei."

"The ballet mistress hums it."

"*Sergei.*"

"Madam Petrova's students have been heard to *dance* to it," said Sergei, and then, with the instinct of a good tailor who knows exactly when to put down the shears, stopped.

Dmitri did not turn his head. He did not stiffen. He did not do any of the things that a man does when a nerve has been touched, because for sixteen years he had practised not doing them. He merely placed one hand flat on the closed lid of the Bechstein, as one steadies a horse, and said in his most perfectly courteous voice:

"The snow will be heavy by seven. I should like the stage-door lamp to be lit."

"It will be lit."

"Thank you."

"It is no trouble," said Sergei. And, thoughtfully, after a long moment, to the empty house: "It is *all* trouble."

---

Above the theatre, on the fourth floor, in a studio with a bare wooden floor and a long barre and a window from which one may see, on a clear night, the whole slow shining curve of the river, a woman of thirty-eight stood with her hand on a child's back.

"*Longer,*" said Anya Petrova. "The neck is a swan, not a *goose.* Yuri, are you listening? You are standing like a man waiting for a bus."

"I'm waiting for you to say stop, Anna Sergeyevna."

"I shall say stop when you have *earned* it."

The boy was twelve, thin as a blade of grass, with a cough he had had since October and a determination to conceal it that touched her more than she would allow. His name was Yuri Orlov. He was the best pupil in the school and the worst liar, and she did not mention the cough. She had her reasons. She was a woman who had been told by doctors, when she was thirty-two, that a knee is a hinge and a dancer's knee a hinge made of glass, and she had learned in the long winter afterward which truths to press and which to leave, like a sheet, folded.

She had been, for ten years, the first dancer of the Nevsky. They had written about her in four languages. They had compared her to a candle, to a bird, to a thing seen once in a childhood dream; and then one night in the second act of a ballet she had danced two hundred times, the left knee had gone out beneath her, quietly, like a lamp in the next room, and she had finished the act on one leg and sat down in the wings, and had not stood on a stage since.

That was six years ago.

She walked now with the slightest hitch, a faint hesitation upon the left foot as it landed, so small that strangers took it for a mannerism and friends for a courage. She had a bad temper, a good sister, and a flat above a theatre in which every night, after the last curtain, a man sat down at a piano in the dark and played twenty-four bars of waltz.

"It is a very *beautiful* waltz," said the boy shyly, and the sentence went through her like a fork.

"What waltz?"

"The one he plays. Upstairs. Downstairs. I do not know where. I hear it through the floor." He coloured. "Seryozha says it is yours."

"Seryozha is a *rumour* in a cardigan."

"But is it?"

"Plié," said Anya Petrova. "*Now.*"

---

She had known it, of course, from the first night.

She had known it as a woman knows her own footfall upon an empty stair. It had come up through the old planks of the studio, one night in the first winter after her fall, a small clear waltz in three-quarter time, very simple, very sad; twenty-four bars, and then silence; and then the same twenty-four again. At first she had thought it a student's piece. By the third night she had stopped doing her exercises and sat upon the floor with her back against the barre, in her stockinged feet, with her good leg tucked under her and the bad one stretched out, and counted.

In the seventh bar, upon the third beat, there was a rest. A small one. An eighth-note's silence, no more; a hitch, like a breath caught on a stair.

She had *limped* there.

Not on the stage, where she had never limped; in the corridor. In the back stair. She had gone up and down it every evening for six years, and somebody, lying in the dark beneath her, had listened, and had put it in the music.

She had told no one. Not Natalia, her sister, who kept an inn on Kronstadt and cried at weddings. Not Oleg Popov, the policeman, her oldest friend, a thin wry man who had stood guard over her pride for twenty years and had never once been asked to. She had gone on listening. She had gone on being too proud to knock on the door of a man who was too proud to knock on hers.

And on Monday of that week, a niece of Sergei's had put a telephone in her hands and said, "Anna Sergeyevna, you must look; it is the *Register*—" and she had looked.

*Entry 0234 — St Petersburg, Russia. "I wrote a waltz for you and never asked you to dance."*

And there, in the same column, on the other side of the night:

*Entry 0017 — St Petersburg, Russia. "I heard the waltz and knew it was mine."*

She had written that one herself. She had sat up on a winter night six months before, and had written it, and had posted it to an address on the English coast; and had never, until that Monday, understood that somewhere in the same city, on the same night, a man had been writing the other half of her sentence.

"You are not going," said her sister on the telephone, in the voice of an innkeeper who has lost a patience contest.

"I am not."

"They have sent you the card. *Madam Petrova is invited as guest of honour.* Anechka, you have refused six times. You are a woman of principle."

"I am a woman with a *knee.*"

"Then sit down in a box."

"I will not be looked at, Natasha. I will not be *observed* by eleven hundred people to see if I weep."

"Then do not be *in* the box." Natalia Lebedev, thirty-five, innkeeper, sister, had a quick brutal mind that she hid inside a soft round face. "Go to the wings. Go where you used to stand. Stand in the dark behind the black cloth, where nobody can see you and you can hear every note. And if you do not like it, you can go home. And if you do—" She stopped.

"If I do?"

"Then you shall have heard it," said Natalia, "without having to decide anything."

It was, Anya thought afterward, the wisest thing that had been said to her in six years, and she hated it with a fervour she kept for what was true.

---

By half past seven the Nevsky was full.

It was full to the roof: a sea of black and gold, of furs steaming in the warmth, of jewelled throats and old men with medals, and in the pit the musicians were tuning like birds in a dispute. Dr. Oleg Morozov, the chairman of the board, who was also, by one of those arrangements that history makes when it is bored, the theatre's dentist, rose upon the stage to deliver his speech. He had a toothache. He had had it for three days. Every sentence, therefore, came out with a certain compressed fury, like a man reading the Gospel through a hot coal.

"*Ladies and gentlemen!* One hundred and twenty years! A *great* theatre! A theatre of tradition! Of *courage!* Of—" he put a hand to his jaw "—*molars.*"

A rustle went through the house.

"Of *moral* fibre," said Dr. Morozov, recovering, and sat down, and was seen, in the dark, to be offered a clove by the soprano.

The gala went on. A tenor sang, and a girl of sixteen played Prokofiev with her whole face; a mezzo wept in an aria and was forgiven; a chorus of fifty old men from the Naval Choir sang a song about a river, and no one in the house, not the Chairman, nor the Minister's wife, nor the deaf man in the tenth row, was dry-eyed at the end. And in the wings, in a corner so black that the stage-hands had learned never to look at it, a woman in a plain grey dress, with one hand upon the cold brick wall and the other gripping her own wrist, stood and waited.

She had not been seen. Sergei, who saw everything, had seen it, and had put his thumb to his lips, and gone to hide in a basket of wigs.

At twenty to ten, the stage emptied. The lights fell to a single warm pool at the centre. And out of the dark at the left walked a man in a stiff jacket, with his long hands at his sides, who crossed to a piano, and bowed to the house with the rigid exactness of a mourner at a public funeral, and sat.

The programme said: *9. Waltz. Anonymous.*

Eleven hundred people coughed, rustled and settled. A woman in the third row said, in a whisper that carried: "Who is *he?*"

"The pianist," said her husband.

"But who is the *composer?*"

"Anonymous."

"I never heard of him."

"Nobody has," said the husband, with an air of finality, and was, for once in his marriage, entirely correct.

Dmitri Volkov raised his hands.

He did not look at the house. He looked, as he always did before the first note, at nothing, at a point a little above the music-desk, where, a pianist will tell you, the first note is waiting. And in the wings, in the dark, with her hand upon the wall, Anya Petrova heard him breathe in.

And the waltz began.

---

How shall the page say it? Music is the one confession that cannot be forged and cannot be translated. Let it be told, therefore, as the thing was done: plainly, and in three-quarter time.

It began in the left hand: a low, quiet, rocking figure, *one*, two, three, *one*, two, three, like a child being walked to sleep by a mother who is herself too tired to sleep. Over it, the right hand laid down a melody, small and clear, that went up a little way and came down again, as one goes up the stair of a house one has loved. It was not a grand waltz. It was not made for ballrooms. It was a waltz for a corridor: for a lamp at the end of a passage, and a person walking toward it who did not yet know that she was being watched.

And in the seventh bar, upon the third beat, the pianist let go his breath, and the music stopped.

One eighth-note. Barely more.

It was not a hesitation. It was an *attention.* It was the sound of a man standing at the foot of a stair, listening to a footstep that he has loved so long he knows each place where it falters, and *waiting for it,* as one waits for a friend who is always slightly late; holding the whole bar open like a door.

Then the melody went on.

In the wings, Anya Petrova made a sound.

It was not a sob. She would have been the first to say that she was not a woman who sobbed. It was a short, sharp, dry intake of breath, as of someone dipping their hand in cold water, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth, and stood perfectly still; and the whole of her face, which had been for six years a face she held, was for the first time unguarded in the dark.

> *So that is how it sounds, to be forgiven.
> The thing I limped upon, and hid, and cursed,
> He took and made a rest in. I had thought
> The hinge was broken. He has made it music.
> Oh, you have listened to me going home,
> Alone, up all those stairs, and said not one
> Word of it, not one word, in six long years,
> But set it in the middle of a waltz
> Where all the city hears and no one knows.
> I should be angry. I am proud, I know it;
> I am a woman with a temper, and a knee.
> I should go out and strike him for his courage.
> I cannot move. I cannot, for I am
> Dancing. Here, in the dark, against the wall,
> My foot is keeping time.*

It was true. She had not noticed. Under the long grey skirt, upon the bare boards of the wings, her left foot, her bad foot, had begun to move: a little, a very little, a small and obedient step, *one*, two, three, *one*, two, three: and upon the third beat of the seventh bar it paused, like a breath, and took the music up again.

The waltz ended as it had begun: quietly, with the left hand last, a single low chord that hung in the air like a held coat.

There was silence.

Eleven hundred people sat in it. They did not know why. A man in the front row, a retired admiral with a chestful of ribbons, was found afterward to have taken off his glasses and to be unable to find them. A woman in a green dress said, loudly, "*Well!*" and was hushed. And then somebody began to clap, a single pair of hands, high in the gods, and then the house.

Dmitri Volkov rose. He bowed, rigidly, to the dark. He did not look into the wings, because he did not know that anyone was in them. He turned, with his formal and perfect exactness, and walked off the opposite side, and the lights went up on an elderly baritone, who looked at the pianist's departing back with the deep and venomous envy of a man who has been following an act he cannot follow.

By the time the pianist had reached the stage-door, and stood there in the cold, with the snow on his shoulders and his long hands trembling, and the whole of the river dark beyond the lamp, the gala was nearly over; and the woman in the wings had gone.

---

"Dmitri Andreyevich," said a voice at his elbow.

It was the voice of a man who has been doing something he is ashamed of for some time and now wishes to be congratulated upon it. Sergei Orlov, in his shirt-sleeves and with a wig on his arm, was standing at the foot of the iron stair, holding a small cream envelope.

"This," said Sergei, "has been left for you."

"By whom?"

"By *nobody.*" He cleared his throat. "Under the stage-door lamp. In the snow. By a person with a slight hesitation on the left foot, who did not wait. And I should tell you, as an honest Christian, that I have read it, because it was folded, and an unfolded letter is an invitation, and I am a man of weak will."

"Sergei."

"I have *read it.* It is four lines. I want you to know, Dmitri Andreyevich, that I wept like a calf."

Dmitri took the envelope. His hands were not steady, and he disliked the fact, and looked at it. Then, with the tact of a man who had, for forty years, been apologising to doors, he drew out a single sheet, white as the snow, and unfolded it beneath the lamp.

There were four lines. They were written in a quick, proud, upright hand, with a pen pressed hard.

*Seventh bar. Third beat. I limp there.*
*How long have you been listening?*
*Six years is a very long time to hold a door.*
*— A.P.*

He read it.

He read it again.

The snow fell, issued by the sky, upon the bronze horses and the green roof and the long black river. A tram went by, in a rattle of yellow light. On the fourth floor, in the window of a studio above the theatre, a lamp, which had not been lit in half an hour, came on.

"Well?" said Sergei Orlov, in a hushed and sacred voice, with the wig clutched to his bosom like a lamb. "What will you do?"

And Dmitri Volkov, who had played four hundred performances and one funeral, and who had never once been heard to say what he thought, folded the letter very carefully along its crease, put it in the inner pocket of his jacket, over his heart, and said, with an expression that no one in the whole Nevsky Chamber Theatre had seen on his face in sixteen years:

"I think I shall need to go upstairs."

"*Now?*"

"It is only four flights."

"It is *six years,*" said Sergei. "But go. Go, go. Take the lamp."
