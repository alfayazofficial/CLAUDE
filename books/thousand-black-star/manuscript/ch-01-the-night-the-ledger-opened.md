# ACT I — THE LEDGER OPENS

## Chapter 1
### The Night the Ledger Opened

> *"I forgave you years ago and never said so."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0388

---

There is a house on the Cornish cliffs where the wind knocks at every window as though it, too, had a letter to deliver.

It is called Lantern House, and on the last night of her life it held three things worth the keeping: an old woman, a young one, and a thousand secrets in a locked oak cabinet that smelled of salt and cedar.

The old woman was Seraphine Wyndham-Rao. She was seventy-eight years old, born in Colombo under a monsoon sky, trained in London under a ceiling of lamps, and she had spent forty-five years learning that the heart is the only telescope that points both ways. She lay propped on four pillows beside a window that looked out upon nothing at all, for it was the dark of the moon, and the sea and the sky had agreed to be one colour.

The young woman was her grand-niece, Nadeesha Fernando, twenty-nine, a nurse from Galle, who had flown six thousand miles to hold a hand and had learned in four days what her profession had not taught her in seven years: that one can be perfectly competent and perfectly helpless in the same breath.

"You are doing it again," said Seraphine.

"Doing what, Aunty?"

"Counting my pulse as though it were a debt you hoped to settle." She smiled, and the smile cost her something. "Put down the wrist, child. It is not going anywhere that it has not been before."

Nadeesha put down the wrist. She did not put down the fear. Fear, like a stray cat, is not so easily dismissed once it has been fed.

"Tell me what is tonight," said Seraphine.

"The new moon."

"The new moon." The old woman closed her eyes. "The one night in the month when the sky withholds its lamp and shows you what is truly there. Do you know what an astronomer loves best, Nadeesha? Not the stars. The *dark between* them. For the dark is where the unseen things hold their court."

"Aunty, you should rest."

"I shall rest presently, and at length, and with no one to wake me." She opened her eyes, and they were bright and grey and absolutely clear. "Listen, for I will say this once, and then I shall be an inconvenience no longer."

She spoke then, not in prose, for the dying sometimes find their sentences fall into measure of their own accord, as water finds its level:

> *Beneath my bed, the cabinet; the key
> Lies in the lamp. A thousand letters keep
> Their silence there. I swore I would not share
> A single word while I had breath to guard it.
> I have no breath to guard it now. At midnight
> Unlock the doors. Let every letter speak.
> Let strangers read what strangers feared to say,
> And let them learn what I have learned too late:
> The word unspoken is no silent thing.
> It bends the course of every life it touches,
> As unseen stars bend light.*

"A thousand letters," said Nadeesha. "I thought—"

"You thought it was a few dozen love notes from sailors." Seraphine's laugh was a dry, small thing, like the rustle of a page. "A thousand people, child, over forty-five years. Each wrote me one letter, and each letter holds the one sentence its writer could never say to the person who needed to hear it. I numbered them in the order they came. I answered none. I kept every one." She paused. "The first, I confess, was not a stranger's."

"Whose was it?"

"A man's. A foolish, frightened, honourable man, who left me on a railway platform in nineteen seventy-seven because he was afraid he would love me too much." The grey eyes went somewhere very far. "He wrote it from a ship. I read it a hundred times. And then I thought: if one man has a sentence like that, how many more? And so I began a ledger. And so it became a life."

"And the thousandth?"

"Ah." Seraphine's hand moved, very slightly, toward her own breast. "The thousandth is mine. You will find it in the lamp with the key. You are not to read it until I am dead, and not aloud until the proper hour, and then only to the one it is for. He will know."

"Aunty—"

"There. I have been long enough with you. Go and open the doors."

---

She died at eleven minutes before midnight, which is to say she died exactly as she had lived: a little early for her own appointment, so as to be sure of finding a good seat.

Nadeesha sat with her for the length of one breath, and then another, and then a third, as though each might be given back. Then she wept, plainly and without shame, the way the very young and the very honest weep. Then she wiped her face with her sleeve, for she was a nurse, and nurses are trained to finish what the hour requires.

She found the key inside the lamp. She found the cabinet. She found, beneath the cabinet's loose board, a small laptop with a single file on its screen, and one button, and one line of text:

*PUBLISH THE REGISTER. — S.W.R.*

Behind her the window rattled. The clock on the mantel, which Seraphine had wound daily for forty years, said four minutes to midnight.

On the screen of a small laptop, in a house at the edge of England, a young woman put her trembling finger upon a key.

*Mark this, reader. A thousand doors, long locked, are about to open at once. And what comes through them is not wind.*

**Lisbon, 00:02.**

In the great grey sorting office by the river, a postwoman named Inês Carvalho was eating a custard tart over a tray of undelivered letters, which is a sin against both pastry and postage.

"They say it is the end of the world," said Pereira, the lamp-seller, who had no business in the sorting office at midnight and had come in, as he always did, to tell her that business was bad. "Not the whole world. Only the part where people keep quiet."

"What are you talking about, Pereira?"

"The Register." He held up a phone as though it were a gospel. "Some old English witch has died and given away a thousand secrets. All free. Confessions. *Love* confessions. There is a search bar, Inês. You type a city, and up come the sinners."

Inês, who read every address twice and every postcard three times (and had never, in nine years, been caught), took the phone out of his hand and typed, with the casual air of one who is merely checking the weather: *Lisbon*.

Eleven names appeared. Not names — numbers, and beneath each number a sentence.

The sixth said: **"I wrote to a woman whose face I have never seen."**

Her tart went cold in her hand.

For she, Inês Carvalho, twenty-seven, postwoman of Alfama, had delivered a letter exactly seven months ago, addressed in careful block capitals to a *Senhora, Rua da Saudade 14, Lisboa*, and the street did not exist, and she had kept it, and she had read it in the bath, and she had never been able to forget the second line.

*It is not much of a Register, Pereira,* she thought, *if it tells the truth.*

"Well?" said the lamp-seller. "What does it say?"

"It says," said Inês, closing the phone, "that the night is long, and the post is slow, and you should go home."

"That is not what it says. Your ears have gone red."

"My ears are cold."

"It is a warm night."

"Then my ears," said Inês with great dignity, "are *ambitious*."

**Dhaka, 06:05.** *(The same hour of the world, seen from the other side of it. The clocks, as we shall see, disagreed.)*

In a rented flat in Mirpur, where the electricity came and went like a temperamental relative, a young man sat before a laptop at the first grey of morning, with his sleeves rolled and his pride rolled up with them.

His name was Fayaz Rahman. He was twenty-six, and he looked like a man who had once been very sure of something and was now merely well-mannered about its absence. He had run a small software company for eighteen months, and the company had died as small things die: quietly, owing money. He had a father whose business had gone with it, a mother whose breathing had grown shallow, a sister named Tithi whose exams required fees, and two embassy letters, each beginning *We regret.*

He had, in short, a great many problems and no leisure in which to be unhappy about them. That, as he sometimes observed to the tea-stall boy at the corner, is how you know it is serious.

"Bhaiya," said Tithi, appearing in the doorway with a phone held out like a hot coal. "Look. There is a thing. Everyone is sharing it. *The Register of Black Stars.* You *wrote* to this, remember? Two winters ago. You said it was a stupid idea and then you did it at three in the morning."

"I never said that."

"You said it *and* you did it. Look. Your number. *Zero-seven-one-three.*"

He took the phone. The screen said, in plain white type on black:

**ENTRY 0713 — Dhaka, Bangladesh.**
***"I would tell you everything, if I were free to."***

He had written that. He remembered writing it, at a desk that was then a good desk, in a company that was then alive, to a person who was then a mere shape he hoped one day to meet. He had not expected it to be *read.* One does not write to the dark expecting an answer. It is, rather, a way of dropping a coin down a well and listening for the splash.

"It is embarrassing," said Fayaz.

"It is *poetry*," said Tithi.

"It is a sentence with a conditional."

"That is what poetry is."

He nearly smiled. Then his eyes, which were tired and honest, moved a little to the right, as eyes will, and fell upon the entry beside his own.

**ENTRY 0714 — Yogyakarta, Indonesia.**
***"I am an hour ahead of you, and I keep looking back."***

He read it. He read it again. A line, in a telescope of ten thousand words, can be the one that fits the lens.

Underneath, in the margin, in a small, steady, old-fashioned hand, someone had pencilled a note, and the note had been scanned along with the page:

*0713 posted 22:40, Dhaka. 0714 posted 23:41, Yogyakarta. One minute apart, by the stars. One hour, by the clocks. — S.W.R.*

"Bhaiya?" said Tithi. "Why are you looking at it like that?"

"No reason."

"You are *blushing*."

"The generator is on the wrong circuit."

"You do not have a generator."

"Then it is the *idea* of a generator," said Fayaz, with the dignity of a man in considerable trouble, "and I would be grateful if you went to get ready for college."

**Yogyakarta, 07:06.** *(An hour ahead. Always an hour ahead.)*

In a workshop that smelled of hot wax and indigo, with a copper canting in one hand and a half-drawn flower in the other, a young woman who had not been to bed was swearing at a parrot.

"Ayu," said Ermis Larasati, twenty-four, designer of batik and keeper of very large ambitions, "if you say it one more time I will give you to the cousins."

"*TOO LATE!*" said the parrot, which belonged to her grandmother, spoke only three sentences, and had learned them all from an argument. "*TOO LATE! TOO LATE! BEAUTIFUL!*"

"That is two sentences."

"*TOO LATE!*"

Ermis put down the canting. Her phone, propped against a jar of wax beads, was lit by a stream of messages from her cousin Dewi in Bali: *Have you seen it? THE REGISTER. Look at yours. Your number is next to somebody's!!!*

She had written to the old astronomer one night in her last year of design school, when she had been very tired and very sure that the thing she wanted most would be taken from her by the thing she wanted next. She had put a sentence in an envelope, because it was easier than putting it in a person, and sent it to an address in England that she had found on a foreign website. She had half-forgotten it.

Now she opened the link, and found her own sentence in the open air, and was a little annoyed at how much it sounded like truth.

*I am an hour ahead of you, and I keep looking back.*

She had meant a friend. She had meant a boy at university who had gone home to a city one time zone away, and she had never told him why she kept watching his lit window long after it went dark. It was a small sorrow, tidy as a pressed flower.

But that was not what she looked at now. She looked at the line on the *left* of her own.

*I would tell you everything, if I were free to.*

She read it aloud. The workshop was very quiet. Even the parrot, who had views on everything, said nothing.

"Dhaka," said Ermis. "Bangladesh." She looked at the clock above the door, which said 07:06. "That is an hour behind me."

She did the sum again, not because it was difficult, but because it was a pleasure to do.

Then, as sensible people do when something strange has happened, she took out a pencil and drew a small flower on the corner of the page, and wrote beneath it, in the Latin letters she used for clients and strangers:

*Hello, Dhaka. You are one hour behind. I am not sorry about it.*

She did not send it. Not yet. A message is a lamp, and one should know where one is hanging it.

"*BEAUTIFUL!*" said the parrot.

"Hush, Ayu."

"*TOO LATE!*"

"Is it?" said Ermis, to no one. And then, to the pale morning outside, and to the one window an hour behind it that seemed to hold her gaze: "Is it, though?"

**Cornwall, 00:00.**

At the stroke of midnight, a thousand doors opened.

They opened in Lisbon, where a postwoman sat very still over her cold tart. They opened in Accra, where a pharmacist and a taxi-fleet owner, who had not spoken in six years, each reached at the same moment for a phone. They opened in Osaka, in Cairo, in Bergen, in Kochi, in São Paulo, in Lagos, in Lyon, in Kraków, in a hundred other places where somebody was awake and ashamed.

They opened in a lighthouse in Galle, where a young man named Anil Perera watched a video call fail, and failed to know why he trembled.

They opened at dawn in a window in Dhaka, and an hour later, by the clocks, in a window in Yogyakarta: two mornings, one hour apart.

And on a cliff in Cornwall, in a house with the wind at its door, a grey-eyed old woman lay quite still, with a smile on her face that said she had never in her life been so completely on time.

*Margin note, in pencil, in the hand of S.W.R.:*

> *The thousand are not a thousand people. They are one people, sleeping in a thousand beds, dreaming the same dream of being known. Let them wake. Let them read. Let them, if they are brave, answer.*
>
> *— and if they are not brave, let them at least be kind.*
