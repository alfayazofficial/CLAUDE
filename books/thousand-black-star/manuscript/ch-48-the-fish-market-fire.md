## Chapter 48
### The Fish Market Fire

> *"I may have started the fire with one cigarette."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0316

---

### SCENE I
*Marseille. The quay of the Old Port at six in the morning, the day after the worst night in the history of the fish market. Smoke, steam, and ten thousand gulls, all of whom feel personally addressed.*

There is nothing so ridiculous, or so sad, as a fire in a fish market.

It is a place built entirely of ice and water. Every stall has its tank, every table its drain, every crate its little coffin of crushed cold. And yet, at ten to four in the morning, with the mistral blowing down the quay like a drunk with a grudge, the polystyrene boxes at the north wall had caught, and the whole thing had gone up in nine minutes, and the fire brigade, arriving to find the blaze put out chiefly by its own stock, had been obliged to stand in a lake of sea bass with their hoses idle.

Nobody was hurt. That was the miracle, and nobody in Marseille had time to mention it, being too busy telling everybody else what had caused the fire.

"Electrical," announced Mathieu Vidal, fifty-eight, librarian, who classified scandal as he classified books, by subject, author, and date, and who had been at the barrier since half past four with a notebook. "A short in the compressor of the number six freezer. I had it from the *fireman's cousin.*"

"It was the mistral," said a fishwife.

"It was the *Spanish*," said another. "Who were in bed. Which is exactly what they *wanted* us to think."

In the middle of the blackened aisle, in what had been, till midnight, the stall called *La Daurade Joyeuse,* stood Hugo Martin. He was thirty-five, built like a door, and he was laughing. He had been laughing since five o'clock. The stall was a skeleton of tin; the awning was a blackened sail; the family scales, three generations old, had melted into a small bronze puddle on the slab. And Hugo Martin, fish seller, loud enough to be heard from the Panier to the Prado, stood in the wet ash in rubber boots with his arms spread wide and announced, to a quay of staring people, that the first round of pastis was on the house.

"At six in the morning?" said a policeman.

"A fire is a *festival,* monsieur! Nobody died! The fish went out *gloriously!*"

There is a kind of laughter that is a shout, and a kind that is a shield. Only the second cannot be turned off. At the barrier at the end of the aisle, in the navy jersey and cap of the Old Port ferry, a woman stood very still with her hands in her pockets and did not laugh at all.

Camille Bernard was thirty-three, a ferry pilot, calm and dry as a biscuit. She had piloted the little boat that crosses the harbour from the Town Hall quay to the Place aux Huiles every day for nine years without once being rattled by weather, or tourists, or the Marseillais. She was not rattled now. She was stone. At ten to four that morning she had been standing at the north wall of the market, in the dark, waiting for the five o'clock crossing, with a cigarette between her fingers that she had told every person she knew, in March, she would never touch again.

She had not seen where it fell.

"Madame Bernard," said a young inspector, taking off his cap. "I understand you were on the quay."

"I am always on the quay. I drive a ferry."

"Did you see anything? Smell smoke?"

She thought of the red dot, small as a seed, going out over the dark water on the edge of the wind. She thought, *I looked. I looked at the water.*

"I don't recall," said Camille Bernard.

Behind her, with a hose coiled round one shoulder and a wrench in each pocket, came Colette Lefèvre, thirty-one, plumber, so patient that fishermen came to her to be taught to wait. She had been called in at dawn to inspect the water-mains and had spent the morning defending them from accusation.

"It was not the pipes," said Colette to the inspector, with immense gentleness. "A fire is only a pipe whose water has gone the wrong way. It wants to be somewhere. Tell me where it wanted to be, and I will tell you why it left." She turned, and her eyes, which were kind and slow, rested a moment on Camille's pale face, and she added, to nobody, in the voice of one remarking on the weather: "Funny how often it is a small thing. A washer. A spark. A bit of cold. A little cigarette."

Camille went the colour of the ash.

"What did you say?" said the inspector.

"Nothing," said Colette placidly. "I said it is going to rain."

### SCENE II
*The following night. The burnt market, under a clean sharp moon and a ribbon of police tape. The wind has dropped. The smell is smoke, salt and wet char. A single stall light, run off a car battery, shows a broad man sitting on an upturned crate.*

He had sent them all home. That was the mark of Hugo: at midnight he had given every fish-wife a hug and every fireman a bass, and told the crowd to go to bed, and then he had sat down alone in the ruin and stopped smiling.

He spoke to the dark, as the cheerful do, when nobody is in a position to be cheered:

> *They said I laughed. I did. The way you laugh
> When someone's handed you a thing too large
> To hold in just one pair of hands, so you
> Hold up the other side with joking. Father,
> This was your stall, and his, and his before.
> I took it with the scales; I took the name.
> I never learned to lose a thing. I learned
> To make the losing sound like a good story,
> And put the story on the slab, and sell it.
> Tonight I have no story. I have ash.
> If someone came now, quiet, with no joke,
> And sat beside me while the ash went cold,
> I think I'd tell them how it really was:
> I smiled so none should see me not know how.*

"I have brought coffee," said a voice at the tape.

He did not jump. He knew the voice; he had known it for nine years, across a stretch of green water, in every weather. She ducked under the ribbon and came across the wet floor with two paper cups, and put one into his hand, and sat down beside him on a second crate, not close, not far, as a ferry lies against a quay.

"It is terrible coffee," said Camille.

"It is the best I have had all day."

"That is not the praise you think."

He laughed, and it was a real laugh this time, small and cracked, and neither of them said anything for a while. A gull walked the length of the ruined slab and inspected the ash for unreasonable hopes. Across the black water the lights of Notre-Dame de la Garde burned on her hill, small and gold.

"You didn't laugh this morning," said Hugo.

"No."

"Everybody else did. They think I'm mad. I let them. It is easier."

"I know." She turned the cup in her hands. "That's why I came. I have been sitting in my kitchen since noon trying to think of a way to say it that was better than the way I'm going to say it, and there is none, so I will say it the poor way. Hugo, there is something I must tell you. About the night of the fire."

He went very still.

"I told you in March that I had stopped smoking," said Camille. "I said it on the quay. You were selling oysters. You said, *Good, a woman who can stop a thing can stop a boat,* and I was—I was so pleased, Hugo, I have never been so pleased by a thing a man said to me about boats." She breathed in. ### SCENE III
*Buenos Aires. La Boca, in the damp middle of July, which here is the middle of winter. A social club of corrugated tin painted the colours of a parrot's wing; a wooden floor worn pale by sixty years of shoes; and a hall full of people who have all agreed not to look at the dance floor.*

It was the night of the anniversary, and no one was dancing.

In Buenos Aires a milonga has laws more binding than any parliament's. The men sit on one side of the hall, the women on the other. A man who wishes to dance does not cross the floor. He looks. He catches the eye of the woman he wants, and nods, once, the smallest of nods, and if she nods back, he comes; and if she looks at her shoes, he has been refused, and nobody in the hall has seen a thing. It is called the *cabeceo.* It is the most merciful form of rejection ever devised by the human race.

Mateo Fernández, thirty-five, tango dancer, arrogant and graceful, had sat on the men's side since nine o'clock. For a year he had danced with nobody. He had danced alone in the dark of his flat with a chair, and at the corner of a rehearsal studio with a mirror, and once, in a moment he did not care to remember, with a coat-stand. But on the floor of a milonga, among the living, he had not taken a woman in his arms since the night his partner died.

"You are staring at the ceiling," said Ramiro Romero, sixty-seven, retired choir director, blunt as a brick and the evening's DJ, from the tiny booth behind the bar.

"I'm resting."

"You are resting with your whole *face.* She is over there. She has been looking at you since the second tanda." He put on the next record. "I choose the music for this room, Mateo, and I will tell you what I have chosen: three tangos and a waltz and a cortina. After the cortina, it is the last tanda. I have put in the one you used to dance to. And I have put it *last.*"

"You are cruel."

"I am a choirmaster. We believe in resolution."

On the opposite wall, in a black dress with a plain neckline and her hair drawn tight back, sat Valentina Cruz, thirty-three, tango teacher, proud and guarded, with her hands folded in her lap. She had taught him for a year. She had taught him to lead, in a studio where the sunlight came in at an angle, and she had hated every minute of it, and she had not missed a lesson. She did not look at him. She looked, instead, with great care, at an old woman in the corner who had begun, quite without hurry, to pour mate from a thermos into a gourd.

This was Agustina Benítez, fifty-four, a tea-seller from Rosario who had come three hours by bus because someone ought to. She was gentle, rather deaf, and believed the temperature of water to be the foundation of civilisation.

"It is too hot," said Agustina, to nobody, tasting. "Water at the boil is a *scream.* A person is not scalded into a conversation. A person is *coaxed.*" She held out the gourd to Valentina, without looking at her. "Take it."

"I don't want any, Señora."

"It is not for wanting. It is for holding. A woman who is holding something is a woman who does not have to hold *herself.*"

And then, from the bench beside Valentina, came a dry old voice. Julieta Romero, eighty, retired ferry hand, who had worked the river-boats of the Riachuelo for forty years and was said to have known every secret that ever crossed it, put a small brown hand over Valentina's.

"I took them across," said Julieta. "Your sister and him. Twice a week, when they were poor. They danced on the deck. Nobody asked them to; they did it as other people breathe." Her eyes, pale with age, did not leave the floor. "She told me on that boat, the last summer. She said, *Julieta, if I go first, make him dance.*"

Valentina did not move.

"I have not told you," said Julieta, "because I thought you hated him. And then I thought: she is jealous, and that is not hate; it is only love that has been left in the rain." She patted the hand. "I took the blame once for a thing you did. A broken window. You were seven. It is the only lie I have told in my life. I should like to tell a second, to say that I did not hear her say it. But I did, child. I did."

In the corner a girl of fourteen, small and suspicious, in school shoes, who had come in from the street to watch the dancers and had not been told to leave, said, quite clearly, in the hush: "Why doesn't one of them just *nod?*"

It was Lucrecia Medina, from the next block. Nobody answered her.

The cortina came on, a thin sweet curtain of music between one set and the next, and the floor, which had been empty, emptied still further.

Mateo stood. He did not walk. He lifted his eyes across thirty feet of polished wood, in front of the whole hall, and the DJ, and the old women, and the girl in school shoes, and a dead woman's ghost who had come, perhaps, in the mate-steam, and he nodded.

Valentina looked at her shoes.

For one long second the hall stopped breathing. Then Valentina Cruz, who had been taught from the age of six that a woman refused loses nothing, and a woman who accepts has surrendered something she can never get back, set down the mate-gourd, and raised her head, and nodded.

They met in the middle of the floor. They did not smile. He lifted his arm and she settled into it, the way a thing slips into the place it was always cut for, and the old record began, the one he used to dance to, a slow tango, with a bandoneon that breathed in and out; and they walked.

Mark this, reader: they did not dance *well.* That is the thing the Keeper would have you know. He was stiff. She was angry. Every step was a negotiation. For the first minute there were two of them and a ghost, and for the second the ghost was only a shape of warmth at the edge of the lamp; and by the third there was only a man and a woman crossing a floor, with the dead sister's name unspoken between them like a hand resting lightly on each of their backs. *Go,* it said. *Go on.*

The last tango ended. The cortina began. The floor, by every law of the milonga, cleared. The couples drifted away to their sides, and the men sat down and the women sat down, and Ramiro in his booth, with tears running down into his collar, put on the next tanda and did not need to.

Valentina and Mateo stood alone in the centre of the floor, in the cortina, which is the law of leaving, and did not leave. A man and a woman who stay on the floor after the curtain have declared a thing before the whole room. They stood there with her hand in his, and the music went on, thin and sweet and unfinished, and neither of them moved toward either wall.

"The girl is right," said Valentina, very quietly. "I should have nodded first."

"You are not supposed to nod first."

"I am a teacher. I have been breaking the rules for years." She looked up at him. "Mateo. Is this a thing that you can do?"

"I do not know," said Mateo. "Ask me after the next song."

"And the one after that?"

"And the one after that."

On the side wall, a girl of fourteen sniffed and said, with unconcealed superiority, "*Finally.*"

### SCENE IV
*New Delhi Railway Station. Platform Six. The tail of the monsoon, at twenty to six in the evening. Every train on the northern line has been halted by the flooding of the Yamuna, and the platform is crowded with the stranded, the sleeping, the praying, and a very great number of sweets.*

In nineteen years of driving trains, Ravi Chaudhary had never been late.

It was his only vanity. He was forty-one, a train driver, punctual and patient, able to tell a delay by the hum of the rails; and tonight, for the first time in all those years, he had nowhere to be. The Lucknow line was shut. The river had risen over the tracks at the bridge, and the 5:40 stood on Platform Six with its engine ticking as it cooled, and its driver, in his cap and his blue jacket, had stepped down from the cab and begun, for the first time in his life, to walk the whole length of the train.

At the far end of the platform, on the bench under the pillar, as she had been every evening for eleven years, sat Sunita Verma.

She was thirty-nine, a teacher, wry, resolute, with a cloth bag of exercise books on her knee and a tiffin carrier at her feet, and she did not look up as he came. She had learned, in eleven years, never to look up. It was a kind of discipline. The 5:40 left Platform Six every evening, and each time, as it passed her bench, the driver sounded his horn twice. It was not necessary. It was not in the regulations. It was a sentence in a language that had two words and no grammar.

"You have stopped," said Sunita.

"The signal is red."

"It was green for eleven years."

"It was," said Ravi gravely, "a very reliable signal."

On the next bench an old woman, eighty-eight, in a faded cardigan, with a camera round her neck older than most of the trains, lifted her head from her chin. This was Neha Mehra, retired photographer, who had been taking one photograph a day of Platform Six for sixty years, and who now, with the air of a connoisseur, raised the camera to her eye.

"Don't mind me," said Neha. "I am furniture."

Beside her, wedged between a family of nine and a man asleep on his luggage, sat a plump young woman from Jaipur surrounded by forty tin trays. Pooja Singh, thirty-four, a baker, generous, had been on her way to a wedding in Lucknow with three hundred jalebis, and had now, in the course of a six-hour wait, given away two hundred and ninety, including a number to a policeman, who had accepted them under protest.

"Take one!" said Pooja Singh, thrusting a tray at the driver. "The syrup is melting. A jalebi that is not eaten is a tragedy, and I have been very sad all day."

"I cannot. I am on duty."

"You are on a platform in a flood, sir. The only duty you have is to be *sweet.*"

He took one, a small gold spiral, dripping. Sunita took one too, because it would have been ridiculous not to, and the two of them stood and ate in silence, before a camera and a baker and an audience of nine.

"I have a question," said Sunita, at last, licking her thumb. "I have had it for eleven years."

"Ask it."

"Why twice?"

"Twice?"

"The horn. Two blasts. Why not one? Why not three?"

Ravi Chaudhary, driver of the 5:40 to Lucknow, took off his cap. He turned it round, once, in his hands.

"One is a warning," he said. "Three is an emergency. Two is—" He looked for the word and found only the true one. "Two is *hello.* It is what a man says when he cannot stop the train."

"And now that you have?"

"Now it is the wrong number," said Ravi. "I will have to think of another."

Sunita, who had spent eleven years teaching children that there is no such thing as an impossible sum, set down her bag. "Sit," she said. "The bench is long."

"I am in uniform."

"The bench," said Sunita Verma, "does not care."

He sat. A respectful two feet of wood lay between them, and a third of a jalebi, and eleven years of platform. On the far bench, Neha Mehra took her photograph, and was heard to say softly, "*That's* the one." The rain, which had never entirely stopped since July, began again, drumming upon the great arched roof of the station; and in a signal-box a mile away a man who had worked the points for forty years looked at a red light and, for reasons he could not have explained, decided to leave it red for a little longer.
