## Chapter 48
### The Fish Market Fire

> *"I may have started the fire with one cigarette."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0316

---

### SCENE I
*Marseille. The quay of the Old Port at six in the morning, the day after the worst night in the history of the fish market. Smoke, steam, and ten thousand gulls, all of whom feel personally addressed.*

There is nothing so ridiculous, or so sad, as a fire in a fish market.

It is a place built entirely of ice and water. Every stall has its tank, every table its drain, every crate its little coffin of crushed cold. And yet, at ten to four in the morning, with the mistral blowing down the quay like a drunk with a grudge, the polystyrene boxes at the north wall had caught, and the whole thing had gone up in nine minutes, and the fire brigade, arriving to find that it had been put out chiefly by its own stock, had been obliged to stand in a lake of sea bass with their hoses idle and their dignity at about knee level.

Nobody was hurt. That was the miracle, and the one thing that nobody in Marseille knew how to say, because every one of them was too busy telling everybody else what had caused it.

"Electrical," announced Mathieu Vidal, fifty-eight, librarian, who classified scandal as he classified books, by subject, author, and date, and who had been at the barrier since half past four with a notebook. "A short in the compressor of the number six freezer. I had it from the *fireman's cousin.*"

"It was the mistral," said a fishwife.

"It was the *Spanish*," said another.

"The Spanish were in bed."

"Which is exactly what they *wanted* us to think."

In the middle of the blackened aisle, in what had been, till midnight, the stall called *La Daurade Joyeuse,* stood Hugo Martin. He was thirty-five, built like a door, and he was laughing. He had been laughing since five o'clock. The stall was a skeleton of tin; the awning was a blackened sail; the family scales, three generations old, had melted into a small bronze puddle on the slab. And Hugo Martin, fish seller, boisterous, loyal, loud enough to be heard from the Panier to the Prado, stood in the wet ash in rubber boots with his arms spread wide and announced, to a quay of staring people, that the first round of pastis was on the house.

"At six in the morning?" said a policeman.

"A fire is a *festival,* monsieur! Nobody died! The fish got out!"

"The fish are *dead.*"

"Then they went out *gloriously!*"

There is a kind of laughter that is a shout, and a kind that is a shield. Only the second cannot be turned off. At the barrier at the end of the aisle, in the navy jersey and cap of the Old Port ferry, a woman stood very still with her hands in her pockets and did not laugh at all.

Camille Bernard was thirty-three, a ferry pilot, calm and dry as a biscuit, and she had piloted the little boat that crosses the harbour from the Town Hall to the Hôtel de Ville quay every day for nine years without once being rattled by weather, or tourists, or the Marseillais. She was not rattled now. She was stone. At ten to four that morning she had been standing at the north wall of the market, in the dark, waiting for the five o'clock crossing, with a cigarette between her fingers that she had told every person she knew, in March, she would never touch again.

She had not seen where it fell.

"Madame Bernard," said a young inspector, taking off his cap. "I understand you were on the quay."

"I am always on the quay. I drive a ferry."

"Did you see anything? Smell anything? Smoke?"

She thought of the red dot, small as a seed, going out over the dark water on the edge of the wind. She thought, *I looked. I looked at the water.*

"I don't recall," said Camille Bernard.

Behind her, with a hose coiled round one shoulder like a sea-serpent and a wrench in each pocket, came Colette Lefèvre, thirty-one, plumber, a woman so patient that fishermen came to her to be taught to wait. She had been called in at dawn to inspect the water-mains and had spent the morning defending them from accusation.

"It was not the pipes," said Colette to the inspector, with immense gentleness. "A fire is only a pipe whose water has gone the wrong way. It wants to be somewhere. Tell me where it wanted to be, and I will tell you why it left." She turned, and her eyes, which were kind and slow, rested a moment on Camille's pale face, and she added, to nobody, in the voice of one remarking on the weather: "Funny how often it is a small thing. A washer. A spark. A bit of cold. A little cigarette."

Camille went the colour of the ash.

"What did you say?" said the inspector.

"Nothing," said Colette placidly. "I said it is going to rain."

### SCENE II
*The same night. The burnt market, under a clean sharp moon and a ribbon of police tape. The wind has dropped. The smell is smoke, salt and wet char. A single stall light, run off a car battery, shows a broad man sitting on an upturned crate.*

He had sent them all home. That was the mark of Hugo: at midnight he had given every fish-wife a hug and every fireman a bass, and told the crowd to go to bed, and then he had sat down alone in the ruin and stopped smiling, like a man putting down a very heavy sack he has been carrying for a mile uphill.

He spoke to the dark, as the cheerful do, when nobody is in a position to be cheered:

> *They said I laughed. I did. The way you laugh
> When someone's handed you a thing too large
> To hold in just one pair of hands, so you
> Hold up the other side with joking. Father,
> This was your stall, and his, and his before.
> I took it with the scales; I took the name.
> I never learned to lose a thing. I learned
> To make the losing sound like a good story,
> And put the story on the slab, and sell it.
> Tonight I have no story. I have ash.
> If someone came now, quiet, with no joke,
> And sat beside me while the ash went cold,
> I think I'd tell them how it really was:
> I smiled so none should see me not know how.*

"I have brought coffee," said a voice at the tape.

He did not jump. He knew the voice; he had known it for nine years, across a stretch of green water, in every weather. She ducked under the ribbon and came across the wet floor with two paper cups, and put one into his hand, and sat down beside him on a second crate, not close, not far, as a ferry lies against a quay.

"It is terrible coffee," said Camille.

"It is the best I have had all day."

"That is not the praise you think."

He laughed, and it was a real laugh this time, small and cracked, and neither of them said anything for a while. A gull walked the length of the ruined slab and inspected the ash for unreasonable hopes. Across the black water the lights of Notre-Dame de la Garde burned on her hill, small and gold, like a stub of candle somebody had set on a very high shelf.

"You didn't laugh this morning," said Hugo.

"No."

"Everybody else did. They think I'm mad. I let them. It is easier."

"I know." She turned the cup in her hands. "That's why I came. I have been sitting in my kitchen since noon trying to think of a way to say it that was better than the way I'm going to say it, and there is none, so I will say it the poor way. Hugo, there is something I must tell you. About the night of the fire."

He went very still.

"I told you in March that I had stopped smoking," said Camille. "I said it on the quay. You were selling oysters. You said, *Good, a woman who can stop a thing can stop a boat,* and I was—I was so pleased, Hugo, I have never been so pleased by a thing a man said to me about boats." She breathed in. "I hadn't stopped. I had told everyone I had, because I wanted it to be true, and it is very easy to tell people things you want. On Tuesday night I was on the quay at ten to four. By your north wall. I was waiting for the first crossing. And I had one. It was the last one in the packet. I stood there in the wind, with my back to your stall, and I smoked it, and when the wind came round I—
