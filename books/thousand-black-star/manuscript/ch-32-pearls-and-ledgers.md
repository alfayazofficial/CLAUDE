## Chapter 32
### Pearls and Ledgers

> *"I test every pearl and I have never tested you."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0949

---

**Dubai. The thirty-first floor of a glass tower above the Creek. Eight in the morning.**

(*Below the window the old Creek went about its business as it had for a thousand years: abras crossing with their cargo of clerks, wooden dhows riding low with refrigerators and tyres and cooking oil for the far shores of the Gulf, a single heron, who did not care what a dirham was worth. Above the Creek, the new city rose in mirrored towers, and the sun came off them in knives.*)

Tariq Al-Rashid wore the best suit in Dubai, and it was, in the strictest sense, a lie.

It was charcoal, cut in Milan, with a lining of dove-grey silk and a faint hand-stitched buttonhole on the lapel; it fell from his shoulders as water falls from a rock. He was thirty-seven, a shipping broker of the old Creek school, polished, courteous, quick with figures, with a smile that opened doors in four languages and shut, very quietly, in a fifth. He had brokered the freight of half a million containers. He had never in his life been late for a vessel. And for fourteen months, behind that suit, in a locked drawer of a desk of Italian walnut, he had kept a stack of papers, each of which began with the same word: *Notice.*

It was a matter of a guarantee. A friend, an honourable man, an old schoolmate, had chartered a ship and a cargo and lost both to a storm and a bad insurer; and Tariq, who had signed the guarantee in a moment of friendship and a restaurant, had been left to pay. Four million, two hundred thousand dirhams. It was not the end of the world. It was only the end of every plan he had made for the next ten years. He had spoken of it to nobody. He had spoken of it, in fact, so little that the silence had acquired a shape, and a weight, and a smell, rather like the drawer.

"Sir," said a voice from the toilet, in Arabic with a Sharjah accent. "I am afraid I have found something."

"What is it, Saeed?"

Saeed Al-Balushi, fifty-eight, plumber, had been sent by the building to look at a leaking cistern in the executive washroom, and had been in it for an hour, speaking all the while to the pipes. He was an anxious man, with a face like a worried walnut and the habit of narrating his own anxiety to anyone who would stand still. He emerged now, wiping his hands on a rag, holding in the other hand a soggy bundle of papers the size of a small loaf.

"It was behind the tank. Wedged. It is the reason for the leak." He held it out as if it were a drowned cat. "I did not read it, sir. I want that understood. I have a rule, in forty years of plumbing: I never read anything I find in a pipe. It is the first law of the trade. But—*Ya Allah!* They are so *large.* The letters at the top. It says *FINAL NOTICE.* It says it in red. It says it eleven times."

"Thank you, Saeed."

"Is it a very big debt, sir?"

"It is a very small cistern," said Tariq, "and I am obliged to you."

"I loved your mother, you know," said the plumber, apropos of nothing, as he gathered his tools. "Before you were born. I mean, in the sense that I once mended her sink in 1983, and she gave me tea, and I thought: *there is a woman who has the correct opinion of water.* I would not say a word to anyone, sir. I want you to know that. I am a vault. I am a *grave.*"

"Thank you."

"Not a word," said Saeed, with great sincerity; and he went down in the lift, and out through the lobby, and across the Creek by abra, to a kiosk in Deira, where a man named Rashid Al-Harthy sold cold drinks, prepaid SIM cards, and, to the discerning, the whole inner life of the quarter.

By ten o'clock, as the reader will have foreseen, it was known at the kiosk. By eleven it was known to the old women of Al Rigga. By noon it had crossed the Creek, risen in the lifts, and reached the mother of the man in question, Mariam Al-Harthy, seventy-three, retired schoolteacher, gossip of the first magnitude and a woman who could keep a secret for exactly as long as it took to find a second person who would be moved by it; and by one o'clock a great storm was under way in a small flat in Satwa, with a telephone at its centre.

At twenty to two, a second call reached the thirty-first floor, from a woman with a calm, clear, humorous voice: Latifa Al-Habsi, forty-two, journalist in Sharjah and the oldest friend he had.

"Tariq. I have a thing to tell you and I am going to say it fast. There is a piece going out tomorrow in the *Gulf Maritime Review.* It has your name in it. It has the guarantee, the notices, the figure. A young man has been collecting them for a month. I stopped it for two days. I cannot stop it for three."

"Thank you, Latifa."

"You are not surprised."

"I have been surprised for fourteen months. I am tired of it."

"What will you do?"

He looked out of the window. Far below, in the Creek, an abra was crossing, with a clerk in a white kandura sitting very upright on the bench and a very small girl beside him, trailing a hand in the water.

"I will go to Muscat," said Tariq.

---

**Muscat. The souq of Mutrah, in the late afternoon.**

(*The labyrinth of Mutrah: carved lintels, frankincense smoke, lanterns of pierced brass, a thousand narrow lanes that smelled of silver and rosewater and the dust of the sea. The call to prayer from three mosques at once, rising and overlapping like a rope being plaited from three strands of one thread.*)

The shop of Al-Sayed Pearls had no sign. It had a heavy teak door, a brass knocker in the shape of a hand, and a single window in which, on a tray of black velvet, there lay, at that hour of the afternoon, one pearl.

It was not large. It was not perfect. It was the colour of a very old tooth: faintly pink, faintly gold, with a small flaw at the crown. It had been brought to the shop forty years before by a diver's widow, in a twist of cotton, in exchange for six months' rent. The grandfather of the present owner had looked at it for an hour and said, *It is not for sale.* It had lain in the window ever since.

Maryam Al-Sayed was thirty-three, and she sat behind a long counter of carved walnut with a jeweller's loupe in one eye and a pair of silver tweezers in her hand. She wore a long dress of dark green and a loose black scarf of fine cotton, and she had a face that was composed from the forehead down: straight brows, steady eyes, a mouth that did not waste itself. She was shrewd, formal, courteous to the point of hostility; she called her suppliers *ya sheikh* and her customers *madam,* and she could tell a Gulf natural pearl from a Japanese culture in the time it took to boil a kettle. She had tested nine thousand, one hundred and twelve pearls in the nine years since her grandfather's death. She had been wrong, she often said, never.

"They are not real, madam," said the old man at the other end of the counter.

"Which one?"

"All of them. A pearl that is bought by the dozen is a pearl that has been *cooked.*"

"I will be the judge of that," said Maryam. She took the first pearl from the tray, held it up to the lamp, and, in the unembarrassed manner of her trade, laid it gently against the front of her teeth and drew it across. A genuine pearl, as every dealer knows, is gritty. It grates, finely, like the smallest sand. A false one slides as glass slides. "This is real."

"I have said that they are not."

"You have said many things." She took up the second. "You are a gentleman, Sheikh Khalid, and a florist, and you have never in your life bought a pearl. You are here because you are my neighbour and you wish to be sociable."

"I am here," said Khalid Al-Mazrouei, sixty-five, dreamy, who ran the flower stall at the end of the lane and was never quite at home on the ground, "because there is a man coming. I saw him in the lane. He has a suit. He is carrying a briefcase, and the shape of his shoulders suggests a letter."

"I do not receive men in suits."

"You are about to receive this one." The florist sighed, and settled a bunch of jasmine on his knee. "I found a letter once, on your doorstep. Last summer. Sealed. It had your name on it, in a man's hand, very upright. I did not open it. I put it through your door."

"I did not receive a letter."

"Ah," said Khalid, serenely. "Then he sent another."

On the wall behind her, from a shadowed corner of the shop, came a slow dry *tick, tick, tick,* and a soft cough. This was her uncle, Majid Al-Hinai, fifty-six, clockmender, who had raised her after her parents' death, and who sat at a small bench at the back of the shop with a magnifier on his forehead and forty clocks in various stages of death around him. He did not look up. He was her chaperone, for the purposes of propriety, and her counsellor for the purposes of life, and he performed both offices principally by being, at all times, present, unobtrusive and extremely amused.

"A man in a suit," he said, to the clock he was mending. "It will be the broker from Dubai."

"Uncle."

"The one with the very good manners. The one who has called nine times about the shipment, each time with a different excuse for calling."

"It is an *important* shipment."

"It is a very *large* excuse." *Tick.* "I have sat in this corner for three weeks, and I have watched you take the loupe out of your eye every time he enters the lane. I am an old man. I mend clocks. I know the sound of a mainspring that has been wound too tight."

"I am not a mainspring."

"No," said Majid, mildly. "You are a pearl. Which is worse. A pearl cannot be unwound."

The teak door opened. The bell above it rang a short, bright note. And in the light of the lane, with the shoulders of a man who has been carrying something heavy for a very long way and is trying to look as though it were a briefcase, stood Tariq Al-Rashid.

---

It was a long afternoon, and a courteous one. This is the Gulf, and nobody in it has ever said a hard thing before the coffee.

She received him in the back room, which was the majlis of the shop: a long low room of carpets and cushions, with brass lamps and a window on a courtyard in which a lemon tree stood in a pot. Her uncle sat in the corner with his clocks, winding. An elderly man in a long white dishdasha and a small embroidered cap, who had come from Dubai that morning on the same flight and had been introduced as an old friend of the family, sat cross-legged on a cushion with his hands on his knees and an expression of immense contentment. This was Salim Al-Balushi, eighty-three, retired imam of a small mosque by the Creek, a man of theatrical piety and quiet mischief, who had, as it turned out, been the first to go to Tariq's mother's flat that afternoon and the first to be told. He had come, he said, to see the pearls. He had come, in fact, because he was eighty-three and wanted to see how a story ended.

The coffee was brought in a brass pot, thin and bitter and scented with cardamom, and poured into tiny handleless cups, with the right hand. Dates were passed. Greetings were exchanged for six whole minutes. *How are you; how is your family; how is the health; how is the work; praise be to God.* And then there was a pause, and the sea, somewhere, breathed.

"I should like to say," began Tariq.

"First," said Maryam, "I should like to ask Sheikh Salim to tell us about the pearl in the window."

The old man's face lit up like a lamp. "Ah! The pearl of the widow! I know that pearl. My father dived for it." He leaned forward, and his voice altered, grew deeper, the voice of a man who has told a story a thousand times and wishes to tell it a thousand and one. "He was a diver, ya Maryam, on a dhow out of Dubai, in the days before oil, when a man went down on a rope, with a stone at his feet and a clip of turtle-shell on his nose, for as long as he could hold his breath. A minute. Two. And came up with a basket of oysters, and went down again. Ninety times a day. And the *nahham,* the singer, stood on the deck and sang to him, from the dawn to the dusk, so that he would know where the surface was."

> *Down on the rope, down through the green,
> Where the light grows old, and the stones are keen,
> Hold your breath, my brother, hold your breath—
> There is a pearl, in the house of death.
> Up on the rope, up through the gold,
> Where the sun is warm and the air is cold,
> Come up singing, my brother, come—
> The pearl is a wound that has not been dumb.*

"It is a wound," said Salim, "do you see? That is all a pearl is. A grain of sand gets into the oyster, and hurts it, and the oyster does not cast it out. It covers it, layer upon layer, patiently, for ten years, for twenty, in the dark. And the thing it makes out of its pain is the most beautiful thing in the world. That is what my father said. *A pearl is a wound that has been patient.*" He folded his hands. "There. That is the whole of my wisdom. I am eighty-three. I have said it."

There was a silence. In the corner, her uncle's clock ticked on.

"Mr. Al-Rashid," said Maryam. "You came to say something."

"I did."

"Say it."

He set down his cup. He put both hands flat on his knees, as a man does who is about to be weighed.

"I came to Muscat today," said Tariq, "to ask your uncle for the honour of your hand."

The clock stopped.

It had not run down. It had been stopped, by a clockmender's thumb, on the pendulum. In the long gold quiet the lemon tree moved in the courtyard, and from the lane a boy's voice cried *jasmine, jasmine, five baisa a string.*

"That was to have been my only purpose," Tariq went on. "I had it all arranged. I had a speech. I had a ring, which is in my pocket, and which is, I am bound to tell you, *on credit.* I had a very good suit." He drew a breath. "I have just heard, on the aeroplane, that a young man in Dubai is about to publish, tomorrow morning, a number. It is a number I have not told you. It is not a number that a man should have, who comes to ask for a hand. And so I will tell it you myself, before the newspaper does, so that you shall have it from the one person who would have preferred, with all his heart, to be the last."

"Tariq," said her uncle, from the corner, very softly.

"Four million, two hundred and nine thousand, four hundred dirhams," said Tariq Al-Rashid. "It is a guarantee. I signed it for a friend, over a plate of grilled fish, on a Tuesday. He has lost the ship. I have lost the money. I have been paying the interest out of my salary for fourteen months, and I have not told my mother, or my banker, or you. I wanted to ask for your hand as a man who had *nothing to hide.* And the truth is that I have been nothing *but* a thing to hide for a year, in a very good suit."

Nobody spoke. Salim Al-Balushi, retired imam, had closed his eyes, and his lips were moving, very slightly, in the beginning of a prayer.

Maryam Al-Sayed set down her loupe on the carpet. She rose, as slowly as if she were climbing from a well, and crossed to a small inlaid cabinet by the window. From it she took a tray of black velvet. On the velvet lay nine pearls, in a row, each the size of a pea.

She laid it on the carpet between them.

"I test every pearl," said Maryam. "I have tested nine thousand, one hundred and twelve. I have never once been wrong. It is my profession, and my pride, and I have used it, Mr. Al-Rashid, on everything that has entered this shop. I have tested old women's pearls and young men's pearls, and the pearls of a prince, and of a thief. I have tested my grandfather's. I have *never,*" and her voice, for the first time, wavered, a little, like a lamp in a draught, "tested you."

"You are under no obligation."

"I am under every obligation." She sat down on the carpet, across the tray. "There are nine pearls on this cloth. One is false. One only. I should like you to pick it out."

"I have no knowledge of pearls."

"I know. That is why I ask." She looked at him. "I have been watching you for eight months, over a telephone, and a shipment, and eleven manufactured reasons to call. You are a man who knows the tonnage of every ship on the Creek, and the weight of every cargo, and the exact price of fuel at Fujairah. You have never, once, in eight months, said to me a thing that you did not know the answer to. I do not know whether you are honest. I know that you are *careful.* The two are not the same."

He looked at the nine small pearls on the black velvet, dull gold, pink, white, cream, in the soft light, looking, to his eye, exactly alike.

"I cannot tell," said Tariq. "I would be lying if I said I could."

"Then say which."

"I will not guess. I would rather tell you that I do not know, than be right by luck and have you think it was skill." He lifted his eyes. "It is the first time in fourteen months that I have said that sentence aloud. It is a very curious sensation. I feel as if I had put down a great stone, and found that I am still standing."

There was a long, long silence.

Maryam took the loupe from the carpet. She took up the fourth pearl from the left, with the silver tweezers; held it against the lamp; and then, in the unembarrassed manner of her trade, laid it against her teeth and drew it across. She frowned. She took the second. She took the seventh. Then she put them all down in a row, and looked at the man across the tray, with her steady dark eyes, and said:

"Mr. Al-Rashid. There is a thing that a true pearl does, that no false one can do. It is the reason that I test them. It is not the grit, and it is not the weight, and it is not the lustre, though I use all of those. It is that a true pearl, when you hold it to the light, shows you, not the surface, but—"

*Here the page is torn.*

**Buenos Aires. San Telmo, a Saturday night, a milonga in a basement behind the Plaza Dorrego. Eleven forty.**

(*The bandoneón sighed in the corner like a very large animal at the end of a very long walk. Under a low ceiling of old pressed tin, a hundred couples moved in the dark, in a slow clockwise river, their feet making a sound like rain on a roof. The air smelled of cologne, leather, floor-polish, and mate.*)

They called it *El Cielo de los Zapatos,* the Heaven of Shoes, and in the fourteen months since the night that Luciana Cruz died, Mateo Fernández had not set foot in it.

He came in at a quarter to midnight, in a black suit with a white scarf, with the shoulders of a man who has been the finest dancer in the barrio and has forgotten how to be seen. He was thirty-five. He was handsome in a hard, vain, wounded way, like a statue that has been left out in the rain and has decided that the rain was its idea. He stood at the top of the stairs, under the single red lamp, while the room, as it does in Buenos Aires, went on dancing and *noticed.* A hundred heads did not turn. A hundred necks gave a very slight, polite shiver.

"*Che,*" said the woman at the door.

She was Agustina Benítez, fifty-four, a seller of *mate* and hot water, who sat on a stool at the foot of the stairs with a great steel thermos and a basket of sugared pastries, and who had, over thirty years, acquired the right to say what she liked to anyone who had to pass her. She was gentle. She was also, in a way that no one had ever quite established, a fortune-teller, though she charged only for the hot water.

"Mateo," said Agustina.

"Agustina."

"You have come."

"I have come to look."

"Of course you have. Nobody comes to a milonga to *dance.* They come to look, and they find themselves dancing, as a man who goes to the sea to look finds that he is wet." She poured a cup of mate, and handed it up, and he took it as a man takes a thing that he does not intend to drink. "Fourteen months. It is a long time for a foot."

"I have not come to dance."

"No. You have come because she is here."

He did not ask who.

At the far end of the room, at the long table of the teachers, with her back to the wall and a glass of mineral water before her, sat Valentina Cruz, thirty-three: Luciana's elder sister, and the finest teacher of tango in the city. She wore black. She sat as she always sat, upright and a little apart, with her hands folded, like a woman at a funeral who has been told she may leave, and has decided to stay. She had been Luciana's sister; she had been, for the last two years of Luciana's life, her rival, her teacher, her mirror, and the unwilling witness of what Luciana and Mateo had been, together, on a floor. She was proud. She was guarded. She had never, since the funeral, said Mateo's name aloud without something in her face closing, like a shop at five.

"A tanda of vals," announced the orchestra's singer, a big old tender man in a white shirt, with a voice like warm gravel: Gonzalo Benítez, sixty-two, Valentina's godfather and the only man alive who had known them both since they were small. "Three, for the lovers of the dark."

"She will not dance with you," said Ramiro Romero, appearing at Mateo's elbow like a bad conscience. Ramiro was sixty-seven, a retired choir director, the oldest matchmaker in San Telmo, and as blunt as a stone. "She will sit there until it is light. She is a very proud woman, and you are a very proud man, and between you there is a dead girl who was neither. You are like a choir, Mateo, with no basses. All treble, no ground."

"What would you have me do?"

"I would have you *cross the room.*"

"The code—"

"The code," said Ramiro, "was written by people who had never lost anybody. You do not need the *cabeceo.* You need only a pair of legs and no sense."

"I have the legs."

"Then," said the old man, "you are two thirds of the way."

The first bars of the vals began, slow and dark, with the bandoneón climbing, a note at a time, like a man going up a stair with a heavy case. Mateo set down the cup of mate. He crossed the dance floor, through the slow river of couples, who parted for him like water, with a rustle of silk and a silence that was almost a sound. He passed the long table. He did not stop in front of Valentina. He stopped beside her, in front of the chair next to hers, which was empty, and had been empty for fourteen months, for it had been Luciana's, and nobody in San Telmo would sit in it.

He bowed to the empty chair.

It was the old gesture, the courtesy before the dance, the nod that asks, and is not spoken. He held it for a long time. Behind him, the river of dancers faltered. In the whole low, dark, warm cellar, not one foot moved.

Then Valentina Cruz said, in a voice so quiet that only he and the chair could hear it:

"She is not there."

"No."

"You are bowing to an empty seat."

"I know."

"What do you want, Mateo?"

"I do not know." He straightened. He was white to the mouth. "I want to learn how to stand in a room with her in it and not be ashamed that I am the one who is breathing. I cannot do it alone. I am sorry. I am asking."

She looked up at him. Her face, in the red light, was a thing carved, and for a moment, he thought she would rise, and walk out, and he would have understood. But she had been a dancer all her life, and a dancer knows the truth about silence; that it is a part of the music; and that the thing that happens in it is never an accident.

"She was my sister," said Valentina. "And you were hers. And I am still jealous."

"I know."

"Of *both* of you."

"I know that too."

"Good," said Valentina Cruz. "Then at least we are not lying."

She stood. She laid her hand, very lightly, in his.

---

They danced the first song at the width of a hand.

It is a thing the old teachers say, that the embrace of the tango is not two bodies but a third thing between them, an animal made of breath and weight, which has to be fed. Mateo opened his arms, as he had done ten thousand times; and Valentina stepped into them, and there was, between her chest and his, a space as wide as a palm, in which something invisible was being held. Neither of them closed it. He led, very gently, a slow walk, a pause, a turn, a long, held, trembling silence on the downbeat; and she followed, with her eyes shut and her cheek turned away, as one follows a person in the dark who is not quite sure of the road.

They did not look at the floor. They did not look at the crowd. The crowd, for its part, made no pretence of dancing. A hundred people stood at the edges of the room, in their dark clothes and their good shoes, and watched two people dance with the whole of a grief held open between them like a door.

It was the width of a sister.

On the second song, a little of the space closed. His hand moved on her back, a half-inch. She breathed out. The old singer, in the corner, with his eyes shut and his big hand on his heart, had begun to sing, in a voice that cracked in the middle and went on, a tango about a lamp in a window, and a person who did not come home.

On the third song, with the bandoneón crying out its last long chord, Mateo stopped.

He did not drop her. He simply stopped, in the middle of the floor, with his arm at her waist and the space between them, and said, in a voice that was not his best:

"I cannot close it."

"I know."

"I would like to. I am not able."

"I know," said Valentina. She had not opened her eyes. "It is all right. It is the first time in fourteen months that you have danced. Do you know what that means? It means the floor still has you. It means you did not forget it." She breathed. "We will close it when we can. Not tonight."

"And if I never can?"

"Then we will dance at the width of a hand for forty years," said Valentina Cruz, "and people will say, *look at those two, they are so formal,* and we will know better."

At the foot of the stairs, Agustina Benítez, who had not poured a single cup of mate since the music began, took out a handkerchief and blew her nose. Ramiro Romero, beside her, said, in a voice of grudging triumph, to the room at large: "The basses have come in."

"They are *very* quiet," said Agustina.

"The best basses," said Ramiro, "are."

And in the corner, above the bandoneón, on a shelf where it had been put fourteen months earlier by a person who could not bear to take it down, a small framed photograph of a laughing young woman in a red dress seemed, for one moment, in the red light of the lamp, to be looking down with an expression of the profoundest satisfaction.

*(Mark this, reader: the dead are not jealous. They are only punctual. They wait in the place where you left them, and they are extremely patient; and when you finally come, they say nothing, and make room.)*
