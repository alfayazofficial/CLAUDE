## Chapter 22
### The Last Tango Lesson

> *"She was my sister, and you were hers, and I am still jealous."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0027

---

### SCENE I
*Buenos Aires. San Telmo, a Saturday afternoon in January. It is thirty-six degrees. Every shutter in the barrio is closed and every fan is praying. On the first floor of a former printing house, the Academia Cruz.*

January in Buenos Aires is summer, and the only city in this winter's book where nobody is cold. The tar softens. The jacaranda lets go of the last of its purple. The whole of San Telmo smells of hot stone, grilled meat, and the sweet brown ghost of dulce de leche, and in the Academia Cruz, whose floor is old pine and whose mirrors are older, eleven ceiling fans turned over the heads of two persons who were not speaking.

"Again," said Valentina Cruz.

"I have done it."

"You have done it *wrongly.* Again."

Valentina was thirty-three, a tango teacher, small and straight and dark, with a bun pinned so tightly it looked like a decision, and eyes that had been watching other people's feet for fifteen years and had arrived at a low opinion of most of them. She wore black. She had worn it since September two years ago, and told her students that it was practical.

Across the floor stood Mateo Fernández, thirty-five, who had been the best male dancer in the city for a decade, and knew it, and had arranged his entire bearing around the fact, as a cathedral arranges itself around a relic. He was tall, silver at the temples, and graceful in the manner of a thing that has never had to ask. His right arm was raised and curved. His left hand rested on the air at the height of a woman's shoulder-blade, with the fingers a little spread, holding no one.

For two years he had danced like that. He had danced it at the milongas of Villa Urquiza and the Confitería Ideal, at weddings, at the Mundial. He had never been without a partner: she was simply not visible. The patrons had grown used to it, as people grow used to a statue in the corner of a room. They moved aside for the empty place. Some of the old ladies, it was said, curtsied to it.

"I cannot do it with anyone else," said Mateo. "I have told you."

"You have told me nineteen times. It is a very repetitive grief."

"I came here to rehearse."

"You came here," said Valentina, "to dance with my sister. I am not offering a lesson. I am a landlady." Her voice, which was perfectly even, did a very small thing at the word. "Marisol would have corrected your left shoulder. It is a half-beat high. It has been a half-beat high since the funeral. A man holds a ghost higher than he held a woman. Did you know that?"

"No."

"It is the most common error in grief."

From the doorway, where he had been sitting for an hour on a plastic chair with a newspaper held at the angle of disapproval, a voice said: "I put twenty pesos on the shoulder."

This was Ramiro Romero, sixty-seven, a retired choir director, who sat at the door of the Academia every Saturday, because it was the best seat in Buenos Aires for hearing about other people's mistakes. He was blunt. He was a matchmaker by temperament and a gossip by vocation, and he applied to tango the acid ear of a man who had spent forty years listening for the flat tenor in the back row.

"Twenty pesos," said Agustina Benítez, who had come up the stairs with a thermos and a gourd of mate, and was handing it round with the expression of a priestess. She was fifty-four, a tea seller from Rosario, and she sold by the cup at the door of the Academia to the dancers, the loafers, and the heartbroken, the last of whom, in her professional opinion, were the only ones who tipped. "On the *shoulder?* Everyone knows it is the *chest.* A man who has lost a woman keeps the chest closed, like a shop in a bad month. Mateo, sweetheart, open the shop."

"I do not require a *commentary.*"

"Nobody requires one," said Agustina placidly. "It is why it is free."

---

Valentina set down her mate. She crossed the floor, in her flat black practice shoes, in the thick shimmering light that came between the shutters, and stopped in front of him, closer than she had stood since a morning in September in a churchyard.

"Take your hand down," she said.

"Why?"

"Because it is on the wrong person."

He did not move. It was, she saw, the first time in two years that anyone had told him the thing, and he stood quite still, with his arm held out, as though the air were still warm.

"She used to say," said Mateo, quietly, "that I led like a man carrying a tray. Everything perfect and nothing spilt. And then, in the last year, she said: *You have started to listen.* I do not know when it was. I would give anything to have asked her what she heard."

"She heard you, you idiot," said Valentina Cruz, "*breathing.*" And before she knew that she was going to do it, she lifted her own hand, and laid it flat upon his left arm, and drew it down; and then she stepped into the space where the ghost had stood.

It was only a walk. In tango the first thing, the only thing, is the walk: eight steps, forward, with the weight carried from heel to ball like a secret from one hand to another. She did not dance. She stood in the circle of his arm, with her face turned away, with her left hand on his shoulder, which was indeed a half-beat high, and she said, not looking at him: "Walk."

He walked.

She went backward, as a follower goes, with the whole of her attention on the one point of contact on her back, as a blind woman listens to a hand upon a banister. Eight steps. At the fourth he stumbled. At the fifth he was wrong. At the sixth, to his own astonishment, he knew where she was going to be.

"There," said Valentina. "That is her. That is not her. That is *me,* and I am alive, and I do not hold the way she did. I am heavier. I lean on the left. I would rather die than be led through a turn." She stopped. Her voice cracked once, like ice on a pond. "I have hated you for two years, Mateo. She was my sister, and she danced with you, and I taught her. I taught her every step of it. And when she danced with you she never looked at me again."

The room was very still.

"I did not know that."

"No. Nor did I." She let go. "That is the last lesson I shall give you. You will come on Thursday, and we shall walk. Not dance. *Walk.* Eight steps. Then ten. And if you ever say *ghost* to me again, I shall put you out of the window."

"It is the first floor."

"I shall be generous with the height," said Valentina.

In the doorway, Ramiro Romero lowered his newspaper by two centimetres, which for him was a standing ovation.

"I withdraw the twenty pesos," he said. "It was neither."

Later, when they had gone, and the fans turned over an empty floor, Valentina sat in the light on the edge of the stage, with her shoes in her lap, and said what she had never said to the mirror, or the dancers, or God:

> *You had the better part of everything, my sister:
> The stage, the man, the laughter, and the grace.
> I had the stairs, and thirty years of watching
> You turn, and turn, and turn away. And now
> I have the floor you left, and I would give it
> To hear you laugh at me for being jealous.
> You laughed at everything. You would have laughed
> To see me walk a man across a room
> Because I could not bear that he should dance
> With emptiness. You would have said: "Vale,
> You're hopeless. Take him. He is yours to teach."
> I will not take him. He was never mine.
> But I will teach him how to hold a living
> Woman, so that someone, someday, may.*

### SCENE II
*Santos, on the coast of São Paulo state. Sunday, the same weekend. A small blue house two streets from the port, where the cranes stand over the roofs like patient herons.*

The invitation had come in a hand-written envelope, with a drawing of a bus on it. *Dona Zuleide Teles requests the presence of the Doutora and the Seu Carlos at a lunch in memory of her husband, who would have been fifty-nine.*

It was the first time they had been in the same room since the lift. In the intervening nine weeks Dr. Lima had found it necessary to follow up eleven patients at the Santa Aurora rehabilitation wing, every one of whom, Carlos had observed, was in perfect health. On the Thursday of the first, she had stood in the doorway with a clipboard and an air of intense clinical purpose, and said, "I wish to observe the technique with Dona Cida." She had observed Dona Cida for forty minutes. Dona Cida, who was seventy-one and nobody's fool, had said afterward: "That girl has not looked at me once. She is looking at *you.* In my day we called it a follow-up."

Now they sat at a table on a back veranda under a lemon tree, among forty bus drivers in the uniform shirts of the Linha 7, and a widow in black, and a hot Atlantic wind. Beatriz's father, Pedro Rocha, had taken over the kitchen at nine in the morning, in defiance of Dona Zuleide's protests and the entire code of hospitality, and was presiding over a feijoada of such majesty that a driver of sixty, who had not wept at his friend's funeral, was weeping into a pork rib.

"It is very good," said Dona Zuleide to the table, "and I did not make any of it. He will not let me."

"Senhora," said Pedro, from the stove, in the voice of a prophet, "a widow has cooked enough."

She was a small wrinkled woman of sixty-six with a face like a clenched walnut and the eyes of a bird, and she had been watching Carlos since he arrived. She watched him now as he rose, with the plates, as a guest must, and carried them to the kitchen, and ran water over his hands at the sink, scrubbing them, since he was a physiotherapist and a man who believed that hands, after food, should be returned to the profession.

He took off the ring.

He did it without thought, as a man does a thing that has become necessary, and set it on the windowsill beside the soap, where the light fell through the lemon leaves and made it look, for a moment, like a small gold sun.

Then he dried his hands and went out to the veranda, and forgot it.

---

Beatriz found it ten minutes later. She had come in for water.

She did not pick it up. She stood, with the glass in her hand, and looked at a plain gold band on a windowsill, and the light moving over it, and at the inside of the band, where a line of tiny letters had caught the sun: *M. & C. — 14.3.* Under it a second date. There was only the one name. She did not have to be told what it meant. She was a surgeon. She knew what it was to read an X-ray and see, without being told, the old break that has healed.

"He is a widower, child," said a voice at her elbow.

Dona Zuleide Teles had come in, as small women do, with no sound at all.

"I know a widower when I see one wash his hands without his ring," said the old woman. "A married man leaves it on. A widower takes it off in other people's kitchens, where nobody will remark. It is the one room where he is allowed to be a man whose hand is empty." She took the glass out of Beatriz's grip, and drank from it, with a certain firmness. "I have been a wife for forty-one years. I am a widow since the third of December. I have not yet taken mine off. I am telling you what I know, which is that the ring is not a lie. It is a *door.* He has been standing in front of it, holding it open for her, for three years. Somebody has to say to him: *You may come through.*"

"I am not—it is not—Dona Zuleide, I have no right—"

"You have a pulse," said the widow, tartly. "In my experience it is the only credential. Go and ask him."

---

He was in the garden by the lemon tree, looking at the cranes.

"Seu Carlos," said Beatriz. "I have a question, and I am a surgeon, so I am going to ask it in the most efficient manner possible. Are you married?"

He turned. The wind lifted his hair. He looked at her for a long time, with his warm crooked face, and in it she saw, one after the other, six or seven things he might have said, none of which he said.

"I was," said Carlos Mendes. "Marina died three years ago, in March. It was long. I would sooner not say how long. I have worn the ring because it is easier than a sentence." He drew breath. "I did not tell you in the lift because the word was heavier than the lift."

"I know."

"You *know?*"

"I saw it on the sill. I am sorry. I did not touch it."

"It is not a thing one needs to protect," said Carlos, gently, and, to her great surprise, smiled. "I left it there on purpose. I did not know I had. I think my hands had decided something before I had."

They stood together under the lemon tree. Over the roofs the cranes of the port lifted a blue container into the white sky, slowly, with the grave ceremony of the very large.

"I am not asking for anything," said Beatriz. "I wished to know. It is a matter of—of accuracy."

"Doutora Lima."

"Yes."

"I will fetch it," said Carlos. "I would not leave it in a stranger's kitchen." He walked into the house, and when he came out the ring was in his hand, and he stood a moment with it, turning it as he had turned it a thousand times against the hem of his coat.

He did not put it on.

He slid it, instead, into the breast pocket of his shirt, on the left, over the heart, where it could be felt, and was not worn.

"It is not that I am free," said Carlos. "I do not know what I am. But I think I should like to find out, in daylight, with no ring on my hand, and a surgeon who comes to my ward eleven times in nine weeks to follow up on patients who are not ill."

"It was *nine* times," said Beatriz, scarlet.

"Dona Cida counts eleven."

"Dona Cida," said Beatriz Lima, with great dignity, "is a gossip."

And from the veranda, in a voice of sacred solemnity, her father began, over forty bus drivers and a widow, to sing.

### SCENE III
*Yogyakarta. A workshop behind a house in the Prawirotaman quarter. The wet season. The rain is coming down on the tiles in a long continuous roar, as if the sky were being poured out of a very large jug.*

Ermis Larasati opened the email on a Wednesday, at eleven in the morning, with a canting in her right hand.

She had been drawing wax on a cloth. It was a *kawung,* the old pattern of four ovals, which looks like a field of seeds and is said to be the oldest design on Java; and the little copper-spouted pen, a *canting,* had been tracing the line of it, hot wax running from its bowl in a thread as fine as hair. It is the whole art. One draws with wax whatever one wishes to protect, and afterward the cloth is put into the dye, and the dye takes everything that is not protected, and when the wax is boiled away what remains, in a white line against the indigo, is the thing that was kept.

Her phone gave a soft chime on the table. She read the subject line with the pen still in the air, and a drop of wax fell on the cloth, and made a small spreading golden mistake.

*Fellowship Offer — Maashaven Textile Lab, Rotterdam.*

*Dear Ms. Larasati,*

*It is with great pleasure that the selection committee offers you a two-year residency, with a studio, a stipend, and full access to the Laboratory's collection of historical dye-works. The programme begins on the twenty-second of May. We would be grateful for your acceptance by the thirty-first of January…*

She read it three times. She put down the canting, with great care, in its brass dish. She sat for some time with her hands in her lap in the roar of the rain.

Behind her, on the window-rail, the green parrot opened a yellow eye.

"*TOO LATE,*" said Ayu.

"It is not too late. It is January."

"*BEAUTIFUL.*"

"You do not know what it says."

"*TOO LATE. TOO LATE.*"

"Ayu," said a dry soft voice from the doorway, "go and sit on your perch and let the child think."

It was Eyang Sri, her grandmother, eighty-one, who had made batik for sixty-eight years and had never, to anybody's knowledge, been in a hurry. She stood in the door in a faded *kebaya* with a cup of ginger tea, and she looked at her granddaughter's face, and then at the wax mistake on the cloth, and she did not need to be told.

"It came," said Eyang.

"Yes."

"Where?"

"Rotterdam."

"Ah. That is the cold place with the water." She sat down, slowly, on the low stool, and set the tea between them. "And you are going to say yes."

"I do not know."

"You know. Your hand has put a mistake on a *kawung,* and you have not corrected it. When a girl does not correct an error in her best pattern, she is thinking of a very great thing." The old woman took her granddaughter's hand, turned it palm up, and examined it, as if the matter might be written there. "Is there a boy?"

"*Eyang.*"

"I am not asking whether you are going to marry him. I am asking whether there is a boy."

Ermis looked at the rain.

"There is a person I talk to on Fridays," she said. "In Dhaka. He is an hour behind. He has a father in debt and a mother who is ill, and a sister, and no visa. He is the kindest man I have never met. I do not know what he is to me. I have not said the word, and he has not, and I do not think either of us will, unless somebody is made to." She swallowed. "If I tell him, he will tell me to go. He will say it before I have finished the sentence. He will be generous. He will be right. And then I will go, and it will be done. And if I do not tell him—"

"Then you have bought," said Eyang Sri, "a little more of Friday."

"Yes."

"It is a poor currency, child. It runs out." But she patted the hand, and she was smiling a little. "Tell the boy."

"Not yet. Not until I know what I am going to say."

"When you are a girl of twenty-four," said her grandmother, rising, "you think the wax is for the part you wish to *keep.* It is also for the part you are afraid to dye."

She went out. The rain came down.

And Ermis took up the canting again, and stood a long while at her table in the ticking dark of the workshop, and said, not to the parrot, not to the old woman, not even to the wet gold shape of her mistake, but to the whole damp light of January:

> *An hour is not a long way. It is the time
> It takes a pot of ginger tea to cool.
> It is the time it takes a stranger's name
> To change into a stranger's voice. And yet
> I have it in my hands: two years, a lab,
> A studio, a river, and a door
> That opens on the whole wide world of cloth.
> I have wanted this since I was nine years old.
> I wanted it the way the earth wants rain.
> And now it falls, and I am standing here
> Beneath it, with an umbrella that I made
> Myself, of wax and promises. Oh, Fayaz,
> If you were here I would not need to speak.
> But you are there; and that is what I'll say.
> Not yet. Not yet. Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.*

---

On Friday, at 22:00 in Dhaka and 23:00 in Yogyakarta, the green dot came on.

"You are *early,*" said Fayaz.

"I am four minutes early," said Ermis. "It is called being the one who is ahead."

He had come up the stairs from the tea stall, where Bunny had delivered an opinion on the quality of his shirt, and he sat at the desk by the window with the laptop propped on a stack of textbooks. It was the dry cold of Dhaka's winter, the hour of shawls and date-palm juice and wood-smoke, and the lane outside was misted over like a dream of itself. On the corner of the desk, face down beneath a paperweight, lay an envelope with an embassy's crest, which he had been not opening for nine days.

She had her workshop in the background, rust and indigo, and a bird on the rail, and the rain, which came through the little speakers as a faint rush like a held breath.

They talked of everything.

They talked of Ayu, who had learned a fourth word, which was *invoice.* They talked of a firm in Singapore that had sent him a test project, a night job, five days, with a decision after; of his sister's physics, which had taken on the aspect of a religious conflict; of whether a *kawung* was better in blue or in brown. They talked for an hour and ten minutes, as one talks across a very small table. Twice he made her laugh so hard the pen rattled.

Twice she opened her mouth.

"Ermis?" said Fayaz, the second time.

"Yes?"

"You were going to say something."

*Say it,* thought Ermis. *Say: I have been offered two years in a country with water. Say: I am an hour ahead of you and I am about to be nine thousand miles.*

"I was going to say," said Ermis, "that you have a very good face for a man who is in debt."

He considered this.

"It is my only collateral."

"I know," said Ermis. "I have been looking at it for four months." Then, very quickly: "Good night, Fayaz. Tell Bunny the shirt was acceptable."

"I will tell him. He will be insufferable."

"He is already. It is the family trait of the very young." The screen flickered. "Same time next Friday?"

"I have never missed one."

"You were four minutes late in January."

"It was a very slow clock."

She laughed, and cut the call, and the screen went black, and in the bright small mirror of the dark he saw his own face, and the unopened letter behind it, and did not touch it.

In Yogyakarta, in the rain, with the wax going cold in its dish, Ermis sat for the length of one breath and then another, before the glowing rectangle of her second screen. At seven minutes past midnight, by her clock, which was truly seven past eleven in the lane at Mirpur, where a young man was walking to the corner for a last cup of tea and a little of the cold, she put out her hand, and pressed *Accept.*

"*TOO LATE,*" said Ayu, from the rail, very softly.

"I know," said Ermis. "I know."
