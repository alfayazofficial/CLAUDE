## Chapter 40
### The Calligrapher's Ink

> *"I would teach you every letter, if you asked."*
> — Register of Black Stars, Entry 0502

---

### SCENE I
*Marrakech. The spice souk, near the old square of the apothecaries. Noon, and a heat with opinions.*

A man who cannot read must be a very good liar, a very good listener, or a very good nose. Youssef Benali was all three, and handsome besides, which helps.

He was thirty, and he sold spices from a stall the width of a wardrobe, in a lane so crowded with cones of saffron-gold and cumin-brown and paprika-red that the sun came down into it already tinted. He could tell ras el hanout from its imitators with his eyes shut. He knew forty prices by heart and eleven languages of haggling. He had a voice like warm honey poured slowly over a very hot spoon, and tourists from Lyon to Lagos would stand and listen to him describe a pinch of turmeric as though he were reading them a love poem.

He was not reading. He had never read anything in his life. He had, since the age of ten, *remembered* it.

His jars carried no labels. They carried small, careful drawings: a crocus for saffron, a seed with a tail for cumin, a flame for chilli. The tourists thought this was charming branding. His customers thought it was eccentric. His accountant, a bookish clockmender named Mehdi Bennani from the next stall, who had once told him that he had "the most *visual* ledger in Morocco," thought it was a sign of genius, and was quite sincere.

"I have found," said Mehdi that noon, appearing between the saffron and the sumac with a ledger under his arm, "an error in your invoice from Fez."

"I do not see an error."

"You will, when I read it out." Mehdi, who trusted everyone and was wrong about most of them, adored reading aloud, and did so in three voices: a gruff merchant, a prim clerk, and a tragic widow. "*'Seventy kilos of cumin, at forty dirhams—'*"

"I have left my glasses at home."

"You do not wear glasses."

"Then I have left my *eyes* at home," said Youssef smoothly. "It is a hot day. Read on."

Mehdi read on. The lie passed over them as it had passed over every day for twenty years, like a small, tame cloud. It was a good lie. It had a beautiful voice. It had never once been found out, and Youssef loved it and hated it, as one hates an old coat that has come to fit the exact shape of one's shame.

"Do you know what the trouble with you is?" said Mehdi, closing the ledger.

"I am sure you will tell me."

"You are *too* clever. A man like you could do anything, if he would only sit down and learn something."

Youssef smiled at him, and it was a very good smile, and behind it a door in his chest shut as softly as a drawer.

"A fool speaks wisdom by accident," he said, "so they say. You are the proof."

"I take that as a compliment."

"Naturally," said Youssef Benali. "You are an optimist."

### SCENE II
*An atelier in a lane behind the old madrasa. A window on a courtyard with a lemon tree. A long table, a row of reed pens, a smell of soot, honey and gum arabic.*

His shop sign had been commissioned in May. He had to collect it in June. That was the whole misfortune, and he had known for a month that it would end in this lane.

The woman at the table did not look up when he came in. She was finishing a stroke, and one does not interrupt a stroke any more than one interrupts a prayer. She was twenty-eight, with her hair wrapped in a plain indigo scarf, ink on the second finger of her right hand, and a way of holding her breath while the reed was on the paper that made the whole room seem to hold it with her.

Salma Idrissi lifted the pen. She let out the breath.

"You are the spice man."

"I am the spice man."

"Youssef Benali, *Épices*, two lines, one in the Maghrebi hand, one in French for the tourists. It is nearly done." She turned the board toward him. "Tell me if the proportions please you."

He looked at the board.

On it, in black ink as deep as the pupil of an eye, ran a line of Arabic so graceful that it seemed less written than *grown*, curling across a ground of faded saffron paper, the letters leaning against one another like a family on a sofa. He looked at it for a long time. He did it well; he had had twenty years of practice at looking at pages.

"It is beautiful," said Youssef.

"Thank you. And the words?"

"The words are what I asked for."

"Which is the second word?"

It was said so quietly, and so gently, that for a moment he did not understand it was a question. Then he understood, and the room tilted a little, as a boat tilts when the weight of the cargo is suddenly found.

"I would prefer," said Youssef, honey-voiced, hand steady, "to hear you read it. It is rude to read your own sign aloud. A man should hear his name in a woman's mouth."

"That is a very handsome evasion."

"I have had practice."

"I know," said Salma. "I know exactly how much."

She did not say it unkindly. She laid down the reed and wiped her finger on a cloth, and she looked at him full on, as one looks at a letter one has been trying to place for months.

"Forgive me, Monsieur Benali. I have a bad habit. I read the Register of Black Stars at night when I cannot sleep. There was an entry from Casablanca." She said it as if she were handing him something very fragile. "Number four hundred and thirty-nine. It said that a man could not read well, and had hidden it for twenty years. I do not know who wrote it. I never will, unless he tells me. But when you walked in, and looked at my work as a man looks at a closed door in a house he knows very well—"

"I do not know what you mean."

"No," said Salma. "Of course you don't."

Silence. Outside, in the courtyard, a lemon dropped from the tree and rolled across the stone, in no hurry, like a small yellow thought.

"I wrote one, too," said Salma. "It is the five hundred and second. I will not tell you what it says. It is not a thing to be *read* to someone. It is a thing to be *asked* for."

Youssef looked at his hands, which had never shaken, even in a bargain with a rival or a scorpion.

They were shaking.

"I do not ask for things," he said.

"I know. It is the same with me."

"I have lived twenty years behind this smile."

"It is a good smile."

"It is a *wall* with a good smile on it."

"Then," said Salma, with the very slightest tilt of the mouth, "we have the same architect."

He sat. It was the first time he had sat in somebody else's shop without a bargain in his head.

"I did not learn," said Youssef Benali. "The letters moved. The teacher said I was lazy. The teacher hit me, and then said I was stupid. After that I did not need a teacher to say it. I said it to myself so well." He breathed out. "The *ba* looks like the *ta* and the *ta* looks like the *nun*, and when I stare they dance. So I learned the rest. I learned the world with my nose and my tongue and my memory, and I became very, very rich in everything but the one thing every child is born into."

"Do you want to learn?"

"Yes," said Youssef.

It was the word he had not said in twenty years, and it came out of him smaller than he expected and a great deal heavier, like a stone from a well.

"Say it again," said Salma, "and mean the whole sentence."

"I am asking you," said Youssef, "if you would teach me every letter."

"I would."

### SCENE III
*The atelier. Many afternoons. The reeds, the ink, the lemon tree changing its mind about being green.*

She began not with letters but with ink.

"You will make your own," said Salma, "so you will know what you are asking it to carry." There was soot, scraped from a lamp; there was gum arabic, in amber tears; there was water in which a pinch of saffron had steeped, which was the only part he could have done blind. She stirred it for an hour. "Honey," she said, "to keep it from cracking. Everything that endures needs a little sweetness."

"You have been waiting to say that."

"I say it every time. It is a very good sentence." She put a reed in his hand. It had been cut on a slant, with a split at the nib, and it sat in his fingers as awkwardly as a stranger's child. "Now. The *nuqta*. The dot. Press the pen flat and make one."

He made one. A rhombus, small and clean and black.

"That," said Salma, "is your ruler. Every letter in this alphabet is measured by the dot of your own pen. The *alif* is five of them, stacked, one on the other." She drew it: a single upright stroke, plain and tall. "The circle that holds all the other letters has the *alif* for a diameter. Nothing is by eye. Nothing is *guessed.* Do you understand? This is not a thing the clever are born knowing. It is a thing you may *measure.*"

He stared at the line. He did not say anything. He had been measured all his life, and found short, by people with rulers who had never given him a reed.

She taught him by his own trade, because a teacher who ignores the pupil's gifts is a fool, and she was exacting but never foolish. The *ba*, she said, was a small boat, and in its hold a single seed. "A seed *beneath.* You know the cumin seed. Put it in the boat." The *ta* was the same boat with two gulls above. "Two gulls." The *tha*: three cloves, set above like a crown. The *nun* was not a boat at all but a bowl, deep, with one fly floating on the top.

"Ba, a seed in the hold," said Youssef slowly, and made the stroke. The reed squeaked. The line wobbled and sat on the page like a drunk on a pavement. "Ta, two gulls. Tha—three cloves. Nun, a bowl, with one fly."

"The fly is above."

"The fly is *above*," said Youssef, "and may he drown."

She laughed. It was the first time he had heard it, and it was not the tin roof or the bell or the cascade of other people's laughter. It was a short, surprised sound, as of a bird that had not meant to sing. He wrote it down in his head with the other things he was keeping, alongside the smell of the ink and the lemon tree's light on the table, and he thought: *whatever else happens, I have this.*

They had many afternoons. He cried over the second week, not noisily; he wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, and she looked at the window until he was done, and no one mentioned it. In the third week he confused the *dal* and the *ra*, and she did not correct him; she drew a very small camel and a very small tree, and he fixed it himself.

On the fortieth day he read a word.

It was a short one. He did not read it with his eyes, as other children do. He read it with his hand: he felt the stroke before he saw it, the boat, the seed, the little gulls. It rose up out of the page as the first star rises out of the dark, a thing that had been there all along waiting for the lamps to go out.

"*Salma,*" read Youssef Benali.

"That is my name."

"It is on your door. I have walked past it for six weeks, and I did not know that it was *you.*"

"You have never read my door."

"I have read it now," said Youssef. "Now it is the only thing in Marrakech I can read."

She turned her face aside. The scarf had slipped, a little. She put it right.

"Come," said Salma. "You've earned a walk. The square is waking up."

They walked, in the long copper evening, through Jemaa el-Fna, where the snake-charmers were packing and the storytellers were beginning, each in his circle of listeners like a pebble in still water. A storyteller with a voice like an old drum was telling the story of a merchant who lost his ship and found his wife, and Youssef stood at the edge of the ring, as he had stood a thousand evenings, and listened with his whole body.

"You see?" said Salma softly. "There is more than one way to hold a story. The ears are very old. The eyes are only a *recent* arrival. My grandfather, who copied the Book in this hand sixty years, used to say that a letter well made is a courtesy to God and to the reader, but that the courtesy was first learned by those who *listened.* You are not behind, Youssef. You are *early.*"

He did not answer. He did not trust the honey in his voice. He had spent twenty years making it sweet and now it only wanted to be true.

At the end of the story the storyteller held out his cap, and Youssef dropped in a hundred-dirham note, and the old man looked at him, and said, "God make your page the colour of your heart."

"Which colour is that?" asked Youssef.

"Ask your teacher," said the old man, and winked.

That night, when the lane had gone to sleep and the jars stood in their rows like a congregation of small saints, Youssef sat alone at his stall under one bare bulb, with a reed, a pot of ink, and a sheet of cheap paper, and did what he had never done: he spoke to himself without a smile.

> *Twenty years I wore my silence as a coat
> And called it style. I sold the world its saffron
> And bought myself one thing: the fear of paper.
> It is a small thing, on a table, white,
> Which cannot hurt me. Yet I would have crossed
> The desert twice to dodge it. Here it lies.
> I have a seed, a boat, a bowl, a fly.
> I have a woman who will wait while I
> Make crooked letters, and who does not laugh
> Except in the right places. Is that love?
> I will not say so. I will say: the ink
> Is warm. The line is wrong. I'll draw it twice.*

He drew it twice. Then, because the *ba* has a seed beneath it, he drew the seed, and put the date under it, and the first line in his own hand that he had ever shown to nobody: a name, four letters long, with the dot where the dot belongs.

### SCENE IV
*Mexico City. Coyoacán. A Saturday, eleven in the morning, and a church bell with no sense of time.*

Half a world away, in a city where it was still the morning, a man in a black wedding suit was standing on a ladder with a brush.

This was not, strictly, how the programme had been planned.

"Rafael!" shouted Camila Reyes, from the foot of the ladder, in a white dress and fury. "You are *married!* You have been married for forty-five minutes! There is a *mariachi!*"

"I know," said Rafael Ortega, twenty-six, painter, romantic, who had once painted the same woman's face on a hundred and three walls without knowing her name. "I will be a minute."

"You said that at the altar."

"I *was* a minute at the altar."

"You were eleven. Father Anselmo timed you."

For they had been wed at the church of San Juan Bautista at ten o'clock, in the old Coyoacán way: the *lazo*, a great looped rosary of white flowers laid round the shoulders of both in a figure eight, binding them to one another; the thirteen gold *arras* coins poured from his hands into hers, a promise to keep a house, which she received with a look that said she would be checking the accounts. Her mother, Paloma, a hairdresser who had been happy once and not noticed, had wept through the entire Mass and ruined four handkerchiefs and an uncle's tie. Rafael's father, Ignacio, retired vendor of anything, had wept into a tamale.

And now the whole party had spilled out into the lane behind the house, where an old wall of lime plaster, prepared that week by the stonemason Ximena Salazar, a woman of sixty-three with forearms like cables and the severity of an Inca queen, stood white and waiting.

"It is the hundred-and-fourth," said Rafael, not coming down. "I could not marry you and leave you on a hundred and three walls like a lost umbrella. There has to be one that is *finished.*"

"You have painted me a hundred times!"

"I painted a *face.* An idea. The girl in the pictures is a stranger. She is kind and tall and she has never once lost her temper in court."

"I lose my temper in court every day."

"I know," said Rafael. "I am going to paint *that.*"

He turned back to the wall. And the street, which had been squabbling and eating and tuning guitars, went quiet in the way crowds go quiet for a thing they have not been told to watch.

It was not the Camila of the hundred walls. It was the one who stood at the foot of the ladder in a white dress with her hair coming down: sharp-jawed, a little cross, one eyebrow raised in indictment, and in the crook of her arm a thick law book with a broken spine. She was laughing at somebody off the edge of the picture. Behind her he painted marigold petals in orange flame, the *cempasúchil* of the dead and of the living; and the thirteen coins like small suns; and, in the lower corner, very small, a street full of people who had come to a wedding and stayed to be remembered.

"Is that me?" said Officer Alejandro Hernández, forty-five, of the Coyoacán police, who had come to direct the traffic and had been standing at the edge of the mural for twenty minutes stroking his moustache.

"It is a policeman," said Rafael. "Anyone's."

"It has my moustache."

"All policemen have your moustache, Alejandro."

"I do not think that is *true,*" said Officer Hernández, deeply wounded, and went to look at his own reflection in the patrol car, saving money, as he often said, to disappear, and meanwhile appearing at every public occasion in the district.

Camila said nothing for a long time.

Then, in her wedding dress, in front of the whole of Coyoacán, she took the ladder in both hands and began to climb.

"What are you doing?" said Rafael.

"I walked to every wall," said Camila. "A hundred and three walls, in four years, in the heat, in my good shoes, to find the hand that painted me. I found him. And I have never once held the brush." She reached the top, took it from his fingers, and dipped it in black. "Move."

"What are you going to paint?"

"Your hand," she said.

And there, at the edge of the plaster, beside her painted self, a little crooked and a little too thick, with a lawyer's confidence and no training whatsoever, Camila Reyes painted the five long fingers of the man who had painted her: the left hand, the hand that held the brush, reaching up out of the corner of the wall as though to lift her down.

The mariachi, who had been waiting since the end of the Mass, took it as a cue.

"*¡Que vivan los novios!*" cried Ignacio Ortega, with tears running into his moustache.

"*¡Que vivan!*" shouted forty neighbours; and a nine-year-old flower girl named Renata Vargas, who had been told to scatter petals and had thrown them all at the policeman, threw the basket as well.
