# Chapter Twenty-Two
## The Broken Promise

*Eleanor*

Adrian was waiting in the library.

I had known he would be. I had known it from the moment I crossed the stile and saw Corbett's black shape standing at the gate of Vale House, hat in hand, an unreadable statue in the early morning. I had known it as I walked up the drive with the dawn at my back and a night's silence in my hair. And I had known it when Mrs. Crowe met me in the hall, her face gray, her lips pressed so tightly that they had nearly disappeared.

"He's been here since six," she whispered. "He would not be put off. He's in the library with a pistol on the desk."

"A pistol."

"It isn't pointed at anyone. It's just—there."

I took off my cloak.

I handed it to her. I smoothed my hair with both hands, and straightened my collar, and drew a long breath. Then I opened the library door.

---

He sat in my father's chair behind the writing table, with his hands folded on the blotter. The curtains were drawn. A single candle burned beside the pistol, which lay on a sheet of white paper like a specimen. He did not rise.

He looked ten years older than he had the day before.

"Good morning, Eleanor."

"Adrian."

"Sit down."

"I would rather stand."

"You will want to sit," he said gently, "when you hear it."

I sat.

He regarded me for some time. I had the odd sensation of being weighed, of being measured against some private standard, a scale in which my worth in the world was being balanced against something I could not see.

"You went to the abbey," Adrian said.

I did not answer.

"You went down into the crypt, with the keeper. You found a man living there. You found a ledger. You took the ledger, and the man, and carried them to the lighthouse, and you spent the morning in the keeper's cottage." He held up one hand. "I do not ask you to deny it. Corbett followed you from the church wall. He is a very thorough person."

"Then you know everything."

"Almost."

"What do you want?"

"To make you an offer."

I waited.

"The man in the crypt is Samuel Thorne," said Adrian. "He has been dead, in law, for six years. Dead men do not hold property, or sign for bread, or write threatening letters. But a deserter does."

My blood ran cold.

"Deserter—"

"Of Her Majesty's Navy. Absent without leave on the night of the wreck, and thereafter." His eyes were pale and steady. "The penalty is, as you know, severe. I could have him in irons by noon. A man in his state of health would not survive a week in the Penzance gaol. It would be a very quiet death."

"You wouldn't."

"I would prefer not to."

"You *wouldn't.*"

"I would prefer," said Adrian, "to be asked."

He opened a drawer. He took out a paper and laid it on the table beside the pistol. It was a warrant, in his own clerkly hand, with a blank where the name should go.

"I have signed it," he said. "I have not filled it in."

I could not take my eyes from it.

"There is also the question of the keeper," he said. "Gabriel Thorne was present at your mother's death. He was, by his own account, the last to hold her. He took a bribe in return for silence. In the eyes of the law, that makes him an accessory after the fact. The penalty is less than for the other. Seven years' transportation, perhaps. Ten. A man with his record would find it hard."

"I will testify—"

"To what? That he was *kind?*" He smiled, a terrible, patient, tender smile. "My dear. You are very clever, and you are very brave. But you are not a lawyer. A woman who spent the night at his cottage would be a poor witness for him."

I felt the blood leave my face.

"You knew."

"I have known since Tuesday week."

"And you said nothing."

"I was waiting for you to tell me."

He leaned back. The candle flame wavered. In its flicker his face looked carved from wax.

---

"I will tell you what I want," Adrian said.

"Say it."

"I want you to marry me on Saturday."

"Saturday is the seventh."

"I know the date." He drew a breath. "I have spoken to the Reverend. He has agreed to read the banns today. We shall be wed in the parish church at noon, with the harbor in attendance and a feast after. I shall settle upon you the entire estate of Vale House, in your own name, to be held absolutely. I shall give you all the money you require. And I shall give you my full cooperation."

"Cooperation?"

"With whatever you plan to do with the ledger."

I stared at him.

"I will not stop the boat," said Adrian. "I will not intercept the Reverend. I will not touch the Thornes. Samuel may recover in comfort at Marlowe House, if he wishes, or at the cottage, if he prefers. He and his brother will both be free men, and they will remain so." His voice dropped. "And I shall go with the Reverend to the Assizes at Bodmin, and I shall confess it all. The *Sparrowhawk.* The *Halcyon.* My father's key. I shall put my own head in the noose."

I could not speak.

"In return," Adrian said, "you will give up Thorne."

The room swam.

"You will not see him again. You will not write. You will not go near the lighthouse. You will marry me, and you will bear my name, and you will be—" His voice cracked. "You will be *mine,* Eleanor, for as long as I live. Which, if the Assizes do what I expect, will not be long."

"You *want* to be hanged?"

"I want you to be *safe.*" His eyes were wild. "I want to know, in the last days of my life, that I have done one thing for you that was right. That I stood on the scaffold and you were in my house, in my bed, with my name on your finger, and that it was a clean name at last. I want to be the one who saved you. Not him."

"That is not love."

"No?"

"It's ownership."

"Is there a difference?"

"Yes," I said. "You have never learned it."

He laughed. It was a broken sound. "Perhaps not."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I fill in the warrant."

---

I do not know how long I sat there.

There is a silence that comes when all the doors in a house have closed at once, and one stands in the middle of a room hearing the echoes die. I have heard it three times in my life. The first was the night my mother went into the rain. The second was the day my father's will was read. And the third was that morning in the library, with the candle burning and the pistol on the blotter and the man I had known since I was fourteen sitting across from me like a stranger.

I thought of Samuel in the cot, his chest rattling.

I thought of Gabriel's face when I said *I love you.*

I thought of the ledger, the fourteen men of the *Sparrowhawk,* the names on the walls. The tide that came every night and washed them clean and left them whole.

I thought: *One day. Two days. That is all I need. The boat leaves at dawn on Saturday. If I can hold Adrian until it sails—*

"Saturday," I said.

"Saturday."

"At noon."

"Yes."

"And Samuel will be unharmed until then."

"On my word."

"And Gabriel."

"On my word."

"Your word," I said. "Is worth very little, Adrian."

"It is worth something to me."

I stood.

I walked to the window and drew back the curtain a hand's breadth. Outside it was full morning, pale gold, mild and windless. Over the sea the sky was a delicate green. And at the edge of the world, low on the horizon, a thin dark line lay along the water like a smudge of soot.

The storm.

"I accept," I said.

I heard him exhale.

"I accept on one condition. I will go to Gabriel myself. I will tell him. I will do it to his face." I turned. "I will not have him learn it from Corbett."

"Eleanor—"

"It is my price."

He studied me. Then, slowly, he nodded.

"As you wish."

"Alone."

"Corbett will be at the gate."

"Let him."

I crossed the room, and took up the warrant, and tore it in half.

Adrian watched me do it. He did not move.

I tore it again, and dropped the pieces on the carpet.

"You will not need that," I said.

---

I did it at noon.

I walked the cliff path in the full light of a colorless day, with the wind beginning to rise and the first white horses breaking beyond the Teeth. Corbett followed at a distance. I did not look back. When I reached the cottage, Gabriel was at the door, in his shirtsleeves, with a smile already breaking on his face.

I did not let it finish.

"I cannot see you again," I said.

His smile stopped.

"I have promised to marry Adrian Ashcombe on Saturday. I came to tell you so." My voice was flat and level and cold. "I have done a great deal of thinking. You are a good man, Gabriel. But you are a man who lets go. And I do not think I could love someone who lets go."

He stood quite still.

"Eleanor—"

"I need a husband who will hold on. Who will stay." I lifted my chin. "Adrian has stayed. For twelve years. He has asked for nothing. I find that I admire it."

"You don't mean this."

"I do."

"You said last night—"

"I said a great many things last night." I could feel Corbett's eyes on my back, a physical pressure between the shoulder blades. "I was tired. I was grateful. I mistook one for the other."

"You're lying."

"I am not."

"Look at me."

I looked.

I have done many hard things. I have played Brahms with a broken finger. I have buried my father. I have stood on a platform in a black dress and watched a coffin go down. But I have never done anything as hard as that: stand on the step of that whitewashed cottage in the thin wind and look into the eyes of the man I loved, and say, in the voice of a stranger, "Goodbye."

His face did not change.

That was the worst of it. It simply went out, like a lamp, all at once. The light left it. The color left it. He stood there with his arms at his sides, and he said, in a perfectly steady voice:

"I understand."

"Gabriel—"

"I understand. You're right. I'm a man who lets go." He bowed his head. "I wish you happy."

He stepped inside.

The door closed.

I stood there with my hand against my mouth, and I did not weep. I could not. If I had wept, Corbett would have seen. So I stood, and breathed, and counted: *one, two, three, four.* I counted to forty.

Then I turned and walked back the way I had come.

And behind me, through the closed door, faint but unmistakable, I heard a sound I had never heard from him. A low, cracked, animal sound. The sound of a man who has been struck in the heart and does not yet know how to fall.

I did not turn around.

I did not turn around, and I did not stop, and I did not stop until I reached the stile, where I put both hands on the rail and bent double and was sick in the grass.

Corbett came up behind me.

"All right, miss?"

I straightened. I wiped my mouth.

"Quite all right, Mr. Corbett," I said. "Walk me home."
