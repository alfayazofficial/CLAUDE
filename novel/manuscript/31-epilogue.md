# Epilogue
## The Sound of Their Names

*Penhallow, 1934*

Margaret Thorne came to Vale House on the first of September, in a motor-car borrowed from her husband's brother, with a trunk of sheet music and a letter from a solicitor in Penzance.

She was forty-three. She had her father's gray eyes and her grandmother's way of standing, and she taught the piano to the daughters of bank managers in Plymouth. She had never lived on the island. Her father had refused to speak of it, except on the rarest of evenings, with a glass of rum in his hand and his eyes on the fire, and then only in pieces, as one hands a child something that might cut.

He had died in the spring. He was seventy. The cough had finally taken him, thirty years late.

In his will he had left her three things: a red wool scarf, a battered tin of brass buttons, and a key.

"What does it open?" she had asked the solicitor.

"A house," he had said. "On Penhallow. It was left to your father by a Mrs. Hester Crowe, in nineteen-twelve, on the condition that it should pass to the first Margaret in the family who could play." He had paused. "I am instructed to say that the piano is included."

---

The house had been empty twenty-two years.

It stood gray and enormous on its headland, with ivy to the second story and the sea-wind in its chimneys. Half the slates were gone. The garden was a jungle of lavender gone woody, nettles, a collapsed greenhouse. But the door opened to the key, and the hall, when she stepped into it, smelled of salt and dust and old roses, and she stood a long time on the marble with her hand on her mouth.

Two portraits hung above the fireplace, cracked and brown with damp.

A man, heavy and fair, with a weak mouth.

A woman in white, with pearls, and dark hair pinned loosely at the nape of her neck. She had a smile that did not reach her eyes. The painter had caught the mouth and missed the sadness.

*Grandmother,* thought Margaret, absurdly.

She was not related to her. She was not related to anyone in the house. But she had a photograph in a tin box, a small brown carte de visite from Penzance, and the woman in it was laughing, and she stood beneath the portrait for a long time and tried to make the two faces agree.

---

The piano was in the music room.

It was a Broadwood grand, rosewood, with the ivory gone the color of old teeth. The lid was closed. Under the lid, when she lifted it, the strings were slack and green. The felts were moth-eaten. The wooden frame had warped.

She sat down on the bench.

She had been playing since she was four. She could play in a train, in a bath, in the dark, in the heart of a quarrel. She put her hands on the keys. They were cold. She struck the low A, and it gave back a sound like a drowned bell.

She laughed.

"Well," she said aloud. "That's torn it."

She would have to get a man in from Truro to restring it. She would have to get the whole house in from Truro. She picked up her hands, and lifted the lid, and bent to see the damage.

A page fluttered down.

It had been tucked into the groove where the lid met the frame, so carefully that she would never have seen it from above. A single sheet of music. Schubert, an old Peters edition, yellow as parchment, soft as a cloth. She smoothed it on her knee.

*Impromptu in G-flat, Op. 90, No. 3.*

The margin was thick with pencil.

Two hands had written there. One was a firm upright script, in a strong black lead, that she took for a teacher's. It said, in the margin by the first bar, *Slower. Let the left hand sing.*

The other was fainter.

She turned the page toward the window.

---

*Reading the diary was meant to give me a name. Instead it gave me a debt.*

*That is the thing nobody tells you about the dead. They leave you what they could not finish. And they do it so lovingly that you cannot refuse.*

Below this, in the same pencil, but in a different light, as if written at another hour, another day, another weather:

*6th October. Eight in the evening. I am about to climb out of a window. I have a rope of bedsheets and a kitchen knife and no plan. Mrs. Crowe has the poker. If I do not come home, then this is the last thing I shall write in this house, and I would like it to be true.*

*I do not know how the story ends. I think it ends badly. I have thought so since the first night, when the light went out. But I have discovered something unexpected. I am not afraid.*

*My mother wrote that she was not brave. She was wrong, and I will not let her be right. She was the bravest person I ever knew, and I am going to do one small thing tonight and then another, and another, like picking up coins on a road.*

*Here is what the island will want to forget. I set it down so that it cannot.*

*That for sixty years, it killed strangers for their cargo.*

*That the men who did it sat in the front pews.*

*That the women who knew kept silent, and the children who saw were told they dreamed.*

*That a woman named Margaret Vale found the names and put hers beside them.*

*That a man named Gabriel Thorne held her wrist and let go, and spent twelve years learning to hold on.*

*That a man named Adrian Ashcombe was not his father, and did not know it until too late.*

*That I loved a man in a lighthouse. That I loved him in the morning, in the dark, in a room with the shutters closed, in a crypt in a gale. That he was frightened and kind and tired and entirely mine. That it was the best thing I ever did.*

*That none of it was a mistake.*

*If you are reading this, I did not come back, and the light was kept. I shall have asked someone to see to the rest.*

*Do not mourn. Please do not mourn. The sea is not cruel. It is only large, and it keeps what we give it.*

*One more thing. I am writing it in the margin, over the repeat, where the right hand comes in. It is the only place I could find room.*

*Play something for me.*

*E.V.*

---

Margaret Thorne read it three times.

Then she put the page on the stand, and opened her hands on the keys.

She did not tune them. She did not think. The slack strings groaned and slid, and the first notes came out flat and sour, a ghost of a melody. But she played on. She played the Schubert, the opening bars, the long singing line of the right hand over the rippling left, a stream running under ice. And the Broadwood, which had been shut up in the dark for twenty-two years, did something she had never known an instrument to do.

It remembered.

The strings steadied. The pitch rose. The warped frame shuddered and, for a few bars, held. The notes came out round and dark and full, the sound of a rich old voice, and the whole bare room filled with it, and went out through the open window, over the lavender and the cliffs and the wide gray sea.

On the quay below, an old man mending a net lifted his head.

He was seventy-six. He was a Hocking, and he had once, on a stormy night, put down a cudgel. He stood quite still, with the needle in his hand and the twine hanging, and he listened, and his face worked, and he could not have said what he heard.

A woman in a doorway put down her basket.

Two boys on the harbor wall stopped fighting.

And out beyond the Teeth, where the old gray tower stood roofless on its headland with its gallery long gone and its lens long since removed to a museum in Truro, a single shaft of late afternoon sun came out of the clouds and struck the empty lantern room. For a moment, just a moment, the broken glass lit up from within, white and gold and burning.

Margaret did not see it.

She had her eyes shut. She was playing for a woman she had never met, in a room she had never seen, in a house that was not hers. And the tide was coming in over the rocks below, as it had come in every day for a thousand years, slowly, a long dark whisper over stone.

It covered the Teeth.

It covered the wreck of the gallery.

It covered a vault under a ruined abbey, and a wall of names, and a sealed slab of limestone with a posy of sea-pinks long since dust.

And it kept them, as it kept them all.

*The End*
