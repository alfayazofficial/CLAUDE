# Chapter Five
## The Room Beneath the Stairs

*Eleanor*

I went down with a candle in one hand and my skirts in the other, and the steps were slick with age.

There were eleven of them. I counted. They curved into the dark under the great staircase, in a stairwell so narrow my shoulders brushed the walls, and the air that rose to meet me smelled of tallow, damp stone, and something else, something sweet and old that I could not name.

At the bottom, a door.

It was not locked.

I stood with my hand on the iron latch and listened to my own breathing. *For when you are ready.* I was not ready. I do not think anyone ever is. But I had spent twelve years being not ready, and it had taught me nothing but how to wait.

I pushed.

The room was small, perhaps twelve feet square, and it had been a study.

A desk. A lamp. A reading chair with a shawl still draped over the arm, the wool gone gray with dust. Shelves of books on three walls; on the fourth, a chart table, and above the table, pinned with brass tacks, a vast sheet of paper covered in my mother's hand.

I went to it as one goes to a bed where someone lies sleeping.

It was a map of the island.

Not the sort printed for visitors. This was a working map, drawn in ink and annotated in pencil, with every cove and cliff and reef named. The Mare's Teeth were there, a ragged line of black marks off the eastern headland. The lighthouse. The abbey. Penhallow. Vale House. And scattered across the water, in tiny red crosses, nineteen marks, each with a date written beside it.

I read them.

*14 Nov. 1863.*

*2 Mar. 1866.*

*30 Sept. 1871.*

*19 Jan. 1874.*

On and on, down the years, in a hand that grew steadier as it went. The last cross was dated *28 Sept. 1879.*

The night she died.

I put the candle down.

---

Nineteen crosses. Nineteen dates. Beside each, a tiny notation: the name of a vessel, the tonnage, the port she had sailed from.

*Anne Marie, brig, Bristol.*

*Constant, schooner, Falmouth.*

*Sparrowhawk, brig, Cardiff. 14 souls.*

I turned to the shelves.

Ledgers. Dozens of them, bound in cloth and labeled in the same hand by year. I took down the nearest and opened it, and it was a book of weather. Wind, tide, moon, barometer. Hour by hour, night by night. A woman had sat in this room and recorded every storm that struck the island for sixteen years.

And beside each storm, a second column.

*Light: burning.*

*Light: burning.*

*Light: burning.*

*Light: DARK.*

I looked up. I looked at the map. I looked at the red crosses.

*Light: DARK.*

The pages went pale in my hands.

It does not happen in one stroke, understanding. It comes in layers, like the tide coming in over flat sand, so slowly that you cannot say when you began to drown. I thought of the shipwrecks I had heard of as a girl, the poor drowned sailors, the way my father said *the Teeth took her* as if the rocks were a beast with a will. I thought of the Michaelmas supper. The beams in the roof, salvaged from ships.

The beams in the Assembly Room.

The silver candlesticks on my father's sideboard, the lacquered chest in the hall, the Chinese bowl my mother would not allow us to use. Cargo. Every one. Dragged up from the foam and the rocks and the drowned.

I sat down heavily in the reading chair, and a puff of dust rose around me.

"Oh, Mother," I said aloud. "What did you find?"

---

There were letters in the desk drawer.

Not mine. Not hers. Drafts, scored with crossings-out, addressed to men whose names I knew: the Bishop of Truro, the Board of Trade, a magistrate at Penzance. The first was dated June of that last year. *Sirs, I write with the greatest hesitation, and with the knowledge that you will think me mad.*

The second was blotted with something that might have been tears.

The third was not a draft at all. It was a clean copy, sealed, stamped, and never sent. Addressed to the Reverend Thomas Blackwood. Beneath the seal she had written, in small neat letters: *To be delivered by hand. Not by post. Not by any servant of this house.*

I did not open it.

I held it for a long while, and then I put it back, because I knew what it would tell me, and because I could not yet bear to be told.

At the bottom of the drawer lay something else.

A small velvet bag, drawn shut with a cord.

I loosened it and tipped it into my hand, and nine pearls rolled into my palm, round and flawless and cold.

I counted them twice.

Nine.

On my mother's portrait upstairs, the necklace had been strung with ten.

---

I do not know how long I sat there.

At some point the candle began to gutter, and I took a fresh one from the shelf and lit it from the stub. The flame steadied. In its light I saw, behind the chart table, a narrow door I had not noticed, set into the stone and no taller than my shoulder. Its iron hinges were fresh with oil.

I bent to it.

It was bolted from this side.

Beyond it, I could hear air moving. Slow. Cold. A long, low exhalation, like something breathing in a cave.

I drew the bolt.

The door swung inward onto a passage cut in the living rock. The walls sweated. The floor sloped away into the dark. And at the edge of my candle's glow, three paces in, there was a footprint in the damp.

A man's boot. Large. Fresh.

Beside it, a smear of wax, still soft.

My candle shook.

*Someone has been here.*

*Someone has been here today.*

I thought of the footsteps outside my bedroom door. The slow, deliberate tread. I thought of the note that had come in the night, the damp paper, the unfamiliar hand. I thought: *The house is not empty. It has never been empty. It has other doors than the ones I know.*

From somewhere far down the passage, a sound drifted back to me.

A cough.

Low, rasping, human.

Then a scuff of leather on stone. Then nothing.

I stood in the mouth of the tunnel with my heart in my throat and my mother's pearls in my fist, and I did not go in.

I am not brave. People say I am. I am only stubborn, which looks the same from outside.

"Who's there?" I said.

The tunnel swallowed my voice and gave back nothing.

I waited a full minute. Then, very softly, I closed the little door, shot the bolt, and backed away.

The reading chair creaked as I passed it.

On the arm lay the shawl, and under the shawl, something I had missed: a small book, bound in black cloth, its spine unmarked. I took it up. It was warm, as though a hand had just left it.

I opened it to the first page.

*This is the private diary of Margaret Vale. If you are not Margaret Vale, you should stop reading. If you are Eleanor, I am so sorry.*

I closed it.

I opened it again.

I read the first line, and the second. Then I heard, high above me, the heavy tread of boots in the entrance hall.

"Eleanor?"

Adrian's voice.

"Eleanor, my dear. Mrs. Crowe says you've vanished. Are you in the house?"

I blew out the candle.

In the dark I stood very still, holding my mother's diary against my heart, and listened to the man I was going to marry walk across the hall above my head, back and forth, back and forth, slow and unhurried.

He stopped.

Directly above the green door.

I did not breathe.

Then, as slowly as he had come, he went away.
