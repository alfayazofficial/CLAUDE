# Chapter Twenty-Six
## The Confession

*Adrian*

They were standing at the window when I came up the path.

I saw them through the glass, two dark heads close together, her hand in his. I saw the lamplight on her wet hair. I saw her turn, and look at me, and not look away.

I stopped at the garden gate. Behind me the six men halted in a ragged line, lanterns swinging, the rain hissing off their hats. Corbett stood at my shoulder. I could smell the wet wool of him, and the tobacco, and under it the faint sweetish smell of a man who has done this before.

"Wait here," I said.

"Sir—"

"Wait."

I went on alone.

The door of the cottage opened before I reached it. Thorne stood on the threshold with the light behind him, bareheaded, in his shirtsleeves, with a short iron bar in his right hand. He did not raise it. He only held it, loose and ready, the way a man holds a tool.

"Mr. Ashcombe."

"Mr. Thorne."

"You said you wouldn't stop them."

"I said a great many things."

"You gave her your word."

"Yes," I said. "I am told it is worth very little."

Behind him, in the small bright room, Eleanor rose from the hearth. She had a blanket round her shoulders and her boots in her hand. She looked at me, and I saw in her face not rage, and not fear, but something that made my breath catch.

Grief.

For me.

"Adrian," she said. "Come in out of the rain."

---

I sat at their table.

It was an absurd thing to do. I sat at the keeper's table with my wet oilskin dripping on the flagstones, and a mug of hot tea in front of me that I did not touch, while the storm shook the windows and the lamp turned overhead and two people I had wronged stood watching me from opposite sides of a stove. Thorne had put the bar on the shelf. He had not put it far.

"You've come to put out the light," Eleanor said.

"Yes."

"Why tell us?"

"Because you deserve to hear it first."

She said nothing.

"I have brought six men," I said. "They are outside. They have orders to take the keeper and the lady into custody at my word. Corbett will go up the tower. I will go with him. At eleven fifty-two I will turn the key, as it has been turned on this night every year since I was a boy."

"There are four men on that boat."

"Five. The Reverend, young Trevennick, the Thorne boy, and two hands from the harbor."

"They will die."

"Very likely."

"Adrian."

"I know." I looked at my hands. "I have known since I left Marlowe House. I knew when I burned the diary. I knew, I think, when I signed your confession and put it in your drawer. I have known my whole life what I am."

"What are you?" said Gabriel.

I raised my eyes.

He was standing very straight, his hands at his sides, his gray gaze fixed on my face. There was no anger in it. That was the strange thing. Only a kind of patient, appalled attention, such as a surgeon gives to a wound.

"I am my father's son," I said.

---

I told them.

I had not known I would. I had come up the cliff with a speech prepared, short and cold, an ultimatum. But as I sat at that table and Eleanor's eyes rested on me, quiet and steady, the speech went out of my head like water down a drain. And what rose in its place was the truth, all of it, in the order it had happened, as simply as a man might read a ledger.

"I was twenty-two. My father came to Marlowe House at nine on the night of the twenty-eighth and said, *Come.* I came. I did not ask why. I had never asked why.

"We walked the cliff in the storm. He talked the whole way. About the island. About duty. About how a gentleman's first obligation is to the people he commands. He said it as one says the Creed. When we reached the lighthouse he took out the key and showed me, once more, the three turns, and I watched the lamp die.

"A little after eleven, Margaret Vale came up the stair.

"I had never seen her so. She was white, soaked, with her cloak half off and her hands wrapped in rags. She looked at my father and said, *I will light it.* And he said, *Margaret, be reasonable.*

"And he struck her."

I stopped. The kettle sang. Somewhere above us the great lens whirred.

"I was at the head of the stair," I said. "I saw it. I saw him hit a woman I had known all my life, who had given me barley sugar when I was seven, who had taught me my notes. I saw her stagger. I saw the rail. I saw the boy come up through the hatch, and fling himself across the boards."

I looked at Thorne.

"I saw you take her wrist."

He did not move.

"I saw you hold her. I saw your face. I heard her speak to you. I could not hear the words, but I saw her lips." My voice shook. "I saw her smile. And I saw you let go, and I knew, in that instant, what I'd always known. That the thing I'd been told was a lie. That a man may be a coward without being a villain, and a villain without being cruel. And that my father, who had never raised his voice to me, was the most dangerous creature on earth."

"Adrian—" said Eleanor.

"Let me finish."

She bowed her head.

"I went down the rocks," I said. "I did not run. I think that is the one thing I did right. I went down, and I stood in the sea to my waist, and I found her. I pulled her out. I carried her up the cliff to the grass and wrapped her in my coat. And I looked up, and there was a white shape in the window of Vale House, and I knew who it was.

"I thought: *She will remember me.*

"And I thought: *She will be right to.*"

---

I paused.

I wanted, very badly, to stop there. It was a good place to stop. It made a story. A boy, frightened, who did the one decent thing in a night of cowardice. If I stopped there, she would have forgiven me, I think. In time.

But I was done with stories.

"There is one thing more," I said. "I have never told it. I have not told a priest. I have not told God."

Eleanor lifted her head.

"Penhallow," I said.

Silence.

"My father did not die by accident. You know that, I think. You have always known." I felt my mouth twist. "He had been ill for a year. Consumption. He knew it. He drank to ease the pain, and the drink made him cruel. The night he died was the eleventh of December. Eleven weeks after your mother.

"I came home at ten. He was in the east wing, in the library, drunk, with a lamp. He was reading the ledger, the *copy,* the one he had kept for himself. He looked up and said, *Ah, there you are. We must talk about the girl.*

"Eleanor. I mean you.

"He said you were a danger. That you had been at the window. That you had seen. He said, *The Vale girl will have to be dealt with. Discreetly.* He said it exactly as he had said it of your mother."

Her face had gone gray.

"I said, *No.*

"He said, *Adrian—*

"I said, *No. Not her. Not again.*

"And he laughed.

"He stood up. He swayed. He took a step toward me with the lamp in his hand, and the lamp tipped. I saw the oil spill. I saw it catch on the Turkey carpet. I saw the flame run along the floor to the curtains, and up the curtains to the ceiling, and my father standing in the middle of it all, laughing."

I breathed.

"I could have pulled him out. It was twelve feet to the door. I was strong. I was young. I could have taken him by the collar."

"You didn't," said Gabriel, very low.

"I stood in the doorway," I said. "I watched. I counted to sixty. I told myself I was waiting for him to come. And when I could not hear his laughter any longer, I closed the door.

"I closed it. I turned the key.

"And I walked out of the house into the garden, and found you at the gate in your black dress. And I said I would never let anything hurt you."

The only sound was the rain.

I had expected a horror. I had braced myself for it, as for a blow. But when I looked up, Eleanor was not recoiling. She stood quite still, with her hand against her mouth, and tears running down her face, and I understood that she was not weeping for him.

She was weeping for me.

---

"Adrian," she said at last. "Why are you here?"

"To put out the light."

"No."

"Eleanor—"

"You don't want to. You didn't want to at the Assembly Room, or in the library, or in the study with the pistol. You've never wanted to. You've been looking for someone to stop you since the day you were fifteen." She took a step toward me. "Why tell us, if you mean to go on?"

"Because I do not know how to stop."

It was the truest thing I have ever said.

"I have a key in my pocket and a man at my back and a lifetime of habit in my hands. I have been told what to do since I was a child. I have *done* it. I have sat in the magistrate's chair and done it." I stood. "Do you think I have not tried? Do you think I have not stood at that window every night for twelve years and thought, *tonight, I will not?* And in the morning I wrote the order."

"You didn't write it this year."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because you were coming home."

She looked at me for a long time.

"Then don't," she said.

"I cannot."

"You can."

"I *cannot.*" My voice cracked. "Eleanor, I am going to be hanged. If that boat reaches Bodmin, I shall stand in the dock and hear my father's crimes read out, and mine, and they will take me to Bodmin gaol and break my neck. I will die in disgrace, and the name of Ashcombe will be spat upon in every harbor in Cornwall. And you will be *free.* You will go away with him, and have children, and play the piano, and in fifty years you will say, *Oh yes, I knew a man once.*"

"That's not—"

"It is exactly so."

"Adrian."

"So I have decided," I said, "that I would rather you hate me."

Thorne moved then.

He took one step forward, between us, quite deliberately, and stood with his hands open.

"Mr. Ashcombe," he said. "You are not your father."

"No?"

"A man who is his father's son does not sit at a kitchen table and confess to the woman he loves." He regarded me steadily. "Whatever you do tonight, you have already done the hardest thing. You have said it. Don't throw that away."

I stared at him.

I had hated this man for six weeks. I had hated him with a purity that had warmed me through the long nights. And here he stood, the man who had held Margaret Vale's wrist and let go, who had taken my father's money and sold his silence, who had taken the woman I loved and the life I could not have, and he was speaking to me with a kind of tenderness.

It was unbearable.

"Corbett," I said.

The door opened.

"Sir."

"Take them."

---

I will not describe it.

I will say that they came in quickly, three of them, with their lanterns and their boots, and that Thorne fought. He struck one man to the floor with the iron bar and broke another's wrist. For a few moments the little room was a roaring chaos of limbs and shouted curses and the splintering of furniture. Eleanor was against the wall, her hand at her throat. I stood by the table, quite still, with my father's pistol in my hand, and did not know when I had drawn it.

"Enough," I said.

No one stopped.

"*Enough.*"

I fired into the ceiling.

The report was enormous. Plaster rained down. The room froze.

"Tie him."

They bound Thorne's hands behind his back with a length of cord. He stood under their grip, bleeding from a cut above his eye, and looked at me.

"You'll regret it," he said.

"I regret everything."

"I mean tonight."

"I know."

I turned to Eleanor.

She had not moved. She stood at the wall in her blanket, her bare feet on the cold stone, and her eyes were enormous.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I truly am. You'll be kept here, with two men at the door, until it is over. No one will harm you."

"Adrian—"

"And in the morning, I'll come and tell you how it ended."

She said nothing.

I pocketed the pistol. I took the key from my coat, the heavy iron key with the notch like a bird's beak, and held it in my palm. It was warm.

I went out into the storm.

Behind me, through the door, I heard her say, quite softly, not to me but to the man in the cords:

"Gabriel. We have fifty minutes."

And his voice answering, steady, almost gentle:

"Then let's not waste them."
