# Chapter Eighteen
## The False Confession

*Adrian*

Corbett told me at breakfast.

He stood at the end of my dining table, hat in hand, while I buttered a piece of toast I had no intention of eating. He told it plainly, in the flat voice of a man reporting a change in the weather. Miss Vale had gone up the cliff path at dusk. She had not come down. At first light she had left the cottage with the keeper. They had stood on the step for some minutes. They had not touched.

"Not touched," I repeated.

"No, sir."

"But she had spent the night."

"The lamp burned. The shutters were closed. At two in the morning I saw a figure at the window." His small eyes did not waver. "A woman's, sir."

I laid down the knife.

I had known. I think I had known since the day I saw them on the headland through a glass. But there is knowing and there is *being told,* and the second is a different animal. It came into my chest and sat there and put its claws in.

"Thank you, Corbett."

"Will there be anything else?"

"No."

"I might remark, sir," he said, "that the keeper's position is not secure. The Board of Trade has a standing complaint against him. Moral conduct. A word from the magistrate—"

"I said thank you."

He bowed, and went out.

I sat for a long time with my hands flat on the cloth. A clock ticked. Sunlight lay on the silver. And I understood, quite calmly, with the cold clarity of a man contemplating a surgical amputation, that there was one thing left that I could do.

I could tell her the truth.

Not all of it. I am not a fool. But enough.

---

I called at four, as appointed.

She received me in the library of Vale House. She had chosen it deliberately, I think: a long room paneled in oak, lined with Josiah's unread books, with a leaded window that looked out on the sea. She sat at the writing table with her back to the light, and there was nothing on the blotter but a pen.

She had not slept. I could see it. But there was a new quality in her, a kind of luminous stillness, like water just after rain. It cut me to the heart.

"Eleanor."

"Adrian. Please sit."

I sat.

She did not offer tea. She did not offer anything. She regarded me across the table, and I was struck, as I had been struck at the supper, by how much she looked like her mother. The same level brow. The same unflinching eyes.

"You said you would tell me everything you could," she said.

"I did."

"I've come to hold you to it."

I drew a breath.

"There is a paper in my pocket," I said. "I wrote it last night. It is a statement, and I have signed it, and had Corbett witness the signature. I wish you to take it."

"What is it?"

"A confession."

She did not move.

I took the sheet from my coat. Four pages, closely written in my own hand. I laid them on the blotter and turned them so that she could read, and I watched her lower her eyes to the first line.

*I, Adrian Thomas Ashcombe, Magistrate for the Isle of Penhallow, do hereby declare...*

She read for a long time.

---

I will set down what I wrote, because I wrote it with great care and I think it was the best lie of my life.

I wrote that on the evening of the eleventh of April, 1885, I had been approached by a man named Lazarus Quick, a smuggler out of Roscoff, who offered fifty guineas for a single night's blackout of the lighthouse. That the cargo was brandy. That the revenue cutter was due off the headland, and that the darkness would let the lugger slip in unseen. That I had agreed, God forgive me, believing no other ship was expected.

That I had given the order to Corbett. That Corbett had carried it to Pennick. That Pennick had refused. That Corbett's men had, in the scuffle, struck the old man, and he had gone over the rail of the gallery. That they had put out the lamp themselves.

That at a quarter to midnight, a naval frigate had come in out of the storm.

That two hundred and eleven men had died.

I wrote that I was guilty of manslaughter, or something worse. That I would submit myself to the Assizes whenever she bid me. That I would resign the magistracy. That I would make restitution to the families of the dead, to the last farthing.

I wrote that I was sorry.

It was all true.

Except that it was not. Except that the lamp had gone out on the *Halcyon* by the same three turns of the same key that had put out the *Sparrowhawk,* in the same way, by the same hand. Except that I had not given the order in the smuggler's name. Except that Quick had been a fiction invented by my steward and, I now suspected, by a man much deeper in my own pay than I had ever admitted to myself.

The truth was uglier. The truth was that the habit of my father's house had never left me. That in the spring of 1885 the island had been poor, and the harbor dues low, and Corbett had come to me with a ledger of expenses and a suggestion. *One night, sir. Just one. A ship we won't miss.*

I had said yes.

I had not asked which ship.

---

She read it twice. When she had finished, she squared the sheets against the blotter and laid her hand on top.

"Thank you," she said.

"You believe it?"

"I believe you believe it."

My stomach clenched. "What do you mean?"

"I mean, Adrian, that you have confessed to the *Halcyon,*" Eleanor said quietly. "And you have been kind enough to explain it as a mistake. A smuggler. A misunderstanding with a steward." She tapped the page. "But you haven't said a word about my mother."

"I have told you all I—"

"You told me you were on the stair. You told me you heard a cry. You told me you carried her." She looked up. "This says nothing of the lighthouse. Nothing of the key. Nothing of the *Sparrowhawk.* You've confessed to the lesser crime and left the greater out."

"Because I don't know—"

"Don't."

It was the quietest word I have ever heard.

"I have been lied to," Eleanor said, "by an expert. I have been lied to in the kindest way imaginable, by a man who loved me. I recognize the technique." She turned the pages over. "This is very good. It is very nearly true. And it is a confession that will hang Corbett, and ruin your name, and save the one thing you actually care about."

"Which is?"

"Your father's grave."

I stared.

"You cannot bear to say it," she said. "Not about him. Not out loud. You've been loyal to him all your life; it is the only fidelity you've ever had. So you've given me the *Halcyon,* which is yours, and kept the *Sparrowhawk,* which is his. And I think—" She stopped. Her voice shook. "I think there is something else under that, which you have not even told yourself."

I stood.

"You have no right—"

"I have every right. It was my mother."

"She was a woman who—"

"Careful," said Eleanor.

I stopped.

It was the way she said it. Not loud. Not angry. A single quiet word, with all the weight of the dead behind it.

I sat down again.

I do not know how long we remained like that. The clock struck the quarter. A cart passed on the road below, creaking. In the grate a coal shifted and fell.

At last she lifted the confession from the table.

"I'll keep this," she said.

"I meant you to."

"As surety?"

"As proof of my good faith."

"It's a start." She slid the pages into the drawer of the writing table and turned the key. "You'll release Dr. Reed."

"I can't."

"You can, and you will."

"Eleanor, he is under my protection. If I let him go, Corbett's men will—"

"Then keep him. But I'll see him. Today. In the cellar, or the study, wherever you like. Alone."

I thought about it.

I thought about the Reed I had left on a pallet bed, in the cool dark, with his bag of papers and a flagon of water. A shaking, wizened man who had wept when I came down. *Don't give me to Corbett, Adrian,* he'd said. *I'll tell you anything.* And I had said, *I know.*

"Tomorrow," I said. "Midday."

"Tonight."

"Eleanor."

"Tonight, Adrian."

I looked at her. At the straight back, the clear eyes, the mouth set in a line of such weary determination that I wanted to weep.

"Tonight," I said.

---

I stood in the hall, afterward, buttoning my coat.

Mrs. Crowe appeared at my elbow with my hat. She handed it to me without a word.

"Mrs. Crowe."

"Sir."

"How is she?"

"As you see."

"Does she sleep?"

"Not much."

"Does she eat?"

"Enough."

I put on my hat. I stood with my hand on the door.

"You have known her since she was born," I said. "You know her better than I."

"Better than anyone, I think."

"Then tell me." I could not look at her. "Is there anything left in her that is not grief?"

The old woman was silent.

"There's a great deal, sir," she said at last. "But none of it's yours."

I nodded.

I think I had expected it. I think I had known it since the day she came home and I saw her step from the carriage in her black traveling dress with the rain on her face, and I understood in a flash of unbearable clarity that the girl I had kept in my heart for twelve years was gone. That a woman had come back instead, a stranger, a magnificent stranger, who did not need me.

I went out.

The evening was clear. The first stars came out over the sea, and the light on the headland began to turn.

I walked down the drive and through the gate and stood in the road for a long moment, looking up at the lighthouse.

*She loves him.*

I had never said it to myself in words.

*She loves him, and she is going to take him from me, and there is nothing I can do.*

I laughed.

It was a terrible sound. It startled a rook from the hedge. I stood in the road with the wind tugging my coat and laughed until the tears ran, and then I wiped my face with my sleeve and went home to Marlowe House, and took out my father's old pistol, and cleaned it.

Slowly.

Thoroughly.

With the tenderness of a man preparing for a long journey.
