# Chapter Twenty-Seven
## The Last Night

*Gabriel*

They left two men to watch us.

One sat on the step outside, with his back to the door and a lantern between his boots. The other stood at the window, a big silent fisherman named Hocking, with a cudgel under his arm. Neither spoke. Corbett had gone up the tower with Ashcombe. We could hear them on the stair, a slow ringing climb, boots on iron, fading into the roar of the storm.

I sat on the floor with my wrists bound behind me.

They had tied me to the leg of the stove. It was a cruel little irony. I could feel the iron hot against my spine, and the cord biting into my wrists, and my own blood running warm down my face from the cut above my eye. Eleanor sat beside me on the flagstones, with her blanket around her, free. They had not thought it necessary to bind a woman.

She leaned her head against my shoulder.

"Don't move," she said, very quietly. "Hocking's watching."

"I wasn't going to."

"I'm going to look as though I'm weeping."

"Are you?"

"Not yet."

Her fingers were already moving behind me. I felt them find the knot, small and cold and deft, picking at the cord. She worked without a sound, her face turned into my collar, her breath stirring my neck. To anyone watching, she was a woman in despair, clinging to a man. I felt each loop give.

"Where did you learn this?" I murmured.

"Sailors' knots. My father taught me. I was seven."

"He taught you to untie a man?"

"He taught me to tie a bowline. This is the same thing, backward."

I almost laughed.

"Forty-nine minutes," she breathed.

---

It took her six.

When the last coil fell loose I held my wrists together behind me, still, as though bound, and waited for my heart to quiet. Hocking had not turned. The man on the step coughed and spat. Overhead the lamp turned and turned, and the beam swept the walls, and I counted the seconds between each sweep: twelve, twelve, twelve.

"Now?" I whispered.

"Not yet."

"Why?"

"Because we have a little time." She raised her head. In the dim light her face was white and calm and very close to mine. "And I should like to use some of it."

I understood.

"Eleanor—"

"Gabriel."

"We can't."

"We can't do anything else." She smiled, with such tender, rueful, steady courage that I felt my chest crack. "I've counted. It's forty minutes. Twenty to get free, and the stair, and the tower. Perhaps twenty to spare." She drew a breath. "I would like, if it's to be the last, to spend some of it as a person."

"It isn't the last."

"No?"

"No."

"Then it won't matter if we spend it well."

I bent my head.

She kissed me.

It was a small kiss, and a quiet one, in a room full of enemies, with my hands behind my back and a cut on my brow. It tasted of salt and rain and her own breath. Her mouth was soft and certain. She did not close her eyes. She watched me the whole time, and I watched her, and across the inch of air between us something passed that was larger than words.

"Open it," I said, when she drew back.

"What?"

"The letter."

Her face went still.

"It's inside your blanket," I said. "I saw you put it there. Open it. Read it to me. I should like to hear her say it."

"Gabriel—"

"It's what she wanted. And—" My voice failed. I swallowed. "And if I die tonight, I'd like to have heard it. Just once. In her words."

She looked at me for a long time.

Then she reached into the blanket and drew out the little square of paper.

Her fingers were quite steady. She broke the seal with her thumbnail. The wax cracked and fell in a few red flakes to the floor. She unfolded the page. And in a low voice, scarcely louder than the rain, with her head against mine and Hocking's shadow on the wall, she began to read.

---

*My darling Eleanor,*

*If you are reading this, you are a woman grown, and I am not there to see it. Forgive me. I am so sorry. I should have liked to watch you become whatever you are.*

*I do not know what you will have learned by now. I do not know whether you hate your father, or pity him, or have forgotten him entirely. I pray you have not forgotten me.*

*I have been trying to think what a mother ought to say at the end. I have written four pages and burned them all. The truth is I have only one thing to tell you. It is so small that I am almost ashamed.*

*It is this. Be brave in the small things.*

*I was not brave in the large ones. I was frightened of Josiah. I was frightened of the island. I let a great many days go by in a house I knew to be wrong, because it was easier than leaving. When at last I found my courage it came too late, and in an odd shape, and cost me more than I had to give. Do not wait for it to be large. It does not arrive that way. It comes in small pieces, in unremarkable moments, and you must pick it up each time, like a coin you find on the road.*

*Tell the truth when it is easy. Then, when it is hard, you will already know how.*

*Mrs. Crowe will help you. She is the staunchest woman alive. Be kind to her; she has carried more than she shows.*

*Gabriel Thorne will have carried this letter. He is a good man, and he is a frightened one. I wish I could have lived long enough to see him less afraid. If you should find, in the course of things, that you have a tenderness for him, do not be ashamed of it. I would not be.*

*Do not avenge me.*

*I mean that. Do not let the thought of me poison you. Do what is right, because it is right, and because the names on the wall are not the only ones the tide has kept. It has kept mine, too. And it has kept yours, though you do not know it yet.*

*You have my hands. Play something for me.*

*Your loving Mother.*

---

She finished.

The room was very still.

Rain beat on the roof. The lamp turned. Somewhere outside, a long way off, a bell was tolling on the water, thin and silver and brave.

I did not look at her. I could not. I sat with my head bowed and my eyes shut, and the tears ran down my face, and I let them. The cord around my wrists was loose. My hands were free. I did not use them.

"She knew," I said.

"Yes."

"She knew I would be there."

"I think she hoped."

"She said *tenderness.*"

"Yes."

"I would have given anything," I said, "to have her say it to my face."

Eleanor put her arms around me.

She held me against her breast, and her cheek was wet against my hair, and she rocked me gently, back and forth, like a child. And I understood that she was giving me back what her mother had given her. That the whole long inheritance of grief and love and unfinished business had come down in that single letter and been placed in my hands.

"Gabriel."

"Mm."

"I have a thing to say."

"Say it."

"If this goes wrong. If it ends badly. If one of us goes into the sea."

"Eleanor—"

"I want you to know that I would not change it." She drew back and looked into my face. "Not one hour. Not the lighthouse, or the study, or the night in your bed. Not the crypt, or the storm, or the cord on your wrists. If I had it to do again, I'd come back to this island on the same carriage, in the same rain, and walk up the same path, and knock on the same door."

"You'd choose this?"

"I'd choose *you.*" Her voice shook. "Every time. Every road."

I could not answer.

I kissed her again. Her mouth, her eyes, her wet cheeks. She slid her fingers into my hair. And in the warm dim room with the storm roaring outside, I held the woman I loved in my arms, and for the space of a dozen heartbeats, nothing existed in the world but the two of us.

---

*Eleanor*

At eleven forty-one by the clock on the mantel, Hocking turned from the window.

He looked at us.

We were sitting on the floor, side by side, with our heads together, as we had been sitting for half an hour. My eyes were red. Gabriel's face was tracked with tears. We must have looked, I thought, exactly like a pair of beaten lovers waiting for the end.

"It's almost time," said Hocking.

"Yes," I said.

"I'm sorry, miss."

"Are you?"

"My wife's cousin was on the *Sparrowhawk,*" he said slowly. "A cook. Billy Tregear. I never knew till the Reverend told me. Nobody ever told us." He shifted the cudgel. "I'd not have taken the shilling, if I'd known."

I looked at him.

Behind my back, Gabriel's hand closed on mine.

"Mr. Hocking," I said, gently. "Put the cudgel down."

"Miss—"

"Please."

He did not move.

"The lamp goes out at eleven fifty-two," I said. "It's eleven forty-one. In eleven minutes, five men on a boat will be in the channel, in the dark, and they will die. Two are islanders. One is a priest. One has a scar from your own magistrate's whip." I held his eyes. "You have a choice. It's very small. But it's yours."

He stared at me.

His lips moved.

Then, with a kind of slow grinding reluctance, like a man laying down a stone he has carried for years, he bent and set the cudgel on the floor.

"What do I do?" he whispered.

"Open the door," said Gabriel, rising.

He stood. The cord fell from his wrists. He rolled his shoulders, and flexed his hands, and the lamp-light fell on his face, battered and bloody and quite transformed.

I had never loved him so much.

"Hocking. Tie up the other fellow on the step. Quietly. Then come with us."

"And Mr. Ashcombe?"

Gabriel's eyes met mine.

"Mr. Ashcombe," he said, "is a man who's about to be asked a question. And I think—" His voice softened. "I think he's been waiting a very long time for someone to ask it."
