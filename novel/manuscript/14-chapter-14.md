# Chapter Fourteen
## The Missing Witness

*Gabriel*

Dr. Silas Reed had agreed to meet me at dusk.

I had written him a note, two lines, and sent it by the Penrose boy. *Eleanor Vale knows. She wishes to hear it from you. Come to the lighthouse on Thursday after sundown, and bring the certificate book.* I had expected nothing. Reed was a man who had spent twelve years drinking himself quietly toward the grave, and I had given up hoping he would find a conscience at the bottom of any bottle.

But the boy came back at noon with a single word, written on the back of my own note in a shaking hand.

*Yes.*

By four o'clock I had swept the cottage, built the fire, and set out three chairs. By five I had polished the lens twice. By six, when the sun slid under the edge of the sea and the whole western sky turned the color of an old bruise, I stood on the gallery with my hands on the rail and watched the cliff path.

Eleanor was beside me.

She had come at five, out of breath, with a basket from Mrs. Crowe and a face that told me she had not slept. We had not spoken of the study. We had not spoken of anything. She had stood in the doorway of the cottage for a moment, and I had stood in the middle of the floor, and the memory of her mouth had gone through the room like a bell struck once. Then she had said, *Is he here?* and I had said, *Not yet,* and we had both turned with relief to the business of waiting.

"He's late," she said now.

"He's a doctor. They are never on time."

"He was kind to me, when I was small." She did not look at me. "He used to give me barley sugar from his pocket. He said it was medicine."

"He gave it to me, too."

She turned her head.

"The first week," I said. "Before I knew what I was apprenticed to. I thought: here is a man who keeps sweets in his coat. I thought him the kindest man alive."

"And now?"

"Now I think kindness is a thing a coward does when he cannot be brave."

She was silent.

Down on the headland, the shadows had thickened to blue. The first star came out over the Teeth. Behind us, in the tower, the lamp turned in its cradle with the low steady whir that had become the pulse of my life, and the beam went out across the water, white, patient, strong.

Seven o'clock.

Eight.

At half past eight, Eleanor said, very quietly, "He isn't coming."

"No."

"Something's happened."

"Perhaps he lost his nerve."

"Perhaps."

She was looking at the dark. I saw in the lamp's reflected glow that her lip was caught between her teeth, and that the fingers on the rail were white.

"I'll go to him," I said.

"We'll go."

"Eleanor—"

"I know what you'll say. It's dangerous. I am more use to you than you think." She stepped away from the rail. "Besides, if he's dead I would like to be told by someone who isn't Mr. Ashcombe."

---

Reed's house stood at the north end of the harbor, a narrow gray building with a brass plate and a blue door.

The door was ajar.

I stopped on the step, and Eleanor stopped behind me. I put out an arm. We listened. Somewhere in the house a clock was ticking; a draught moved the curtain in an upstairs window; a gull stalked along the harbor wall, scraping its claws on the stone. Nothing else.

"Dr. Reed?" I called.

Silence.

I pushed the door wide.

The surgery lay to the left. A small room, cluttered, clean in the way of rooms kept by a man who has outgrown cleaning. Glass jars, a stained leather couch, a bookcase of dusty pharmacopoeias. I knew it as I knew the palm of my hand. It was the room where I had spent a winter learning to sew men's skin.

It had been searched.

Not violently. Whoever had done it had been methodical. Every drawer in the desk pulled halfway and pushed shut again. Every book removed, shaken, replaced a half inch out of line. The leather couch cushions slit along their seams, the horsehair stuffing teased out and tidily stacked.

"They were looking for something," Eleanor whispered.

"The certificate book."

I crossed to the desk. The top drawer was empty. The pen tray, the blotter, the inkwell: all in place. But on the blotter, in the pool of lamplight, lay a pair of spectacles, folded, with one lens cracked across.

I picked them up.

They were warm.

Not from a hand. From the lamp. They'd been lying under the glow for an hour at most. The cracked glass caught the flame and threw back a small bright wound.

"He wouldn't leave without these," I said.

"He's nearly blind without them."

"Then he did not leave."

I looked around the room again. The chair behind the desk was pushed back at an angle, as though someone had risen in a hurry. The coat stand by the door was bare. A dark smear on the edge of the blotter, no larger than my thumbnail, glistened under the lamp.

I touched it with the tip of my finger.

Wet.

I held it to the light.

Red.

---

We searched the house. The kitchen, where a half-eaten supper lay on a plate. The bedroom upstairs, where the bed was made and a nightshirt hung over the chair. The cellar, where forty bottles of port stood in a rack with a few gaps. Nothing.

Nothing, until I came back to the front hall and noticed what I had missed on entering.

His bag was gone.

The black leather bag that had hung on a peg beside the door for twelve years. The one that held his instruments, his stethoscope, his notebook. The one he had never in my memory allowed out of arm's reach.

"He took it," Eleanor said.

"Or it was taken."

"Why would anyone take a doctor's bag?"

"Because of what's in the lining."

She looked at me.

"He sewed the certificate into the lining," I said. "The page from the ledger. The original. Eleven years ago, he said, when he first started to be afraid. A small leather pocket under the false bottom. I helped him cut it." My mouth was dry. "I thought he'd destroyed it. I thought he'd drowned it in whisky like everything else."

"And now—"

"And now somebody has it."

A step sounded on the cobbles outside.

Heavy. Measured. Two men.

Eleanor grasped my sleeve. I put my hand over hers.

"Stay behind me," I said.

---

The man in the doorway was Nathaniel Corbett.

I knew him by his shape before I saw his face. A big square body in a black coat; a wide flat face like a shovel blade; hands the size of dinner plates, scarred to the knuckles from forty years on the quay. He was Lord Ashcombe's steward. He had been Lord Ashcombe's steward for thirty years, and was now, in the nominal sense, the magistrate's. In the real sense, he was no one's. He did what he'd always done, which was whatever was necessary.

Behind him stood a constable in a stiff blue coat, young, pale, with the look of a man who would rather be at home.

"Mr. Thorne," said Corbett.

"Mr. Corbett."

"Miss Vale."

"Good evening," said Eleanor, with a self-possession that startled me. "What brings you here at this hour?"

"A report of disturbance." His small pale eyes slid over the room, taking in the overturned cushions, the open drawers, the spectacles in my hand. "A neighbor heard noise."

"There is no one here."

"So I see." He took a step into the surgery. The boards creaked under his weight. "Dr. Reed's gone off, I understand. This afternoon. On the packet to Falmouth."

"The packet?"

"Left at three. Said he'd a patient on the mainland and might be some days." He smiled, with no part of his face but his mouth. "A man gets tired of the island, now and again."

"He left his spectacles," I said.

"Did he?"

"Cracked. With blood on the blotter."

The constable shifted. Corbett did not.

"A doctor cuts himself shaving," he said gently. "Now, Mr. Thorne, I'll ask you to step outside with me. The magistrate would like a word. And Miss Vale, I'll see you home."

"I'll see myself home."

"Mr. Ashcombe was most particular."

"I'm sure he was."

"It's dark, miss. And the cliff road is bad."

"I walked it this morning, Mr. Corbett." She lifted her chin. "Tell Mr. Ashcombe that I'm quite well, and that I'd be obliged if he'd call on me in the proper way, by day, with a card, like a gentleman."

I thought for a moment that Corbett would take her arm.

He looked at her. Then at me. Then, slowly, as though reading some unwritten line, he stepped back.

"As you say, miss."

"Thank you."

We walked out through the blue door, past the constable, into the harbor night. Behind us I heard Corbett's heavy tread cross the surgery floor, the scrape of a drawer, the dull *thunk* of something put down hard.

We were at the end of the quay before Eleanor let out her breath.

"Did you see his hands?" she said.

"No."

"There was blood on the left cuff."

I stopped walking.

"Fresh," she said. "Under the button. A brown stain the size of a sixpence. He tried to turn his wrist away."

The packet *Sea Swallow,* I remembered, had a rope-burned hull and a captain named Trevennick who owed me a favor for setting his daughter's arm. I stood on the quay in the cold, with the water slapping the stones, and made a decision.

"Go home," I said. "Lock the door. Tell Mrs. Crowe."

"What are you going to do?"

"Ask the captain whether he took on a passenger at three."

"Gabriel."

"Eleanor." I took her hands. "Dr. Reed is the only man alive who can put his name to a certificate that says your mother was struck before she fell. If he's dead, the case is dead. And if he is not dead, then somebody has him, and he has perhaps a day."

She looked at me, her eyes enormous.

Then she kissed me.

Once, quickly, hard on the mouth, in the open street, under the lamp.

"Come back," she said.

"I will."

"If you don't, I swear I shall come after you."

"I believe you."

I watched her go up the road, a small dark figure with her chin high, until she turned the corner of the churchyard wall.

Then I walked down to the boats.

The captain of the *Sea Swallow* told me, with great reluctance, over a mug of rum at the Gannet, that the packet had sailed at four, not three. That she had carried six passengers, all islanders, all known to him by name. That nobody had come aboard with a doctor's bag.

That a man had been seen at the quay at dusk, a stout man in a long coat, arguing with two others.

That the stout man had been put into a cart.

"Whose cart?" I said.

Trevennick looked at his rum.

"I couldn't swear to it," he said at last. "But it was drawn by a gray mare with a white star. And there's only one gray mare with a star on this island."

It belonged to Marlowe House.

It belonged to Adrian Ashcombe.
