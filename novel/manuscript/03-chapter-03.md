# Chapter Three
## A Promise Made in Ashes

*Adrian*

I have always been good at being looked at.

It is a skill like any other. One learns where to stand in a room, how long to hold a glance, when to laugh so that others will laugh with you. My father taught me that. He taught me a great many things, and I have spent most of my adult life trying to decide which of them I am allowed to keep.

The Michaelmas supper was held, as it had been held for two hundred years, in the Assembly Room above the harbor. Long tables. Tallow candles. Goose and cider and the whole island in its best black, crowded shoulder to shoulder beneath beams that had been salvaged from ships.

I did not think about the beams.

I thought about Eleanor.

She sat at my right hand in a gown the color of wet slate, her dark hair drawn back, her mother's eyes lowered to her plate. She had said perhaps forty words since we entered. She had looked at the windows eleven times. I had counted.

"You are quiet," I said.

"I am listening."

"To what?"

"The sea."

"There is a great deal of it, I'm afraid."

She did not smile. Twelve years ago she would have. Twelve years ago she would have laughed and kicked my ankle beneath the table and told me I was insufferable, and I would have gone home and lain awake with joy.

Now she turned her glass by the stem and said, "Mrs. Crowe tells me the light failed on Tuesday night."

I kept my face perfectly still.

It is another skill. I have been practicing it since I was twenty-two.

"The mechanism is old," I said. "I will speak to the Board."

"Mr. Thorne says it was extinguished by hand."

My knife stopped against the plate.

"Mr. Thorne," I said, "says a great many things. He is a man who has spent six years alone with a lamp. One learns to expect eccentricity."

"You have met him?"

"I appointed him."

"Did you not hold the second key?"

I looked at her then. She was looking at the candle, not at me, and her voice was light, almost idle. But Eleanor had never been idle in her life. She had been a child who took clocks apart.

"It is in my desk at the courthouse," I said. "Where it has been for six years. Would you like to see it?"

"No."

"Then what would you like?"

She raised her eyes.

I have thought of that moment a great deal since.

"The truth," she said.

And the whole room went on laughing around us, and the candles burned, and I lifted my glass and said, very gently, "Then I am afraid you have come home to the wrong island."

---

I rose at nine.

The room fell silent by degrees, the way a field of wheat goes quiet when the wind drops. I had rehearsed the speech. I had written it twice. But standing there, with two hundred faces turned toward me and Eleanor's hand lying cold beneath mine on the cloth, I found that I did not want to give it.

I wanted to say *please.*

"Friends," I said instead. "Neighbors. Most of you knew Josiah Vale, and all of you knew Margaret. You know what this island took from that family. And you know that when Eleanor left us, it seemed to many that she would not come back."

Silence.

"She has come back."

Someone at the far table began to clap, and then another, and the sound spread through the room like a warm tide. I felt Eleanor's fingers tighten once on mine.

Not in pleasure.

In warning.

"I have asked Miss Vale to be my wife," I said, "and she has done me the honor of agreeing. We will be married at Christmas in the abbey church."

The room rose.

I will remember the noise as long as I live. Cups beating on tables, boots on boards, old Mrs. Penrose crying openly into her apron. I turned to Eleanor, and she was smiling, perfectly, beautifully, exactly as she had been taught to smile in the drawing rooms of London.

She did not look at me.

Over my shoulder, through the tall windows, she was looking at the lighthouse.

---

Afterward I walked her home.

It was a mile along the harbor road and then up through the old orchard, and I sent the carriage ahead so that we might go on foot. The storm had cleared. The stars were enormous. Below us, the sea lay black and enormous and smooth, with the white beam moving across it.

We did not speak until we reached the gate of Penhallow.

I had not planned to come this way. It was not the road. But my feet turned of their own accord, as they always did, and Eleanor stopped beside me, and we both looked at what was left.

The manor had stood for three hundred years. Now it was a shell. Black chimneys. Windows like missing teeth. The great stair open to the sky, climbing to nothing, and the garden gone to nettles and ash-pale grass.

"I haven't been here since the night it burned," Eleanor said.

"I know."

"You stood in that doorway."

"Yes."

"You said—"

"I know what I said."

She turned toward me. In the starlight her face was very young.

I had been twenty-three. She had been nineteen, and in mourning, and so thin that her wrists looked like they might snap. My father was ten hours dead in the cellar of the east wing. The fire had eaten the roof by dawn. And I had walked through the smoke and found her standing at the gate in her black dress with her hair coming down, staring at the flames, and I had said:

*I will never let anything hurt you again.*

It was the truest thing I have ever spoken.

It was also the most foolish.

"You have kept it," Eleanor said quietly.

"I have tried."

"Adrian." Her gloved hand came to rest against my sleeve. "Did you love your father?"

The question went through me like a blade between the ribs.

"No," I said. "But I obeyed him. It is a very different thing, and it took me a very long time to learn the difference."

She was silent.

"And my father?" she said. "Mine?"

"He loved you."

"That isn't what I asked."

I looked at the ruins and did not answer.

After a while she said, "I think I'd like to go home now."

I took her hand and tucked it beneath my arm, and she let me, and we walked on up the orchard in the dark.

At the door of Vale House I kissed her forehead. She permitted it.

"Good night, my dear."

"Good night."

I stood on the step until the door closed. Then I stood there a little longer.

Behind me, across the water, the lighthouse burned.

I took out my watch and looked at the time. Eleven forty.

Twelve minutes.

For twelve years I had written the same sentence on the same night. *Extinguish the lamp at 11:52. Relight at 1:14.* I wrote it at my desk with a steady hand, and I signed it, and I sent it by the boy to the keeper, and I did not ask what the keeper thought of it.

This year, for the first time, I had not written it.

I had told myself it was for Eleanor's sake. That she must not see the dark. That one night, at least, I could keep the light burning.

And the light had gone out anyway.

I watched the beam sweep once across the water, steady and white and true.

Then I walked home to Marlowe House and sat in my study until morning with the second key in my hand, and I did not sleep, and I did not pray.

I only wondered who had known to do my sin for me.
