# Chapter Twenty-Three
## The Storm Warning

*Gabriel*

The glass fell all through the night.

I watched it as a man watches a dying friend. By dawn it stood at twenty-nine point four. By noon, at twenty-nine point one. By evening the needle had crept below twenty-nine, into the region marked in faded red *STORMY,* and the wind, which had held mild all day, had begun to moan in the lantern gallery like a woman singing in another room.

I did not sleep.

I did not do anything. I sat on a stool by the stove and stared at the wall. Samuel slept on the cot behind me, his breath rasping, and I listened to it the way I listened to the sea. Overhead the lamp turned. I cleaned the lens. I trimmed the wick. I did every task that remained, in the order set down in the Trinity House manual, and when there were no more tasks I sat.

*I understand. You are right. I am a man who lets go.*

I had said it. I had meant it. That was the horror. Standing in the doorway of my own house with the wind in my face and Eleanor's eyes fixed on mine, as cold and clear as the lens of a lamp, I had felt not anger but something much worse: relief.

*At last,* some small tired animal in me had whispered. *At last it's over. She has seen what you are.*

I had gone inside. I had sat on the floor with my back against the door. I had put my fist in my mouth and bitten down until I tasted blood.

And I had not once thought: *She is lying.*

---

Samuel woke at dawn.

He lay for a time staring at the ceiling. Then he turned his head, and his gray eyes found me on the stool, and a long silence stretched between us.

"You look like hell," he said.

"You look better."

"I feel like I've been dug up." He tried to sit. I went to him; he waved me off. "I can manage. Where's the girl?"

"She's not coming."

"Why?"

"She's marrying Ashcombe."

He did not move.

"On Saturday," I said. "At noon. She came yesterday to tell me. She was most clear."

"Gabe."

"I'm told I'm a man who lets go."

"Gabe—"

"It's all right. It's true."

Samuel swung his legs over the side of the cot. He sat, swaying slightly, his big knuckly hands on his knees. He looked at me for a long time, with the same hard level look our father used to turn on a mended net.

"You're a bloody idiot," he said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"I said, you're a bloody idiot." He coughed, and wiped his mouth. "Did she cry?"

"No."

"Did she look at you?"

"She looked at me."

"Did she look at your *face,* or past you?"

I thought.

"At my face."

"And she was dry-eyed."

"Perfectly."

"With Corbett behind her."

I opened my mouth. Then I closed it.

"I spent six years watching that man in the dark," Samuel said. "I know every move. He's got the eyes of a heron. He never stops. He has a way of standing where he can see both of you and the door. If Ashcombe had been fool enough to send him to follow her, he'd have been there. Right there." He looked at me. "She told you with him at her back?"

"I... didn't see him."

"You wouldn't."

"Sam—"

"She was *lying,* you great ox." Samuel's voice cracked on a laugh. "She was putting on a play for the audience. Holy Mother. And you swallowed it whole."

I sat very still.

The wind howled in the chimney. The lamp whirred in its housing. And a thing began to rise in my chest, slow and enormous, like a diver coming up from a great depth, a thing that was neither joy nor terror but both, with a bright cold edge.

"I made her say it," I whispered.

"You what?"

"I told her I understood. I told her she was right."

"Well, that was clever."

"I wished her *happy.*"

Samuel put his face in his hands.

---

By midday the clouds had come up from the west like a wall.

I stood on the gallery and watched them. They were a color I had seen only once before, on the night of the *Halcyon,* a dark purple-black, tinged underneath with a sulphurous yellow. The sea beneath was a sheet of lead. The first rollers were beginning to break on the Teeth in long slow explosions of white, and the spray hung in the air like smoke.

Below, in the cottage garden, a figure was coming up the path.

I knew the gait. A short, square, determined woman in a black bonnet, with a basket on her arm and her skirts clutched in one fist against the wind.

I went down to meet her.

"Mrs. Crowe."

"Mr. Thorne." She was out of breath. "I've little time. He's in the stables with Corbett and the horses. I told the cook I was going to the village."

"What is it?"

She put the basket on the step and drew from beneath the cloth a small packet wrapped in brown paper and tied with twine.

"From Miss Eleanor," she said.

I took it.

Inside the paper was a sheet of cream notepaper. A single line.

*I have not forgotten.*

Under it, in a different hand, shakily but unmistakably in pencil, another line:

*I would have said it to your face if they had let me.*

I sat down on the step.

"She wrote it at midnight," said Mrs. Crowe. "By candle. She had me stand at the door. She tore up the first four." She sniffed, fiercely. "I don't hold with deceiving a man. But I held with it that night."

"Where is she now?"

"In her room. With the door locked. Mr. Ashcombe has a man on the stair." She gripped my sleeve. "She says you're to go on. You're to sail on Saturday's flood, before dawn, as agreed, with the Reverend and the cutter, and not to wait for her. She'll find a way."

"There's no way. She's to be married at noon."

"She says there is."

"What?"

"She wouldn't tell me. She said, *Mr. Thorne will know when it's time.*"

I looked at the paper in my hand. The wind tugged at the corner. The ink was already blurring from the first fine drizzle that had begun, unnoticed, to fall.

"Mrs. Crowe."

"Yes?"

"Did she say anything else?"

The old woman's face softened. For a moment, under the severity, I saw something very young.

"She said," Mrs. Crowe repeated slowly, "that she would see you again. And that she would not be late."

---

The storm came on in earnest at nightfall.

It came in four stages, like a symphony. First the wind, rising from a moan to a roar to a shriek. Then the rain, driving flat across the headland in sheets that hit the tower with a sound like gravel flung against a drum. Then the sea, which began to climb the cliff face in white slabs, and break against the rock, and hurl spray as high as the gallery. And last the dark, a thick complete blackness, solid as velvet, in which nothing existed but the lamp and the noise.

I stood in the lantern room with my hand on the brass rail.

The lens blazed above me, a hundred prisms throwing rainbows on the streaming glass. The beam went out, a white road across the water, and every few seconds the rain caught it and turned it to a shaft of silver. I could see the Teeth, a ragged line of black and white a quarter mile out, and beyond them the great gray hills of the sea moving in to break.

*Keep it burning.*

*No matter what.*

At nine o'clock, Samuel appeared on the stair.

He was gray and sweating and leaning on the rail. He had put on my old oilskin over his shirt and a pair of my boots, and he looked like a scarecrow dressed for church.

"Lie down," I said.

"I'm going to the cove."

"You're not."

"The Reverend's expecting me. The boat's rigged, Gabe. We sail at one, on the flood. It's the only tide." He coughed. "Blackwood will have the crew in the harbor by midnight."

"In this?"

"It'll blow harder before it blows out. We'll go in the lull."

"There is no lull. Not in this."

"There's always a lull."

"Sam—"

"If we wait for fair weather, Ashcombe will have us all in irons by morning." He caught my arm. His grip was weak but fierce. "She's giving us the time. She's paying for it. If we waste it, then what was it for?"

I looked at him.

I thought of Eleanor on the step of this cottage, white-faced, saying *I do not think I could love someone who lets go.*

I thought: *She was lying. She said exactly what would hurt me most.* And I thought: *She knew it would work.*

I put my hand over his.

"I'll take you to the cove," I said. "I'll light you out."

"And Ashcombe?"

"Let him come."

I took my brother's weight against my shoulder.

Together we started down the hundred and twelve steps.

---

The wind struck us at the bottom like a fist.

I had thought myself used to storms. I had been in the Bay of Biscay in a January gale, with the sea breaking over the foremast, and thought nothing of it. But this was something else. It had a voice. It howled and tore and pulled at our clothes, and it was cold, bitterly cold, with a freezing edge of rain in it.

We got Samuel across the headland by inches.

The path to the cove ran along the cliff, then down a gully to a shingle beach at the foot of the abbey ruins. In the dark, it was a river of mud and loose stone. Twice I lost my footing. Once Samuel went down on one knee, and I hauled him up by the collar, and he swore in a whisper that would have shamed a bargeman.

At the bottom we saw the lantern.

A tiny light, swinging, in the lee of a broken wall. A huddle of men in oilskins. The shape of a boat drawn up on the shingle, black against the white of the surf.

"Mr. Thorne?" called a thin voice.

"The Reverend!" Samuel croaked.

Blackwood came forward out of the dark. He wore a seaman's slicker over his cassock, and a sou'wester tied beneath his chin, and he looked about as much at home in it as a bishop in a brothel. In one hand he carried a lantern. In the other, a boat-hook.

"My son," he said. "You're alive."

"So they tell me."

"And the ledger?"

"In my coat."

"And Reed?"

Samuel's face fell. "We don't have Reed."

"We do not."

"Then we have nothing."

"We have you," said the Reverend, quietly. "And the ledger. And a letter, which Miss Vale sent by hand this afternoon. It names the day, the place, and the court. If we reach Bodmin, we have a case."

"If."

"If." He glanced past me, up at the cliff. "Mr. Thorne. Will you keep the light?"

"I'll keep it."

"All night?"

"Until you're clear."

"And after?"

I looked at the sea.

It heaved and boiled under the beam. The white road shone and shivered on the water, and at the end of it, hardly visible, a line of white foam marked the Teeth.

"I'll keep it after," I said.

The Reverend nodded.

"God keep you," he said. "God keep us all."

He put out his hand, and I took it, and Samuel took mine, and for an instant the three of us stood together in the howling dark with the sea at our feet.

Then I turned and began the long climb back to the light.
