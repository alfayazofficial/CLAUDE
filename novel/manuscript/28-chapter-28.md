# Chapter Twenty-Eight
## The Sea Takes Everything

*Eleanor*

A hundred and twelve steps.

I counted them, going up. I counted them aloud, under my breath, the way one counts in a dream, with the iron ringing under my boots and the whole tower shuddering round me in the wind. Gabriel took them three at a time ahead of me. Hocking pounded behind, wheezing. The stair spiraled up through the whitewashed throat of the lighthouse, and each turning showed us a slit of window, and each window showed us the night: black, streaming, a madness of rain and spray and tearing cloud.

At forty I could feel my heart in my teeth.

At eighty I stopped being afraid.

I do not know why. It came over me like a hand laid on the head. A strange, tranquil lightness, as though the long weight of the last twelve years were falling from me, step by step, like stones from a pocket. I thought of my mother kneeling in the crypt with her pen. I thought of her smile on the gallery. *Be brave in the small things.* There is nothing smaller than a step. And there I was, taking one more.

At a hundred and ten, Gabriel stopped.

He stood with his hand flat on the lantern room door. He did not look back. In the yellow glow from beneath the sill I could see his shoulders rise and fall.

"Eleven fifty-one," he said.

"Yes."

"If he's turned it—"

"Then we relight it."

"If Corbett's armed—"

"Then you'll hit him harder."

He laughed.

He put his shoulder to the door and pushed.

---

The lantern room blazed.

I had been in it before, in sun, on a morning of blue and silver, and thought it the loveliest place on earth. At night in a storm it was another thing entirely. The great lens turned in its brass cradle like some enormous glittering animal, and every prism flung a spear of light through the streaming glass. The rain beat on the panes in a constant roar. The wind shrieked in the ventilators. Under our feet the whole structure thrummed like a struck bell.

Two men stood beside the lamp.

Adrian was nearest the lens. He stood with his back against its brass pedestal, white as paper, his hands hanging. His oilskin lay in a puddle at his feet. He wore only his shirt and waistcoat, and the wet cloth clung to him, and the beam as it came round lit his face from within like a lantern, then left it dark.

Behind him, vast and square, with a pistol in his hand, stood Corbett.

"Ah," said Corbett. "The keeper."

"Mr. Corbett."

"I'd thought Hocking had more sense."

"He found some."

"Pity." The pistol rose, quite slowly, until it pointed at Gabriel's chest. "Come in, Mr. Thorne. Shut the door. You'll let the draught in."

Gabriel did not move.

I stepped past him.

"Adrian," I said.

He lifted his head.

"It's eleven fifty-two," I said. "Isn't it?"

His lips parted. A kind of tremor ran through him, from head to foot. His right hand went to his waistcoat pocket, and came out clenched around something that gleamed. The key.

"Eleanor."

"Don't."

"Eleanor, I—"

"Look at me."

He looked.

I do not know what he saw in my face. I know only that I stood in the full blast of the lamp, with my wet hair plastered to my cheeks and my mother's oilskin hanging off my shoulders like a rag, and I looked at him with every particle of love I had ever had for the boy who gave me a brass lighthouse in a handkerchief. I let him see it. I let it all show. I did not pretend that it was gone.

"You are not your father," I said.

"I—"

"You are not."

"Sir," said Corbett, softly. "The hour."

"I know the hour."

"It's now, sir."

"I *know.*"

"Then if you'll permit me—"

The pistol did not waver. With his left hand, almost delicately, Corbett reached across Adrian's shoulder and plucked the key from his fingers.

It happened so quickly.

I saw Adrian's hand close on air. I saw Corbett slide the key into the lock at the base of the pedestal. I saw his thick wrist turn, once, twice, three times, with a steady, practiced, almost tender motion. Something in the machinery clicked and ground and went still.

The lens stopped turning.

The great white arm of light swung once more across the sea, slowly, slowly, as the mechanism coasted, and halted, and pointed to the west, at nothing, at a wall of rain.

And then, with a soft cough, the lamp went out.

---

The darkness was total.

It was the dark of a closed box. It fell on us like a hand. For an instant there was nothing but the sound of the storm, the wail of the wind and the drum of the rain, and the hiss of four people breathing. I put out my arms. I could not see them. I could not see my fingers.

Then Gabriel roared.

I have never heard a sound like it. It was not rage, exactly. It was something from further down, a cry torn from the same place that had made him sit weeping on a stone floor. I heard the thud of his body striking Corbett's, and the clang of the pistol hitting the brass, and a shot, deafening, enormous, a gout of fire in the black.

Glass shattered.

Somebody screamed. I think it was I.

Then a vast roaring gust blew in through the broken pane, and in the sudden sheen of lightning I saw them: two men locked together at the door to the gallery, wrestling, their faces inches apart. Corbett's thick hands were at Gabriel's throat. Gabriel had his knee in Corbett's belly. They crashed through the door.

Out onto the gallery.

Into the rain.

"*Gabriel!*"

I ran.

At the doorway a hand caught my arm, hard. Adrian. He stood behind me with his face stark and wet and wild.

"The lamp!" he shouted over the wind. "Eleanor, the lamp! Leave them!"

"I can't—"

"The *boat!*" His fingers dug into my sleeve. "Four minutes! They have four minutes before they strike!"

I looked at him.

He was weeping. He was laughing. His eyes were enormous. "Help me turn the key," he said. "It has to be unlocked. Counter-clockwise, three turns. Corbett took it with him—no, there's the second—" He fumbled at his waistcoat. "I have it. Come. I can't do the wick alone."

I looked at the gallery.

Through the streaming glass, in the flash and flare of the lightning, I saw two black figures struggling at the rail. One was huge. One was lean. The rail, the rotten northeast corner, the section the Board had marked for repair, shivered between them.

*Let him. Let him fight. He is stronger than he thinks.*

I turned and followed Adrian to the lamp.

---

We did it together.

I held the oil lantern. He knelt at the base of the lens with the iron key trembling in his hand. His fingers shook so that he could not find the lock. I put my hand over his, cold and wet, and guided it. The key slid in. He turned.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Something in the clockwork groaned and caught. The great bronze cradle gave a shudder, and the lens began, slowly, to move.

"The wick!"

I struck a lucifer on the brass. It guttered. I cupped it with both hands, my body between it and the draught from the broken window, and lowered it to the lamp. For a heart-stopping moment the flame bent, bowed, flattened. Then it caught.

A small blue bud. A yellow flower.

And then the whole lens leapt into radiance.

The beam shot out. It lit the sea for twelve miles, white and furious and beautiful, and I saw below us the whole black scene laid out in a single frozen instant: the Teeth, a ragged line of foam; the mountain of the swell; and, in the very mouth of the channel, a tiny cutter plunging, tilted, her brown sail in tatters, her lantern swinging.

"She's there," I whispered.

"Yes."

"She's *through.*"

"Not yet." Adrian's hand gripped mine. "The channel turns at the third rock. She needs to see it."

The beam swung.

The boat, in its light, was a toy. A chip. A scrap of wood on a hillside of water. As we watched, she came round, heeling, hard on the wind, into the white lane the lamp laid down across the dark. A great sea lifted her. She hung on its crest. For one dreadful instant I thought she would be driven onto the rocks.

Then she slid down the back of the wave and into the channel, and the beam swept after her, and I saw a figure at her stern wave an arm.

A thin figure. In a long dark coat.

"Sam," I breathed.

Beside me Adrian made a sound I had never heard, a low broken exhalation, half a sob, half a laugh. He bent his head, and I saw the tears pour down his face. And he said, to no one, to the lamp, to the long line of dead Ashcombes receding behind him into the dark:

"I turned the key."

---

I found them on the gallery.

Gabriel was alone. He stood at the rail with one arm hooked round a stanchion, drenched, bleeding from the nose and mouth, his shirt torn open to the waist. Of Corbett there was no sign. The northeast corner of the rail was gone. A jagged gap showed where it had been, and the iron stubs of the uprights were bent outward like the fingers of a hand.

I did not ask.

I went to him through the wind. I put my arm round his waist, and he put his around my shoulders, and together we stood in the lee of the lantern wall and looked down at the sea.

"They're through," he said.

"Yes."

"We kept it burning."

"We did."

"I held on."

I turned my head.

His face in the white wash of the beam was cut and bruised and radiant. There was blood in his teeth. He was smiling. And in the smile there was a thing I had never seen before, in all the weeks of watching his face, a thing so clean and so complete that it stopped my breath. It was relief. It was pardon. It was the look of a man who has put down a stone.

"I held on," he said again, wonderingly. "To a man. By the throat. I had him over the edge, and I held on. And he went anyway. But I *held.*"

"Yes."

"It was different."

"I know."

"Eleanor—"

"I know."

He kissed me, there in the rain, with the beam sweeping over us, white, white, white.

And I saw it over his shoulder.

---

I have tried to tell this part many times.

I have never found the words. It was not a wave. It was a wall. It rose out of the western dark a quarter mile off, a vast smooth wall of water taller than the lighthouse, with a crest of white and a face of black glass. It came on without a sound. I saw the lamp strike it, saw the whole lit face of it blaze green. I saw the Teeth vanish under it, one by one. I saw the little boat in the channel, far to the east now, safe, tiny, turn her stern to it and ride up like a gull.

I opened my mouth to scream.

"Gabriel—"

He had already turned.

I felt his arm tighten round me. I felt the whole hard length of him against my side. He pulled me into the lee of the door, and hooked one hand into the iron frame, and with the other he took my hand in his and held it with such force that the bones ground together.

"I have you," he said in my ear.

"I know."

"I have you. I won't let go."

"I know."

"Eleanor. I won't."

The sound reached us then. A roar, a rising thunder, like the end of the world. The tower shook. The whole stone shaft quivered. A shriek of metal as the lantern gallery, the old iron gallery with its rotted rail, tore itself from the wall.

I looked back through the doorway.

Adrian stood in the lantern room, clinging with both arms to the lens pedestal. His face was turned toward us. His mouth was open. He was reaching, and I saw his hand stretched out across the heaving floor, the fingers spread, and the look in his eyes was the look of a man who has only now understood what he was for.

"*Eleanor!*"

The wave struck.

---

*Gabriel*

I held on.

I want that to be what is remembered. Whatever else, in the end, the island chooses to say of me, whatever the Board puts in its reports or the Reverend in his register or the children in their skipping songs, I want it written plainly, once, in one place, where it cannot be taken out.

I held on.

I felt the water take us. It lifted us from the gallery like a hand lifting a leaf. It was cold, and it was black, and it was enormous, and it had no malice in it at all. It went through me the way a river goes through a reed. I had her hand. I did not let go. I felt the iron frame tear out of my fingers and the world turn upside down, and the stars were below us, and the light was above, and I thought, quite clearly:

*She is warm.*

*She is warm, and she is here, and I have her.*

We fell.

We fell for a long time. Or perhaps it was only a moment. It is hard to tell, in the sea. The beam went round overhead, steady and white and patient, and for an instant I saw her face, turned up to mine, pale as a pearl in the dark, with her mouth open and her hair streaming. She was not afraid. She was smiling.

She said something.

I could not hear it. The sea was in my ears. But I saw her lips, and I knew the shape of them as I knew my own name.

*Play something for me.*

No.

*I love you.*

I tightened my grip on her hand.

I said it back.

And the last thing I saw, before the black water closed over us both, was the great lens above, turning, turning, turning, throwing its beam out over the Teeth and the channel and the little cutter far away and safe in the lee, as steady as it had ever been, as steady as the night Margaret Vale fell, as steady as the day I was born.

It did not go out.

It never went out again.
