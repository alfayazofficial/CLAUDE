# Chapter Thirty
## The Tide Keeps Our Names

*Samuel*

I was the one who found them.

I have not told this to anyone but my wife, and she did not ask me twice. It is not a thing one says across a table. But I have a daughter now, and a granddaughter coming, and I am an old man with a cough that never quite left, and I have decided that someone ought to write it down. Not in the court record. Not in the Bishop's sealed box. Here, in a plain notebook, in my own bad hand, where the next one can find it.

The *Faith* made Mousehole at six on the morning of the seventh. We had been eleven hours in the Channel with the sail in ribbons and the pumps going, and when I stood on the quay at last and saw the sun come up red over the Lizard, I fell down on my knees on the granite and could not rise. The Reverend, who is a very strong old man, picked me up and carried me to the inn. He did not say a word.

I did not ask what had happened to the light.

I knew.

I had seen it from the stern, in the last half hour, a single white eye over the black. I had seen it go out and come on again. I had seen the wave, a vast smooth shoulder of water rolling in from the Atlantic, and the tower standing in front of it like a candle in front of a mountain. And I had seen the beam go on turning, after, over the wreck of the gallery, steady and white, through the hours of the night.

*Gabe,* I had thought. *Gabe, you great ox. You held it.*

I did not know the rest until the Reverend told me, a week later, on the road to Bodmin.

---

I went back in November.

I was not yet strong. They had let me out of the infirmary on condition that I did not go near the sea, and I went directly to the sea. I took the packet from Penzance. I stood on the deck in the cold with the old red scarf of Gabriel's round my neck, and watched the island come up out of the mist, small and gray and wet and stubborn.

The lighthouse stood against the sky.

The new one was not yet built. The old tower stood alone on its headland, its lantern room dark and ragged, the gallery gone. Seen from the water it looked like a bone from which something had been taken. I stared at it for a long time. Then I turned my face away.

Mrs. Crowe met me at the quay.

She did not weep. She was past it. She took my arm in her small hard hand and led me up the harbor road, through the stares of people who looked quickly away, to Vale House. The door stood open. The hall was dark. She did not light a candle. She took me by the wrist and led me straight through the house, past the portraits, past the great stair, to the green door.

"Are they—" I said.

"Not here."

"Where?"

"Come."

---

We went down at the spring ebb, a little after four in the morning, on the first of December.

The Reverend was with us. He carried the lantern. He walked slowly now, with a stoop he had not had in October, and he stopped often to rest his hand against the wall. Mrs. Crowe carried a bundle of clean linen. I carried a crowbar, and my heart was a stone.

I knew the way. I had known it since boyhood. The tunnel, the stair, the black door behind the press. The twenty-three steps. The smell of brine and rot and cold deep water. And at the bottom, the great vault, with the sea breathing at its mouth and the moon making a white road across the floor.

The names glimmered on the walls.

I did not look at them. I had read them all, a hundred times, in six years of nights.

I looked at the third niche.

It was at the far end, behind the broken column. The upside-down cross. The hole where the ledger had lain. Before it, on the flat stone ledge where I had slept, the water had left something.

I saw her hair first.

Dark. A long wet rope of it, trailing over the lip of the ledge, coiled like a sleeping snake. Then the gray wool of her coat, my sister-in-law's coat, I almost said, and my mind recoiled at the word. Then the shape of her, small and straight, lying on her side with her knees drawn up, as a child lies in a bed.

And Gabriel.

He lay behind her, along her back. His body curved around hers. One arm was crooked under her head. The other lay across her, and in it, between the two of them, locked so tightly that the fingers had gone white and the knuckles stood out like pebbles, was her hand.

They were not hurt.

I want that known. I have seen drowned men. I have seen what the Teeth do to a body, and I braced for it. But the sea had been kind. They lay as if asleep, with their faces calm, their eyes closed, their mouths a little open. The cold had kept them. There was not a mark on either. The water had carried them, God knows how, through the old sea passage, a mile under the cliff, along the black channel that the monks had cut for their smuggling, and laid them in the one place on the island where the dead were already waiting.

I knelt.

I put my hand on my brother's hair.

It was dry.

I do not know how it was dry. The water had been on them eight weeks. I have asked the Reverend, and the Reverend says that the Lord moves in mysterious ways, and I have asked Reed, who says it was probably the lie of the stone, and I have asked myself, at three in the morning, in the dark, and I have no answer. I know only that when I touched him, my brother's hair was dry and warm, as though he had just put down a book and fallen asleep in the sun.

I wept then.

I wept the way I had not wept in six years. I put my head on his shoulder and sobbed, and the Reverend knelt beside me and said the Office for the Dead in a voice that kept breaking, and Mrs. Crowe stood a little apart, with the linen in her arms, and her lips moved, and I do not think she was praying.

---

We could have taken them out.

I want that understood. We discussed it, in whispers, in the grey cold, with the tide beginning to make. We could have carried them up the stair and through the vestry and laid them in the churchyard, under the north wall beside the stone I had never lain beneath. There would have been a funeral. The island would have come. Two coffins, two names, two wreaths of sea-pinks.

And the island would have had to look.

That was what stopped us.

The judge would say it, a fortnight later, in the court. *The peace of the Duchy would not survive the truth.* Everyone on the island had known it for twelve years, and would know it again as soon as they stood at an open grave. They would stand with their hats in their hands and look at the lighthouse keeper and the daughter of Margaret Vale, drowned together, and understand exactly what they had done.

They would not forgive us for it.

They would bury it.

And Eleanor and Gabriel would be buried with it, in a corner of the churchyard, under a stone that said *Lost in the great storm,* and in fifty years the grass would grow over the name.

"No," said Mrs. Crowe.

I looked up.

"No, Mr. Samuel." Her voice was very quiet. "Not up there. Not where they can forget."

The Reverend rose slowly.

"Hester—"

"She's with her mother," said Mrs. Crowe. "Look."

She raised her lantern.

---

I have said the wall was full of names.

It was. But at the very end of the vault, to the left of the niche, where the rock bent toward the sea, there was a space. A long smooth tongue of bare stone, six feet by four, which Mrs. Crowe, in her grief, had once told me was the place where the hand had grown too tired to go on.

I had always seen it so. A blank. A silence.

But in the lantern light, now, it was not blank.

Someone had written there.

I stepped closer.

The carving was deep. It had been done with a hard, steady hand and an edge of iron, and done slowly, over many hours. I counted the letters. I read them once, and read them again. They were old. They were not scratched this autumn, nor last. The edges had gone soft, as stone does that has been washed by the tide for a decade.

*MARGARET VALE. 1842–1879.*

I recognized the hand.

I had seen it in the ledger. In the pencilled margins of the map in the study. In the postscript of a letter that a woman had read aloud by candlelight to a man with his hands bound behind him.

She had carved her own name.

Twelve years ago. On the afternoon of the twenty-eighth of September, by candle, on her knees. Before she went up the cliff. She had stood in front of this blank stone, and she had known what she meant to do and what it would cost, and she had put her name to the wall of the drowned as one signs a will.

And beneath her name, in a smaller hand, a single line:

*The tide keeps what we write. Add the others as they come.*

Below that, a space.

A long smooth space, the length of a man.

And in the space, so freshly cut that the stone-dust still lay pale in the grooves, were two more names.

*ELEANOR VALE.*

*GABRIEL THORNE.*

---

I stood and stared.

The lantern shook in Mrs. Crowe's hand.

"Who?" I whispered.

Nobody answered.

"Who carved them? I didn't. The Reverend didn't. We came straight down. There's been no one—"

"It was done in the last week or two," said the Reverend, in a voice I did not know. "Look at the edges. The dust. A week old. No more."

"But *who?*"

He did not answer.

I turned, slowly, and looked at the two on the ledge. At the girl in the gray coat, and the man curled round her, and their joined hands.

Neither had a knife.

I looked at their fingers. The right hand of my brother, the one that lay across her breast, was clenched, and under the nails, I saw, there was a fine pale grit.

Stone dust.

I do not know what happened in that place between the wave and the dawn. I do not know how two drowned people, a mile under the sea, in the black, came to lie dry on a ledge before a wall of names. I do not know who cut the letters. I have heard it said that the dead can write. I have heard it said that the tide is a mind. I have heard it said, by old Hocking, who is a Methodist and a sensible man, that Margaret Vale has been waiting twelve years for her daughter to come down to her, and that when they came she was not about to let them go unrecorded.

I know only what I saw.

Two names, in a hand I knew. In the stone. Beneath their mother's.

*The tide keeps what we write.*

---

We buried them where they lay.

The Reverend and I carried the stone ourselves. A long flat slab from the broken floor of the vestry, which we laid over the ledge on iron bars, and sealed round with lime. It took three nights. On the third, when the work was done, the Reverend stood before it with his hat in his hand and said the last prayers, and Mrs. Crowe laid a posy of sea-pinks on the stone, and I put the old red scarf on top.

Then we went up the stair, and I shut the door, and put the press back in front of it.

The tide came in at five.

It came in as it always has, slowly, a long dark whisper over the black stone floor, and rose over the ledge, and rose over the slab, and covered the foot of the wall. It covered the names of the *Anne Marie* and the *Constant* and the *Sparrowhawk.* It covered the names of the unknown boy of nine. It covered Margaret's, and then, slowly, as the moon pulled it higher, her daughter's, and the keeper's.

And it will go out again.

That is the thing I want you to know, whoever you are, reading this. It always goes out. It goes out twice a day, forever, and every time it goes it leaves the names. Clean. Whole. Nothing washed away. A hundred years from now, if the roof of the abbey falls and the vestry is a heap of stones and every living soul on the island has forgotten what the sea took from it, still, at the spring ebb, a person may stand in that vault with a lantern, and read.

*Margaret Vale.*

*Eleanor Vale.*

*Gabriel Thorne.*

*And fourteen men of the* Sparrowhawk, *and two hundred and eleven of the* Halcyon, *and nineteen ships before them, and a boy of nine, and a Breton cook, and a woman with her child.*

They are not lost.

The tide keeps our names.

It always has.
