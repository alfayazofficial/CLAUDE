# Chapter Thirteen
## A House Full of Ghosts

*Eleanor*

We waited in the tunnel for forty minutes.

I counted them in Gabriel's breathing. He stood with his back against the wet rock and his arm around me, and neither of us said a word. I could hear Adrian's tread overhead, back and forth across the study, the scrape of a chair, the soft slide of a drawer. Once, a long silence, and I knew he was reading the draft letters. Once, a laugh, short and dry, with no mirth in it.

At last, the boots climbed. The green door shut. The latch clicked.

We did not move for another ten minutes.

"He knows," I whispered.

"He knows you were here," Gabriel said, very low. "He doesn't know what you've found."

"He read my mother's drafts."

"He read some drafts. Not the letter." His breath stirred my hair. "He left because you weren't there. A man who truly wished to confront you would have broken the door."

"What does he want?"

"For you to come to him."

I shivered. "He will ask me tomorrow."

"Then tell him you went to the abbey. A walk, alone, to think. He'll believe the lie that costs you least."

I turned in the circle of his arm. In the dark I could see only the pale oval of his face.

"You're very good at this."

"I've had practice."

"At what?"

"Being afraid of men I once trusted."

---

I did not sleep.

I lay in my four-poster with the curtains open and the moon on the counterpane, and the house gave up its sounds one by one. The tick of a cooling pipe. The scurry of mice in the wall. The settling groan of a floor. Somewhere below, very faintly, a note of music: not the Broadwood, which was shut, but something thin and high, a fiddle, or a woman humming.

I rose at four and went to the window.

The garden lay silver and black. At the end of the lawn, where the terraces met the cliff, a figure stood in the moonlight looking up at my window.

A woman.

She wore a long pale dress. Her dark hair was loose. She stood perfectly still, with her hands at her sides, and the wind that stirred the lavender did not stir her hem.

My breath stopped.

I put my hand to the glass.

She raised her head.

I could not see her face. The moon was behind her. But I knew the line of the shoulder, the tilt of the chin, the long fingers. I knew them as I knew my own hands.

"Mama," I whispered.

She lifted one arm. She pointed. Not at me. Past me. At the corner of the east wing, where the ivy grew over a window that had been bricked up years ago.

Then she was gone.

I did not scream. I did not run. I stood at the window with my palm on the cold glass and counted my heartbeats until the dawn came gray over the sea. When I could move again I found that my nails had cut into my palm, and a thin line of blood ran down to my wrist.

I told myself it was a dream.

I told myself I had grown too accustomed to dreaming.

But I put on my dressing gown, and I went down through the sleeping house, and I walked to the east wing.

---

The room behind the bricked window was a nursery.

I knew it as soon as I found the door. A narrow door in the wainscoting, papered over with a trellis of roses that had faded to the color of tea. The wallpaper had been slashed and peeled back. Someone had used a knife.

Behind it, a corridor. Seven feet long. A door at the end.

The door was open.

I stood on the threshold and smelled dust, and old paper, and the faint sweet scent of lavender and orris. And I thought: *This was her room.*

It was a girl's room. A girl who had become a woman and never changed it. A small iron bed. A washstand with a cracked pitcher. A shelf of books: Scott, Austen, a battered *Pilgrim's Progress.* On the wall, a watercolor of the lighthouse, signed *M.R., aged 15.*

Margaret Rayner. My mother's maiden name.

She had come to this house at nineteen as a bride. I had never known she kept a room in it.

On the bed lay a little wooden box, and beside the box, a candle stub burned down to a puddle of wax, and beside the candle, a child's slate with chalk writing on it.

Three words, in a hand I knew.

*Ask Hester. Now.*

---

I found Mrs. Crowe in the kitchen at six.

She was alone, kneading dough, her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her forearms white with flour. She looked up when I came in. She saw my face. She saw what I held.

She stopped.

"Where did you find that?"

"You know where."

"Miss—"

"You knew about the room."

"I did."

"You knew about the study. The tunnel. The crypt. You've known all of it, for twelve years, and you let me walk about this house like a blind woman."

"I promised."

"You promised *her.* I am not her. I am not a thing to be protected from the truth. I am *thirty years old* and I have been a stranger in my own home since the day I came back." I laid the slate on the table, hard. "Someone wrote that in chalk this morning. It is not my mother's hand. It is someone who knows the house. Who is it?"

Mrs. Crowe wiped her hands on her apron. She did it slowly, thoroughly, finger by finger.

Then she sat down at the kitchen table, heavily, like a woman who has been standing since before she was born.

"Sit," she said.

I sat.

"I'll tell you the whole of it," said Mrs. Crowe. "And I'll not be interrupted. And when it's done you may dismiss me, and I'll go quietly, and I will understand."

"I would never—"

"Hush."

---

"I was in service here from the age of fifteen," she said. "Your grandmother's time. I saw your mother come as a bride. A slip of a girl, white as milk, who'd never been farther than Penzance. She laughed at everything. She laughed at the fish on the table. She laughed at the servants' hall. I thought: she won't last a winter.

"She lasted twenty years.

"I'll not say Mr. Vale was cruel. He was never that. He was a man who hated a quarrel. When trouble came, he went out walking, and trouble waited for him until he came home. He let things be done in his name that he'd never have done with his own hand. And your mother saw it, bit by bit, as the years went on, and it broke something in her that never mended.

"She found the ledger in June. I knew the day. She came back from the vestry white as the dead, and she sat in the kitchen with a cup of tea and did not drink it. I said, *Madam?* She said, *Hester, who do we work for?*"

I could barely speak. "What did you say?"

"I said, *Mr. Vale.* And she said, *Yes. And whose money is it?*"

A log settled in the range.

"I knew what she meant. I'd known for years. Everyone in the house knew, the way you know the smell of the sea. The chests that came at night. The silver. The cargo that was never quite described. I told myself it was salvage. Lawful salvage, from the wrecks. That the sea gave it and we only took what it gave."

"And the lights?"

"I told myself the lights were weather."

Her voice was flat and dry.

"On the twenty-eighth of September I was in bed when I heard the study door. Your father's. I came down the back stairs in my nightgown, and I saw him in the hall with Lord Ashcombe. Lord Ashcombe was in oilskins. He had a lantern. Your father gave him a key."

"A key."

"The lantern-room key. It had hung on a nail in your father's study for forty years. The Ashcombes keep one, and the Vales another, for the old arrangement. Lord Ashcombe took it and put it in his coat and said, *Is she upstairs?* And your father said, *She's in the kitchen with the boy.* And Lord Ashcombe said, *Keep her there.*"

My lips were numb.

"And did he?"

"No, miss. Your father went upstairs and stood outside the kitchen door and did nothing. He heard her go out through the scullery. He heard her go. And he went back to the study and poured himself a brandy, and sat in his chair in front of the fire, and when I came in he said, *Go to bed, Hester.* In a voice like a man ordering the post."

I stood up.

I did not know I was going to. My chair went over with a crash. I walked to the window and stood there with my back to the room, and the garden outside blurred and ran together in the light.

"Did he know?"

"I don't know."

"Did he *know?*"

"Miss Eleanor." Her voice cracked at last. "I think he knew exactly."

I turned.

She was weeping. Silently, with her mouth shut, tears running down the grooves of her cheeks. She did not wipe them. She sat with her hands in her lap and let them fall.

"I have carried it twelve years," she said. "I loved her. I loved you. I loved him, too, God forgive me, a little. And I thought: if I tell her, she'll hate us both. And if I do not tell her, she'll come to hate the house. And the house is all there is."

"Mrs. Crowe—"

"I will go, if you like."

"You will not."

I crossed the kitchen. I knelt before her chair, and took her floury hands in both of mine, and held them. They were small and hard and cold.

"You kept her letter," I said. "You kept her pages. You waited twelve years in a house that was strangling you, so that I would have something to find. If that is cowardice, then God help the brave."

She bent her head.

We stayed there a long time, the old woman and the young one, in a kitchen full of the smell of yeast.

When at last I rose, I found that the slate was lying face up on the table, and the chalk had been scrubbed from it clean.

*Ask Hester. Now.*

It was gone.

"I didn't touch it," said Mrs. Crowe, staring.

"Neither did I."

We looked at each other.

And from the far end of the house, from the music room, came a single note.

Low A.

Struck softly. Held.

And let go.
