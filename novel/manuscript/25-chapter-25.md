# Chapter Twenty-Five
## The Lighthouse

*Eleanor*

I climbed out of my window at eight o'clock with a rope of bedsheets and a stolen kitchen knife.

I will not pretend it was brave. I had planned it for hours, with Mrs. Crowe holding the door and the maid weeping in the corner, and when the moment came my hands shook so badly that I tied the third knot twice. The man on the landing was an islander named Pascoe, with a face like a boiled egg and a wife expecting. I had knocked on the door and asked, in a small voice, for water. He had come in with the jug. Mrs. Crowe had stepped out from behind the screen with the poker.

I will say, for the record, that she struck him very kindly.

Then I went out of the window into the wind.

It snatched me at once. The rope swung and slammed me against the stone and I hung there, breathless, with the whole black garden tossing under me, and thought: *I am thirty years old, and I am doing this.* Then my arms gave out, and I dropped the last four feet, and landed on my back in a bed of wet lavender with the air knocked out of me.

I lay there for a count of five.

Then I got up and ran.

Not for the cliff path. Corbett had men on it, I was certain. I ran for the house, around to the east wing, to the little door in the cellar wall that I had found in the pantry, the one Samuel had used for six years. It opened onto a stair, and the stair went down, and at the bottom was the study with its chart and its lamp. I crossed it. I pushed open the iron-hinged door in the stone. And I went into the tunnel with a candle in one hand and the knife in the other, and a mile of black rock before me, and the sound of the sea like a beast breathing in the walls.

---

I emerged in the crypt a little before ten.

The tide was high. The floor was a shallow lake. The names on the walls glimmered faintly in the wet, and the water lapped at my knees, and a great swell lifted through the seaward opening and rolled in like a hand, shouldering the candle out. I stood in the dark, soaked to the thighs, and did not mind.

I felt my way to the stair.

I came up through the vestry. I crossed the open nave, with the rain sheeting through the broken roof and the wind roaring in the ruined arches like an organ played by a madman. I came out at the north door. And there, a quarter mile off, high on its headland, with its great white eye turning in the murk, stood the lighthouse.

I had never seen it so beautiful.

It was a blade of light cutting the dark. It hung over the sea like a promise, steady, impossible, defiant. The storm tore at it. The rain flew past it in streaks. And it kept turning, round and round, as it had turned for sixty years, as though nothing in the world could put it out.

I put my head down and ran.

---

He was on the gallery when I got there.

I knew because I heard him. The wind carried his voice down the tower, a long ragged shout, not words but a sound, an exultant cry like a man who has seen a ship. I stood at the foot of the iron stair and looked up, and saw him leaning over the rail, a black figure against the swinging beam.

"Gabriel!"

He did not hear.

I put my hands to my mouth.

"*Gabriel!*"

He stopped.

I saw him look down.

I do not know how to describe what happened next. He did not call. He did not wave. He simply stood with his hands on the rail, staring, and then he turned and disappeared into the tower, and I heard his feet on the iron treads, a long ringing thunder, a hundred and twelve steps taken three at a time.

The door at the foot of the stair burst open.

He stood in it, soaked, breathing hard, with the lamp-oil on his hands and the lens-light in his hair.

"Eleanor."

"I wasn't lying," I said. "I want you to know that. I said it to Corbett, not to you. I was never going to marry him."

"I know."

"You know?"

"Sam told me. I was a fool for a day." He crossed the space between us. "And Mrs. Crowe brought your note."

"I'm so sorry—"

"Don't."

"I said terrible things."

"Eleanor, *don't.*"

He took my face in his hands.

He did not kiss me. He looked at me, and his hands were shaking, and his eyes were enormous and wet. I saw the rain on his lashes. I saw his throat work.

"I thought I'd lost you," he whispered.

"I told you I wouldn't be late."

"You're soaked."

"I came through the sea."

"You're *mad.*"

"I know." I laughed. It cracked in the middle. "I have been told."

He pulled me against him so hard that the breath went out of my lungs. I felt his heart through his shirt, and his arms around my back, and the damp wool of his jersey against my cheek. He smelled of oil and salt and rain, and he was alive, and warm, and mine.

We stood there in the roaring dark, at the foot of the tower, and held on.

---

He took me into the cottage.

The stove was lit. A kettle sang on the hob. The lamp in the window had been trimmed low. Samuel was gone; the cot was bare, the blanket folded in a square. A note lay on the table: *Gone to the cove. Don't be an ass. S.*

I laughed again, and this time it did not crack.

Gabriel wrapped me in a blanket and sat me by the fire and knelt to pull off my sodden boots. His hands were gentle and clumsy. He worked on the laces for a long time, and when he had them off he held my bare foot in both palms and rubbed it between them, and I looked at the top of his dark head and thought: *I would give anything for this to be all.*

"Gabriel."

"Hm?"

"Where is the letter?"

His hands stopped.

"The one she gave you. In August. The sealed one." I touched his hair. "The one for me."

He looked up.

"I think it's time," I said.

He sat back on his heels.

For a moment he said nothing. Then, slowly, he rose and went to the shelf where the books were stacked. He drew down a thick one, Gray's *Anatomy,* its spine cracked, and opened the back cover. A thin leather pocket was sewn into the lining. I had not noticed it. He slid two fingers inside.

He came back and knelt and held out a letter.

It was small. Not much larger than a playing card. The paper had gone the color of weak tea, and the wax seal on the back, a plain red disc stamped with a ship in full sail, was worn smooth by years of thumbs. It had been handled so many times that the corners had softened to velvet.

On the front, in my mother's hand:

*For Eleanor, when she is a woman grown.*

And beneath it, a second note on a separate scrap, fastened to the first with a pin. The paper was stained brown. Stained, I saw, with something that had once been red.

*Gabriel. Take this. Keep it from them. I do not blame you for anything that happens tonight. M.V.*

"She left it on the stair," Gabriel said. His voice was very low. "At the foot of the tower. Under a stone, with the corner showing. I found it the next morning. There was blood on it from her hands."

"Why didn't you give it to me?"

"I tried. When you were eighteen. I came to Vale House in the winter and stood at the gate. I saw you in the window, in your black dress. You looked like a ghost." He swallowed. "I couldn't. I had no right to be the one to carry her last words to you. I thought, *if I give her this, I must tell her the rest.* And I couldn't."

I took the letter.

It weighed nothing at all.

"I won't open it yet," I said.

"No?"

"Not tonight. Tonight I only want to hold it." I closed my fingers on it. "Because when I read it, it'll be over. She'll be done speaking. And I would like, for as long as I can, to have her still say something to me."

He bowed his head.

"I understand."

"I know you do."

He leaned his forehead against my knee.

And from the window, across the storm, over the roar of the sea and the shriek of the wind, I heard the faint clang of a bell.

A boat's bell. Small and thin.

Far out on the black water, below the lamp, a single yellow light was moving.

"The *Faith,*" Gabriel said.

We stood together at the window.

She was tiny. A crumb of wood on a mountain of water, plunging and rising, plunging and rising, with a spark of lantern at her masthead. A scrap of brown sail clung to her. As we watched, a white wave broke over her bow and she vanished, and my heart stopped, and she came up again, streaming, still afloat.

"She's early," I breathed.

"They went at the lull."

"Is she clear?"

"No." His hand closed on mine. "She has the channel to run. A mile of it. And she needs the beam."

I looked up.

Above us, in the tower, the lens turned. A great white arm swept down across the sea, and caught the little boat, and held her, and passed on.

"It's one hour," I said. "Until they're through."

"One hour."

"We can keep a lamp burning for an hour."

"Yes."

"Gabriel."

"Yes?"

"I think someone is coming up the path."

He turned his head.

Through the streaming glass, a hundred yards off along the cliff, a row of lanterns was bobbing in the dark. Five, six, seven of them. The light caught the long black shapes of men. And at the head of the line, tall and straight and walking with the unhurried confidence of one who has never in his life been refused, came a figure in a long dark coat.

"Adrian," I said.

"Yes."

"He said he wouldn't."

"He lied."

"He doesn't know how to do anything else," I whispered, and found, to my surprise, that I was weeping. "God help him."

Gabriel took my hand.

We stood side by side, in the window of the keeper's cottage, in the roar of the storm, and watched the lanterns come on.
