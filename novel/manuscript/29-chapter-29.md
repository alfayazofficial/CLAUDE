# Chapter Twenty-Nine
## What Remains

*Adrian*

They found me at dawn, still holding the lens.

Hocking told me afterward. He had come up the tower an hour after the wave, half drowned himself, with a broken arm and a lantern, and found the lantern room a ruin: the windows gone, the floor awash, the gallery torn from the wall as a child tears the crust from bread. And in the middle of it all, the great lens, turning. Turning still. Its brass cradle was bent. Its prisms were cracked. But it turned, and the beam went out over the gray water, and kneeling at its foot with both hands locked on the iron handwheel, turning it by force when the clockwork failed, was a man in a wet shirt who did not seem to hear his name.

I had kept it burning.

I have no memory of the three hours. I know only what they told me. That I turned the wheel by hand, a quarter turn every twelve seconds, until my palms split and my fingers froze to the iron. That when Hocking tried to take my place I struck him. That when the sun came up I was still turning, and the Reverend's cutter was seen far out in the Channel, a speck of brown sail, bound for Mousehole, and Hocking said *Mr. Ashcombe, sir, they're clear,* and I said *I know,* and could not let go.

They had to cut my hands free.

---

I was tried at Bodmin in the second week of December.

I will not give the whole of it. It has been written down elsewhere, by clerks, in the dry hand of the law. It took six days. The Reverend gave evidence for two. Dr. Reed, who had been found in my cellar by Mrs. Crowe on the morning after the storm, weeping and unharmed and quite sober for the first time in a decade, gave evidence for one. He produced the page from the lining of his bag. He read aloud the words he had written in a steady hand twelve years before: *Contusion to the left temple, consistent with a blow. Fracture of the right radius. Death by drowning, but not accidental.*

The court was very quiet when he finished.

Samuel Thorne gave evidence on the fourth day. He was brought in on a chair. He had been in the infirmary at Penzance for six weeks, and he looked like a man who had been dug out of a bog and set to dry. But he spoke for an hour and a half in a clear, hoarse, level voice, and when he had done there was not a dry eye on the jury benches, nor, I think, among the counsel.

He did not look at me once.

I understood.

The ledger was entered as an exhibit. It was passed from hand to hand along the bench. I watched the judge turn the pages, and I saw his face change, from the fixed professional blandness of a man of the law to something older and more disturbed. He read for a long time. When he looked up, he looked, not at me, not at the dock, but at the gallery where the island's gentry sat in their best black, shoulder to shoulder, with their hats in their hands.

I saw them see it.

I saw the Trevennicks, and the Penroses, and old Mrs. Vivian from the Assembly Room, and Sir Henry Tregenza, who had sat on the Board of Trade, and the Bishop's secretary from Truro. I saw them look at the book in the judge's hands, and I saw the thing pass through them like a cold breath.

Every one of them had had a share.

Every one.

---

I pleaded guilty.

I did it on the first morning, before the indictment was read, in a voice I did not recognize as mine. I pleaded to the manslaughter of the crew of H.M.S. *Halcyon.* I pleaded to being an accessory to the deaths of the brig *Sparrowhawk.* I pleaded to the concealment of the death of Margaret Vale. I asked that Corbett's name be struck from the indictment, for he was dead, and that my own take its place.

My counsel wept.

The judge asked whether I understood the consequences.

I said that I did.

He asked whether I had anything to say before sentence.

"Only this," I said. "I should like it recorded that Eleanor Vale and Gabriel Thorne lost their lives on the night of the sixth of October, in the service of the light, in an act of conscience which saved five men on the cutter *Faith.* That the lamp was relit by their hands, and by mine. That without them I should have been a murderer twice over. And that the court should know their names."

There was a silence.

Then the judge, an old man named Pollock with a face like a walnut, bent his head and wrote for a long moment.

"It is recorded," he said.

---

They did not hang me.

I had expected it. I had wanted it. In the long nights of the Bodmin cell, with the wind in the chimney and the clock on the prison church tolling the quarters, I had rehearsed the scaffold as other men rehearse a speech. I had walked it in my mind from the condemned cell to the trap, forty-one paces, and I had known where I should put my feet.

But on the sixth day they brought in the verdict, and the judge put on the black cap, and I stood.

"Adrian Thomas Ashcombe," said Pollock. "You have confessed to crimes which, in the ordinary course, would take your life. I have considered with great care whether the law requires it. I have concluded that it does not."

I did not understand.

"The evidence before this court," he went on, "is of a kind which has not, to my knowledge, been laid before any English bench. It discloses a conspiracy of generations, in which a great part of the gentry and clergy of a county have been, to some degree, concerned. The Crown has been advised that to proceed to a full prosecution would be to put upon trial not an individual but a community."

A murmur ran through the court.

"The Crown has accordingly seen fit to confine the indictment to your own acts," said the judge. "And I am instructed, on the advice of the Home Secretary, that the capital sentence shall not be sought."

"My lord—"

"You will serve fifteen years' penal servitude."

I could not breathe.

"Fifteen—"

"In the Convict Prison at Dartmoor."

"But the others—"

"The others," said Pollock, quite quietly, "will not be proceeded against."

He looked at me.

I saw, in his old shrewd face, a thing I had never seen in a man of power: shame.

"I regret it," he said. "I want it understood. I regret it, and I think history will regret it more. But the Crown has judged that the peace of the Duchy would not survive the truth. The ledger will be lodged with the Bishop of Truro, under seal. The report of this trial will be abridged. The island will rebuild its lighthouse, and its wrecking will end, and it will be left to bury its dead."

"And Margaret Vale?"

"Is dead, sir."

"And the fourteen?"

"Are dead."

"And the two on the gallery?"

Judge Pollock closed his eyes.

"God rest them," he said. "The court will not name them in its report."

"You will *not*—"

"Take the prisoner down."

---

I write this in my cell at Dartmoor, in the fourth year of my sentence, on paper smuggled in by a kind warder.

I am thirty-eight. My hair has turned white. I have a window the size of a hymnal, through which, if I stand on my bed, I can see a rim of moor and a piece of sky. At night I hear the wind in the tors. It does not sound like the sea.

The island has been quiet.

Mrs. Crowe writes me every month, a single page in a cramped, formal hand. She tells me the weather. She tells me the price of fish. She tells me that the new lighthouse, a handsome gray tower of Cornish granite, built on the same headland by the Trinity House, was lit in the autumn of that year by a keeper from Plymouth who knows nothing. The old tower stands empty. The gallery has not been replaced. Children dare one another to climb to the lantern room, and the Reverend, who is eighty, preaches against it from the pulpit, and they go anyway.

She tells me that no one on the island will speak of what happened.

Not in the Assembly Room. Not at the Gannet. Not at the harbor, where the nets are mended in silence and the boys no longer sing *The Tide Keeps Our Names,* the old counting rhyme. A thing that was once the common knowledge of every household has simply gone out, like a lamp. A generation hence it will be as if it had never been.

She tells me that Samuel Thorne is well.

He has married. A girl from Mousehole, a fish-merchant's daughter, who laughs at his jokes. He lives in a cottage by the old harbor, and keeps a boat, and takes the Reverend out when the weather's fine. He has a daughter, three years old. He has called her Margaret.

He has never written to me.

Last winter, however, a package came. It was addressed in a hand I did not know, a firm, square, rather boyish script. Inside, folded in oilcloth, I found a single sheet of cream notepaper, creased in thirds, and on it, in a hand I knew better than my own:

*Adrian.*

*If you are reading this, I did not come home, and you have found my mother's diary on the pillow. I left it for you. I wanted you to read what she wrote on the stair.*

*She told you that you were not to blame. She told me, too, in her last letter, in a different way. I think she must have said it to everyone she loved. It was the kind of thing she did.*

*I want to say it as well. I have never hated you. I loved the boy who gave me a lighthouse in a handkerchief, and I think he is still in there somewhere, under all that sorrow. If you find him, be kind to him.*

*Whatever you do tonight, you can still stop. It's only a key. Put it down.*

*Eleanor.*

On the back, in Samuel's hand, were four words.

*Mrs. Crowe found it. Schubert.*

I understood. She had tucked it inside the score on the music stand, at the page of the impromptu. Where, of all places on earth, only one person would have thought to look.

I put it against my face.

It smelled of dust, and old rooms, and the faintest ghost of orris and lamp oil.

I did not weep. I have no tears left. But I sat on my bed in the gray light, with the page against my cheek, and I thought of a girl of fourteen with a handkerchief and a brass lighthouse. *So you will always know where home is.*

I have it still. The little brass lighthouse. They let me keep it. I have it on the shelf beside my Bible, and every evening, when the warder has made his round and the lamp has gone out in the corridor, I take it down and hold it in my hands.

I know where home is.

I always did.

It was only that I could not bring myself to go.
