# Chapter Seventeen
## The Dead Man's Name

*Eleanor*

Corbett said nothing.

He walked me home along the cliff road, two paces behind and to the left, like a servant. He did not speak. He did not look at me. When we reached the gate of Vale House he opened it, and I passed through, and he shut it behind me and stood on the other side with his hat in his hands.

"Mr. Ashcombe will call at four," he said.

"I shall be at home."

"Yes, miss."

That was all. But I felt his eyes on my back all the way up the gravel, as cold and steady as a hand.

I bathed. I changed. I sat on the edge of my bed with my hair wet down my back and my mother's letter on my knee, and I stared at the wall and thought: *Everything that was safe about me has been spent.*

I was not sorry.

That was the dreadful part. I looked for sorrow the way one looks for a lost glove, in all the likely places, and found it nowhere. I found only a quiet, drowned, shining gladness, like the color of a street after rain, and under it a bright thread of fear.

I would have to tell Adrian.

I would have to tell him before Corbett did.

But I did not, as it happened, have the chance.

---

Mr. Blackwood sent for me at noon.

A boy brought the message, a small round fisher-child with a runny nose and a note in a clerical hand. *Miss Vale: I have been requested by your late mother to speak with you. If you are able, come to the vestry today. I do not trust the post. T.B.*

I read it twice.

Then I sent for my cloak.

The parish church of St. Piran stood on the headland above the harbor, a squat gray building with a stumpy tower and a graveyard that fell away in terraces to the sea. Behind it, a quarter mile north along the cliff, lay the ruins of the old abbey. I had never visited either. As a child I had been taken to service in the carriage, in my best white, and made to sit very still; my mother had always insisted on the front pew. Now I went up the lych-gate alone, in the thin cold noon, with a gray wind pulling at my hat.

The Reverend Thomas Blackwood met me at the vestry door.

He was a spare man in his late sixties, with a stoop and a white fringe of hair and eyes the color of weak tea. He wore his cassock with the air of a man who had worn it too long and had grown to resent the cut. He took my hand in both of his. They were dry and papery and very cold.

"Miss Vale. I did not think you would come."

"You wrote that you'd been asked by my mother."

"I was." He drew me inside. "Come. We will not be overheard."

The vestry was a low room with a stone floor and a fireplace and a long oak table covered in books. Shelves ran floor to ceiling. Baptismal registers, burial registers, marriage registers. A hundred years of births and deaths in tidy columns. The air smelled of mouse and old leather.

"You have read her letter," said Mr. Blackwood.

I started. "How do you—"

"Because Hester Crowe has not slept in a week and has been seen going into the church at dawn." He gave a thin smile. "I am old, my dear. I am not blind."

"Then you know what it said."

"I know what it ought to have said. I have known for twelve years." He lowered himself into a chair with a creak of bones. "Margaret wrote to me in the summer before she died. Three letters. I answered none."

"Why not?"

"Because I was afraid."

The words fell into the room like stones.

"I am a priest," he said. "I have buried a hundred drowned men. I have said the office over empty coffins, because the sea did not give them up. I have stood on that headland in a gale with a prayer book in my hand and wept for people I never knew. And when your mother wrote to me that the lights were being put out by the very men who sat in my front pews, I burned the letters."

"You burned them."

"I did. And then I went to the Ashcombe house for dinner, and I ate roast mutton, and I said nothing." He met my eyes. "I have been saying nothing ever since."

I did not know what to answer.

"I was not worthy," Mr. Blackwood said. "But I was left something. And I have kept it."

---

He rose, with effort, and went to a cupboard in the corner. It was locked; he took a key from a ring at his waist. From the shelf inside he lifted a large leather-bound volume, heavy as a family Bible, and laid it on the table between us.

"This," he said, "is the burial register for St. Piran's, from 1860 to the present."

I opened it.

Columns of names, in many hands. Dates. Ages. The cause, where known. *Drowned. Drowned. Fever. Drowned. Consumption. Drowned. Drowned.* The ink changed with every clerk, brown to black to brown. My fingers trembled on the pages.

"I have marked certain entries," said Mr. Blackwood. "In the margin. In pencil. There are twenty-three. These are the burials I conducted on the day after a wreck."

I turned to the first.

*14 Nov. 1863. Anne Marie. Eleven unknown seamen, buried together.*

Beside it, a faint pencil cross.

I turned. *1866. 1871. 1874.* A cross by each. I found 1879 and my breath snagged: *Sparrowhawk. 14.*

Seven pages on, in a different hand, the entry for the following spring.

*Halcyon. H.M. frigate. 211 hands. Buried as found.* And below, in smaller letters, a list of the identified dead, thirty-one names, with their ratings.

And then another list, shorter, headed *Not recovered.*

I read down it.

*Wray, Capt. Hallam, Surgeon (previous). Duffy, Midshipman. Penrose, W., Gunner's mate.*

And near the bottom, in a neat clerical hand:

*Thorne, Samuel. Age 20. Ordinary seaman. Lost.*

Beside it, a small, perfectly drawn cross. And a note, in the margin:

*Memorial stone placed at request of the brother. Churchyard, north wall.*

I looked up.

"I have seen the stone," I said. "Coming in. A little slab by the wall. I did not read it."

"It was erected by Gabriel Thorne at his own expense. Six winters ago. He stands there some evenings, in the dusk." Mr. Blackwood's eyes were distant. "I used to watch him from the window. I did not like to intrude."

"Why have you shown me this?"

"Because of what you will find on the next page."

---

I turned it.

It was a loose leaf, not bound into the book. A folded sheet of foolscap, tucked between the pages, soft with age. Written in a boy's hand, round and careless, the letters not quite level.

It was a list of dates. Seven of them. And beside each date, a word.

*Oct. 4. Vestry. Bread.*

*Nov. 9. Vestry. Bread, cheese.*

*Dec. 15. Vestry. Coat.*

I stared at it. "I don't understand."

"They are my own accounts," said Mr. Blackwood quietly. "Of charity. The entries of a parish priest who feeds those in want. There were many that winter. Many men came to this door without a coat."

"These dates—"

"Are from the last year."

"But—"

"Look at the signature."

At the foot of the page, scrawled in a looping hand beneath a column of figures, was a name.

*S. Thorne.*

The room went still. I could hear the wind in the chimney. I could hear my own heart.

"That cannot—"

"It can."

"Samuel Thorne is *dead.* He drowned six years ago. There is a stone."

"There is a stone." Mr. Blackwood rested his hands on the table. "And there is a man who signs for his bread in my vestry, on the fourth of each month, in the hour before dawn. He is thin and silent. He has a scar across his jaw. He eats with his hands, as a hunted creature does. And he asked me, the first time he came, not to tell his brother."

I put my hand over my mouth.

"He thinks Gabriel betrayed him," the priest said. "He believes his brother took Ashcombe's money. He believes that the light went out because his brother, a naval surgeon, was bought. He has been hiding in the abbey for six years, Miss Vale. Living on what he can steal and what I leave in the porch. Writing notes in the dark. And waiting."

"For what?"

"For you."

"For *me?*"

"For the daughter of Margaret Vale." His gaze fixed on mine. "He has known since the day you arrived that you would come to the lighthouse. That you would find Gabriel. That you would unmake the lie. He put out the lamp on Tuesday. He wrote you the note. He slid it under your door by way of the passage behind the pantry, which he has known since boyhood."

"He has been in my house."

"Several times. He has not harmed anyone. He has only watched."

I stood. I could not remain in the chair. My knees shook.

"Does Gabriel know?"

"No."

"Why haven't you told him?"

"Because Samuel forbade it. And because—" He closed his eyes. "Because I am old, and a coward, and I have broken enough promises."

"I must tell him."

"You must."

"Now."

"Miss Vale."

I stopped at the door.

"There is one thing more," said Mr. Blackwood. "The boy is ill. He has a cough. A bad one, wet, in the lungs. He cannot stay in that crypt another winter. And he has said he will not come out until he has seen the ledger put into the right hands." He swallowed. "He means you. I think he means to give you the means to destroy us all."

"Us?"

"The Vales. The Ashcombes. The island. The whole rotten structure." His voice cracked. "And God forgive me, I think he is right to."

I went out into the churchyard.

The wind hit me like a slap. The sky was enormous and pale, scoured clean, the color of a bone. Below me the cliff fell away in a long green slope to the sea. And at the north wall, among the gorse and the wind-bent grass, a small gray slab stood alone.

I walked to it.

*In memory of SAMUEL THORNE,*
*aged 20 years,*
*lost with H.M.S. Halcyon,*
*April 1885.*
*"He maketh the storm a calm."*

Someone had left a posy of sea-pinks at its foot, wired together with a bit of string. The petals were fresh.

I knelt.

And behind me, in the porch of St. Piran's, a thin figure in a long dark coat stepped back into the shadow, and watched me, and was gone.
