# Chapter Eight
## The Magistrate's Secret

*Adrian*

There is a particular silence that falls in a man's study after midnight, when the servants are abed and the fire has burned down to the shape of a face.

I have known it all my life.

It was the silence of my father's study, which I entered as a boy only to be punished and as a man only to be instructed. It was the silence of the courthouse, where I sat in judgment on fishermen who had taken a salmon out of season. It was the silence of the long years in London, when I dined with men who spoke of the island as one speaks of a pretty colony, and I nodded, and smiled, and counted the days.

Tonight it was the silence of a man deciding to burn a house down.

Not a house. A page.

I had it before me on the blotter, flattened under a brass paperweight shaped like a lighthouse. A foolish ornament. Eleanor had given it to me when she was fourteen and I was eighteen, wrapped in a handkerchief, with a note that said *so you will always know where home is.* I had kept it for sixteen years.

The page beneath it was a leaf from the Harbor Register for the autumn of 1879.

I knew every line of it by heart.

*Sept. 28. Wind SW, strong, rising. Barometer falling. Brig* Sparrowhawk, *Cardiff for Falmouth, 14 hands, sighted at four bells in the afternoon watch, standing in to shelter.*

*Light dark, by order.*

*Vessel struck at 12:08 a.m. All hands lost.*

Three lines in a clerk's neat copperplate. I had found them that afternoon in a tin box at the back of Marlowe House cellar, under a sack of seed potatoes, where my father had hidden them thirty years before and where, I suppose, he trusted I would never look.

*By order.*

By *his* order.

I read the lines again. I had read them four times already, and it did not help.

---

I should say here what I knew.

I have promised myself to be honest in these pages, though I do not know who will read them. Perhaps no one. Perhaps the fire. But it is a small mercy to set the thing down in order, so I will.

I knew that my father had darkened the light on stormy nights. I had known it since I was fifteen, when he took me up the cliff path in a gale and showed me, as another man might show his son how to fish, the three turns of the key that stopped the lamp. *It is an old arrangement,* he said. *It feeds the island. Your grandfather did it. His father did.* I asked him about the ships. He said, *The sea takes what it wants. We only help it decide.*

I was fifteen. I believed him.

I knew that Josiah Vale had a share. That every autumn a chest of silver was carried quietly up the Vale House drive, and that Margaret did not ask whence it came.

I knew, because I was told, that on the night of the twenty-eighth of September, 1879, my father had put out the lamp for the *Sparrowhawk.*

And I knew, because I was *there*, because I was twenty-two and had followed him to the cliff in the rain out of some stupid boy's curiosity, what happened on the gallery after Margaret Vale came up the stair.

I did not know what to call it.

I still don't.

I know only that when I came to myself I was standing on the rocks below the lighthouse with my arms in the sea to the shoulder, and that the thing I was holding was a woman's body, and that the water was black, and that there was blood on my hands that would not wash off.

Eleanor saw me. I knew it that night. She stood at the window of Vale House, a white blur behind the glass, and watched me carry her mother up the cliff.

I never knew how much she remembered.

I think I have been waiting twelve years for her to tell me.

---

A knock.

I looked up. Thorne stood in the doorway.

Not Gabriel Thorne. Never him. I would have known his tread. This was a boy of fifteen, in a wet jersey, one of the fishermen's sons; he held his cap in both hands and his eyes were round.

"Mr. Ashcombe, sir. I'm to give you this."

He held out an envelope. I took it. My name, in an unfamiliar, slanting hand, and below it, in smaller letters: *Tonight.*

"Who gave you this?"

"A man, sir. At the harbor wall."

"What man?"

"I couldn't see his face. He had a hood." The boy swallowed. "He said you'd know."

I slit the seal with my thumb.

*Mr. Ashcombe. You have a page that does not belong to you. Burn it, and Eleanor Vale lives. Keep it, and she will find it. I am not your enemy. I only want it finished. A friend.*

I read it three times.

Then I looked at the paper under my paperweight, and the paper in my hand, and thought: *Who knows I have it? Who could possibly have known?*

"Sir?" said the boy.

"Go home," I said. "Tell no one."

He fled.

---

I burned the page at one o'clock in the morning.

I held it by the corner over the fire and watched the flame take the first line, *Sept. 28,* and curl up the paper in a brown tongue. It was a small fire. It made almost no sound. The ink went black, then gold, then white. *Brig* Sparrowhawk *... Light dark, by order ... All hands lost ...*

I watched it all go.

When it was ash, I crushed it between my fingers and let it fall into the grate, and I stood there with my hand black to the wrist and thought: *I have done it again.*

*I have done what my father would have done.*

It is a curious thing, the shame of becoming one's father. It does not strike like a blow. It comes up like damp through a wall. One morning you find that your gestures are his gestures, and your silences his silences, and the very thing you have sworn all your life never to be is looking out at you from the mirror with your eyes.

I had sworn to protect her.

I had never once asked from what.

The clock struck two. Somewhere under the floor the old house groaned, settling in the cold.

I went to the window and drew the curtain.

Across the dark water, the lighthouse turned its patient arm. And at the foot of it, so far away that I might have invented it, a single point of yellow light burned in the keeper's cottage window.

*Thorne.*

I had seen them together on the cliff, that Tuesday. A hundred yards off, through a glass. I had watched Eleanor's face lift to his, and the way he stood very straight with his hands clenched at his sides, the way a man stands who is holding himself back from a fall. I had watched her touch him.

I had put the glass down and gone indoors and drunk a bottle of brandy, and not told a soul.

I loved her. I want to be plain about that, in this one place where it can do no harm. I loved her from the day she was fourteen and gave me a brass lighthouse wrapped in a handkerchief. I loved her through the years of silence, the letters she never answered, the long London winters. I loved her when she came home. I loved her when she looked at me across the dinner table with her mother's eyes and said *the truth.*

I would have given her anything.

I could not give her that.

Because the truth was a house with only one door, and on the other side of it, Margaret Vale lay in the water with her eyes open, and my father stood above her on the gallery with the lamp key in his hand, and I—

I did nothing.

That was the whole of it. That was the secret that had kept me awake for twelve years, and drawn me to the bottle, and pulled me into the magistrate's chair with a hand I could not control.

I did nothing.

I let it happen, and then I carried her, and then I helped him bury it.

---

At three, I went down to the cellar.

The tin box was where I had left it. I put the lid back on, and took it up the stairs, and out the back door into the garden, and I buried it beneath the roses with a spade I found by the shed. The earth was cold and wet. I dug until my arms ached, and when I had done I stood leaning on the handle and looked at the freshly turned soil, black and raw and unmistakable.

*Burn it, and Eleanor Vale lives.*

Whoever had written that note had understood me perfectly. They knew I would do anything for her. They had known exactly which string to pull.

I did not know whether they meant to help me or to make me complicit. I did not know whether it was Reed, or the Reverend, or one of the old sailors who had never forgiven the island. I only knew that I had been warned, and I had obeyed, and that in obeying I had become something I could never undo.

I put the spade back in the shed.

I washed my hands in the scullery sink, a long time, with carbolic soap, until the skin was raw.

The blood did not come off.

It never does.

But in the morning I put on a clean collar, and I took my hat, and I walked up the road to Vale House with a bunch of yellow chrysanthemums, because Eleanor's mother had loved them and Eleanor had said, once, that she did too.

Mrs. Crowe opened the door.

"Mr. Ashcombe."

"Mrs. Crowe. Is Miss Vale at home?"

She looked at me, hard, for slightly too long.

"She's in the music room," she said. "She's been playing since dawn."

I stood in the hall with the flowers in my hand and heard it. Chopin. A nocturne. Slow, grieving, perfect.

I stood and listened until the last chord faded.

Then I put the chrysanthemums in a vase, and straightened my cuffs, and went to tell the woman I loved a lie.
