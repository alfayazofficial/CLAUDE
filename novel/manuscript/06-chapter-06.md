# Chapter Six
## The Sound of the Sea

*Gabriel*

She came back on the third morning.

I had told myself she would not. I had told myself so with great firmness for two days, while I trimmed wicks and scrubbed brass and polished the Fresnel lens until I could see my own face distorted across a hundred glass prisms. A man can tell himself anything if he is busy enough.

At a quarter to eight I heard boots on the gravel, and I knew them as I would have known my own heartbeat.

I did not go to the door. I stood at the chart table with a rag in my hand and listened to her knock, and I counted to five, and then I opened it as though I had not been waiting.

She wore the gray coat again. Her cheeks were flushed from the climb. A few strands of dark hair had come loose in the wind, and she had not bothered to tuck them back, and I discovered that I was looking at her mouth.

I stopped.

"Mr. Thorne."

"Miss Vale. You are early."

"I thought you were a man who rose early."

"I thought you were a woman who took my advice."

"You were mistaken," said Eleanor, "in both cases," and walked past me into the cottage as if it were hers.

---

She laid the things out on my table one at a time, like a surgeon laying out instruments.

A black velvet bag. A faded sheet of paper, folded in eight, which when she unfolded it proved to be a map. A small iron key.

"I found a room," she said, "under the stairs of Vale House."

I said nothing.

"My mother kept it. For years, I think. It is full of her handwriting. She recorded every storm, Mr. Thorne. Every wreck. Every night the light failed." She tapped the map with one gloved finger. "Nineteen ships. Nineteen crosses. The last one is the night she died."

"The *Sparrowhawk*."

"You know the name."

"It's in the logbooks."

"Is it in the logbooks that fourteen men drowned?"

I looked at her. "Yes."

"And that the light was dark?"

"Yes."

"And that this is not a thing that happens by accident, nineteen times in sixteen years, always in a gale, always on the ship with the richest cargo?"

I put down the rag.

"No," I said. "It isn't."

She drew a breath, as though she had been braced for me to deny it. I think she had. It was a courageous breath, and it shook a little at the end.

"My mother found out. She wrote to the Bishop. She wrote to the Board of Trade. And then she died."

"Miss Vale—"

"Do you know who held the key to the lantern room twelve years ago?"

"The magistrate."

"Lord Ashcombe."

"Yes."

"Adrian's father."

I could have lied. It would have been so easy. But I had looked at her face in the doorway of this cottage on that first morning, and I had seen what it cost her to ask a question she already knew the answer to, and I found that I could not add to the weight.

"Yes," I said.

She closed her eyes.

For a moment, neither of us moved. Gulls cried on the cliff. The kettle ticked on the stove. And I thought, with the clarity of a man watching a wave come in: *I am going to touch her.* *I am going to put my hand on her shoulder, and there will be no going back.*

I put my hands in my pockets instead.

---

I took her up the tower because I could not think what else to do.

It is a hundred and twelve steps. The stair is iron, spiraling up through the whitewashed throat of the lighthouse, and by the fortieth she had stopped talking and by the eightieth she was breathing hard. I did not offer my hand. She did not ask for it. At the top she stood with her palm flat against the lantern room door and said, between breaths, "You might have warned me."

"I did. I said it was a long climb."

"You said *a lot of steps.* It's not the same."

"It's a lot of steps."

She laughed.

It was the first time. It startled both of us. It came out of her short and bright and genuinely surprised, like a bird flushed from a hedge, and she put her hand over her mouth, and her eyes above it were wet and shining.

I opened the door of the lantern room.

Morning poured in.

The glass walls blazed with it. Sky on every side, a bowl of blue and silver; the sea below like hammered metal; the whole island laid out like a map unrolled on a table. And in the center of it all, the great lens, taller than a man, a beehive of brass and prisms that took the morning and broke it into a thousand pieces, a thousand rainbows crawling over the walls.

Eleanor stood perfectly still.

"Oh," she said.

I have seen men weep at that sight. Seamen, hard as nails. I have seen a Trinity House inspector take off his hat. And I have lived with it for six years, and it still stops my breath, and I have never been able to say why. But I watched her face as the light moved across it, and I understood, in a rush, that I had been wrong. I had not been seeing it. I had been *waiting* to see it. For her.

"It's a lens," I said, uselessly. "It concentrates the lamp. One flame, twelve miles of reach."

"I know what it is."

"Do you."

"I've been looking at it from my window since I was five years old." She moved closer, and her fingertips hovered an inch from the glass. "I never imagined it was so *near.* Or so beautiful."

"It's very old."

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Be gruff," she said, turning. "You're doing it on purpose. You hide behind facts. I've been doing it for years in company, so I recognize the technique."

"What technique?"

"Telling the truth in such small pieces that no one can ever see the shape of the whole."

I opened my mouth and had nothing to put in it.

She smiled at me, a small tired knowing smile, and I felt the floor of my chest go out from under me.

---

We sat on the gallery afterward, on the stone, with our backs against the lantern wall and the sea a hundred feet below.

The wind was cold. She had taken off her gloves. Her hands were long and narrow, the fingers a little roughened at the tips, and I watched them lie in her lap and thought of the music I had heard from the cliff path two nights running.

"You play," I said.

She looked at me. "How do you know?"

"Vale House carries sound. The wind comes off the water, up the terraces. I've heard Schubert on three evenings."

"You listen to me?"

"I can't help it. It comes in through the glass."

"What did you think?"

I considered lying. I considered *very nice, Miss Vale.* Instead, I said, "That I've never heard anything lonelier."

She was quiet for a time.

"Do you know," she said, "what I was most afraid of, coming back? Not the house. Not the ghosts. I was afraid I would find that I'd forgotten her. That after all these years there would be nothing left except a portrait and an empty room, and I would have to pretend it hurt."

"And?"

"And I have found her handwriting in a hole beneath the stairs, and a map of the dead, and she is more alive to me than she has been since I was eighteen." Her voice caught. "And I can't tell whether I'm grateful, or whether I'm going to be sick."

"Both," I said. "Usually both."

She turned her head.

I had not meant to say it. It was out before I could stop it, and she heard in it what I had not meant to give. A small, quiet, terrible knowledge. The knowledge of someone who has also lost a person and cannot decide whether to curse the grief or the gift.

"Who?" she said.

"My brother."

"Samuel."

"Yes."

"Mrs. Crowe said he was never found."

"He wasn't."

"I'm so sorry."

"Don't be," I said. "It was a long time ago."

"Six years is not very long."

"No."

She reached out then, and laid her hand over mine on the stone.

It was a small thing. An ordinary gesture of sympathy, such as any decent woman might make. But her hand was warm and I had not been touched in six years. Not once. Not a hand, not a sleeve. And I felt it go through me like a current through a wire, down my arm and into my chest and out into the soles of my feet.

I did not move.

She did not move.

The wind moaned in the rail. Below us, a long swell rose and broke on the Teeth, a boom like a distant door.

"Gabriel," she said.

It was the first time she had used my name.

"Don't," I said hoarsely.

"Why?"

"Because I'll answer."

"Yes," said Eleanor, very softly. "I rather thought you might."

I took my hand out from under hers.

It was the hardest thing I have ever done. Harder than the amputations. Harder than the night of the wreck. I stood up, and I looked out to sea so that I would not look at her, and I said, "You're engaged to be married."

"I know."

"To a man I've given you every reason to distrust."

"I know that, too."

"Then go home. Please."

For a long time she sat where she was. Then she rose, brushing her skirts, and picked up her gloves, and stood at my shoulder looking out at the horizon.

"I'll go," she said. "But I'll be back tomorrow. And the day after. And when I come, Gabriel Thorne, you will tell me what you know, or I will find it without you."

"Eleanor—"

"I am not asking permission."

She went in. I heard her boots on the iron stair, a long ringing descent, fading, and then the door of the cottage, and then silence.

I stood on the gallery for a very long time.

In my pocket, wrapped in a square of lint, a single pearl pressed against my thigh like a closed eye.

I thought: *She has nine.*

I thought: *She has no idea what she's holding.*

And I thought, with a despair so clean it was nearly joy, that I would tell her. Everything. In time. And that when I did, she would never touch my hand again.
