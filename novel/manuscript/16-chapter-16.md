# Chapter Sixteen
## The Night We Surrendered

*Eleanor*

I did not ask him.

I want that understood, before anything else is told. I had Adrian's words in my pocket like a stone. *Ask him what he did with his hands.* I had walked the cliff path at dusk with the question lodged in my throat, and I had stood outside the cottage door in the thin blue light with my fist raised to knock, and I had known exactly what I would say.

Then the door opened.

He stood there without his coat, his shirt open at the neck, his hair wet from washing. He had a towel over one shoulder. He looked at me, and I saw the whole day's weariness drop from his face, replaced by something so naked and glad that I could not bear it.

"You came," he said.

"I said I would."

"I thought—after the quay. I thought you might have—"

"Come in out of the wind," I said, "before you catch cold."

He stepped aside.

I walked past him into the warm room, and he shut the door, and the sea sound went out of the world. The fire was lit. A pot of something simmered on the stove. On the table lay a loaf, a knife, a plate of cheese, and a blue enamel mug set out for two.

"You expected me."

"I hoped."

"Then you're a fool."

"I've been told."

I stood in the middle of the floor with my gloves in my hand.

I had one question, and he had one answer, and between us lay the whole bleak weight of a night twelve years gone. I could have said it. I opened my mouth.

Gabriel took a step toward me. "Eleanor," he said, very low. "If you've come to tell me to leave you alone, I'll do it. I swear I will. Whatever else I've failed at, I can do that."

"That's not why I came."

"Then why?"

I looked at him. At the scar above his ear. At the hollow of his throat, where a pulse beat quick and visible. At the hands he held away from his sides as though afraid of what they might do.

*Ask him what he did with his hands.*

"Tonight," I said, "I don't want to know anything."

"I don't—"

"No truths. No letters. No ledgers, no tides, no names." My voice shook. "One night, Gabriel. Out of all the nights there are. I want one that has nothing in it but the two of us."

He was silent.

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I will ask you whatever I must," I said. "And you will answer. And whatever you say, I'll have had tonight."

His eyes closed.

When he opened them, there was something in them that I would see for the rest of my life, in the dark, in my sleep, in the face of every stranger on every London street.

He crossed the room in three strides. He took my face between his hands, and bent his head, and for a long moment he only breathed against my lips, as though he wished to learn the shape of the air.

"Are you certain?"

"No," I said. "Are you?"

"No," said Gabriel. "God help us both."

---

I will not tell it all.

There are things a woman keeps. There are pages of a life that are not meant to be read by strangers, and I have written enough of other people's secrets to know the value of leaving one's own alone. But I will say a few things, because they are true, and because I have no other way to say them.

He undressed me as if I were something delicate and foreign that he had been charged to carry across a border. He took off my gloves first, finger by finger, and kissed the inside of each wrist where the pulse beat. He undid my hair. He found every hook and button of my gown, one at a time, and the room filled with the soft scrape of silk against skin. He was slow. He was endlessly, unbearably slow. I had thought myself brave and I discovered I was not, that I could be reduced to a trembling creature by a thumb drawn along a collarbone.

"Tell me if I hurt you," he whispered.

"You won't."

"Tell me anyway."

The cot was narrow. We did not care. The fire threw red light across the whitewashed walls and made a gold cave of the little room. I saw his scars: a long pale line down the forearm, a puckered mark on the shoulder, a seam of old surgery on the ribs. I traced each with my fingertips, and he shivered, and I thought: *this is a man who has been hurt in every way a man can be hurt.*

I said so.

He said, "I've been healed more than you know."

"By whom?"

"By tonight."

I do not remember what I answered. I remember that I laughed and then did not, that my breath caught on a sound that was not quite his name. I remember the weight of him, the heat of him, the astonishing gentleness of his hands. I remember a moment when he paused, trembling, and looked down at me as though I were a country he had only ever seen on a map, and said, "Eleanor. You're sure."

"Yes."

"I'll be careful."

"Be whatever you are," I said.

And he was.

---

*Gabriel*

I woke at three, in the dark, with her head on my chest.

The fire had burned to coals. The room glowed faintly, a red, drowsy, living gloom. Above us, through the open stairwell, the lamp turned and turned, its beam sweeping slowly across the ceiling like a hand smoothing a sheet. Outside the wind had dropped. The sea had gone quiet. It was the quietest night in six years.

She slept.

Her hair lay across my arm in a heavy dark spill. One hand was curled loosely against my ribs. In sleep her face was unlocked, the guardedness all gone, and she looked a girl of eighteen. I lay without moving, and I looked at her, and I thought:

*I will remember this.*

*When the rest of it is ash, I will remember this.*

I had known desire. I am not a monk. In the navy there are ports, and in ports there are rooms, and I had gone into some of them with other men's loneliness in my pockets and come out with a little less of my own. But I had never known this. This slow, aching, pouring-out. This tenderness so enormous it frightened me. I had held a man's heart in my hand once, in an operating theatre in Alexandria, still beating, and I had thought: *this is the most fragile thing there is.*

I had been wrong.

This was.

She stirred.

"Gabriel?"

"Here."

"Is it morning?"

"It's the middle of the night. Go back to sleep."

"You're thinking."

"I'm always thinking."

"About what?"

I brushed the hair from her cheek. "How it will end."

"Don't."

"I have to."

"You don't." She lifted her head. Her eyes were dark pools in the dark. "Not tonight. You promised."

"I did not."

"You didn't refuse."

A breath of laughter. "That is a lawyer's argument."

"I'm marrying a magistrate. I've picked up the trade."

I kissed her. I could not help it. I kissed her mouth, her chin, the corner of her eye, the soft place under her jaw, and she made a small sound and slid up my body and pressed her lips to my throat, and the thought of endings went out of the world like a candle in a draught.

Afterward she lay with her cheek on my shoulder and her fingers moving idly on my chest, tracing the scar over my heart.

"What's this one?"

"A bullet."

"In the navy?"

"In Alexandria. There was a riot."

"You never told me."

"I never told anyone."

"Why not?"

I thought for a long time.

"Because it would have been a story," I said. "And I didn't want to be anyone's story. I've been a lot of things. A boy with a drowned father. A surgeon. A coward. A keeper. I never wanted to be a tale told in the dark."

"You're not a tale."

"No?"

"You're a man." She raised herself on one elbow. "Gabriel Thorne, aged thirty-two, who keeps a lamp, who has a bullet scar, who is afraid of a letter in his own coat. I know you. I do not need a tale."

I could not speak.

She laid her palm flat against my face.

"Whatever you did on that gallery," she said, softly, "I think it was not what you believe."

I went rigid.

"Eleanor—"

"No. Not tonight. I only wanted you to hear it." She leaned down and kissed my forehead. "I have been looking at you for weeks. I know what a coward looks like. They don't hold a letter against their hearts for twelve years. They don't stay."

I closed my eyes.

She lay down again. I put my arm about her. We said nothing more. Her breath slowed and deepened, and the red light on the ceiling dimmed, and the lamp went round and round overhead, and I lay awake beside her with her words lodged in me like a splinter that was also a blessing.

*I think it was not what you believe.*

She did not know.

She could not.

But oh, God, I wanted to believe her.

---

*Eleanor*

I woke before dawn, with the cold on my face.

Gabriel was up, standing at the window in his shirtsleeves with the shutters open an inch. A strip of pale gray lay across his shoulder. He did not turn. He stood with his hands on the sill, looking out at a sky just beginning to separate from the sea.

I watched him for a long while.

Then I rose, wrapped the blanket around me, and went to stand at his side.

"The light's still burning," I said.

"I'll put it out in a moment."

"Don't. Not yet."

He glanced down. The sunrise caught the edge of his face, making a gold line along his cheek.

"You'll be missed."

"I know."

"Corbett will be at the gate."

"I know that, too." I slipped my hand into his. "Let him wait."

Down on the water, the first fishing boats were putting out. Little brown sails, dotted across a bruised, lilac sea. The air smelled of salt and woodsmoke. A single gull stood on the cliff edge, motionless, as though it, too, were listening.

"Eleanor."

"Hm?"

"Whatever happens."

I turned. His face was grave.

"I wanted you to know. This was not a mistake."

"I never thought it was."

"I know. But I wanted to say it."

I stood on my toes and kissed him, once, softly, the way you might seal a letter.

Then I dressed, and he put out the lamp, and we walked together to the door of the cottage and stood a minute on the threshold in the cold bright morning.

The island lay below us, small and wet and shining.

I took a breath.

And all down the long cliff road, from the stile to the gate of Vale House, I saw a single figure waiting. A big square man in a black coat. His hands were clasped behind him. He had been standing there, motionless, for who knew how long.

Nathaniel Corbett lifted his head, and looked, and did not wave.
