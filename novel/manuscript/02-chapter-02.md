# Chapter Two
## The Man at the Lighthouse

*Eleanor*

I did not sleep.

I sat in the window seat with my mother's letter in one hand and the stranger's note in the other, and I watched the lighthouse until the dark thinned to gray.

The beam had returned a little after midnight. It swept across the water exactly as it always had, steady and indifferent, as though it had never failed at all.

By the time the first gulls began to cry over the cliffs, I had read both papers so many times that the words had stopped being words. They had become shapes. Wounds.

*Do not go to the lighthouse after midnight.*

*Your mother did not die alone.*

*Gabriel Thorne.*

One paper warned me away from the lighthouse. The other pointed straight at the man who kept it.

Someone was playing a game with me. I only had to decide whether I was foolish enough to play.

---

"Who is Gabriel Thorne?"

Mrs. Crowe did not look up from the teapot.

The breakfast room was cold. The fire had not yet taken, and the windows were beaded with salt. She poured with a steady hand, but I had been watching her hands since the day before, and I noticed that the tea reached the rim of the cup and stopped there for a moment too long.

"The keeper, miss."

"I know that. I can see the lighthouse from my window."

"Then you know as much as most do."

"I do not want to know as much as most do."

She set down the pot.

"He came six years ago," she said at last. "After old Pennick drowned. The Trinity House sent a man to replace him, and the man they sent was Mr. Thorne."

"From where?"

"The navy, they say." She straightened a spoon that did not need straightening. "A surgeon, once. Some trouble with a ship."

"What trouble?"

"A wreck, miss. The *Halcyon*. It went down off the Mare's Teeth in a spring gale. His brother was aboard."

"Was he lost?"

"He was never found. The sea doesn't always give them back."

I thought of my mother's letter. *The sea has finally returned what I tried to bury.*

"And Mr. Thorne keeps to himself?"

"He keeps to the lighthouse." Her eyes met mine, and for the first time that morning, they were not careful. "Miss Eleanor. Leave that man alone."

"Why?"

"Because he has a way of finding people who are looking for something."

"And what is it you think I am looking for?"

Mrs. Crowe took up the pot again.

"Whatever it is," she said, "the island will not thank you for finding it."

---

The path to the lighthouse began behind the walled garden and climbed through the gorse to the cliff edge.

I took it a little after nine, in a gray coat and walking boots I had not worn since London, with my mother's letter folded against my ribs. I told no one. Adrian had sent word that he would call at noon, and I intended to be home before then, with a lie ready on my tongue and an answer in my hands.

The morning was washed clean. The storm had blown itself out in the night, and the sky had the pale, scoured look of a thing that had been crying. Below me, the sea moved slowly against the rocks. It sounded like something breathing in its sleep.

I had walked this path a thousand times as a child.

I had never once reached the end of it.

*Don't go up there, Ellie. It's no place for you.*

My mother's voice, twelve years gone and still perfectly clear.

I stopped. The wind pulled at my hair. A quarter of a mile ahead, the lighthouse rose from the headland like a white bone, banded in black, its gallery rail bright against the sky.

Beside it stood a low stone cottage with a slate roof and a curl of smoke rising from the chimney.

Someone was awake.

I walked on.

---

The door of the cottage was open.

I did not mean to look inside. I meant to knock, to announce myself, to behave as a lady behaves. But the room beyond the threshold was so unlike anything I had expected that I forgot to do any of it.

I had imagined a hermit's hovel. Instead I saw order.

Charts pinned neatly along one wall. A narrow cot, made tight as a soldier's. A table covered in brass instruments, each one polished until it gleamed. And books—so many books, stacked on the floor and the windowsill and the mantelpiece, their spines worn pale by handling.

A kettle sang on the stove.

"You're a long way from the road, Miss Vale."

I turned.

He stood at the foot of the lighthouse stair, one hand resting on the iron rail, a coil of rope over his shoulder. He was taller than I had expected. His sleeves were rolled to the elbow, his forearms pale and corded and marked with old, thin scars. His hair was dark and too long, and the wind had pushed it back from a face that looked as though it had been carved by someone in a hurry.

He was not handsome, exactly.

But he was the kind of man you remembered later, in the dark, when you were trying not to.

His eyes were gray.

Not the gray of the sea. The gray of the instant before a storm.

"You know my name," I said.

"Everyone on the island knows your name." He lowered the rope. "They've been saying it for a week."

"I did not intend to be a spectacle."

"Then you chose the wrong house to return to."

It should have been rude. It was not. There was something in the way he said it, quiet and unhurried, that made it sound less like an insult and more like a diagnosis.

"Mr. Thorne," I said.

"Miss Vale."

"I believe you wrote to me."

Something moved across his face. It was so brief that I might have invented it.

"I haven't written to anyone in a long time."

"Then someone else has been using your name."

I took the second note from my coat and held it out.

He did not take it at once. He looked at the paper, and then at me, and then at the paper again, as though weighing the cost of touching it. When he finally reached for it, his fingers brushed mine.

His hand was warm.

His hand was shaking.

He read the six words. He read his own name beneath them. I watched his jaw tighten, a slow, controlled movement, the movement of a man holding something back by force.

"Where did you find this?"

"Beneath my door. Last night."

"What time?"

"Some hours after midnight."

"And the first letter?"

I stared at him.

"How do you know there was a first letter?"

"Because this one begins in the middle." He folded the note along its crease and gave it back to me. "Whoever sent it expects you to have already received something else."

I said nothing.

"You received something else," he said.

"I did."

"May I see it?"

"No."

The word came out sharper than I intended. He only nodded, as though that had been the answer he expected, and he stepped back from the stairs and gestured at the room.

"Then you had better come in and tell me why you've climbed a cliff in walking boots to accuse me of something."

"I have not accused you."

"No," he said. "Not yet."

---

He made tea without asking whether I wanted it.

I sat at the table with my hands folded and my back straight, as my mother had taught me, and I let him pour. The cup was chipped. The tea was strong enough to stand a spoon in. He took the chair opposite and did not drink.

"Your light went out last night," I said.

"Did it?"

"At twelve minutes before midnight. I watched it from my window."

"Then you watched a failure of the mechanism."

"Is that what it was?"

He was silent for a long moment.

"The lamp was extinguished by hand," he said. "I found the lantern room door unlocked at a quarter past twelve, the wick turned down and the clockwork stopped. I relit it myself."

"You were not there when it went dark?"

"I was in the oil store, at the foot of the tower. I did not hear a footstep on the stair." His thumb moved along the rim of the cup. "I keep the key on my person. There is only one other."

"Who has the other?"

"The magistrate."

My heart gave a single, hard beat.

"Adrian."

"Mr. Ashcombe holds the other key," Gabriel said evenly, "as a courtesy of the Board. It has not been used in six years."

"Someone used a key last night."

"Or someone is very clever with a pick."

"Why would anyone put out the light?"

He looked at me then, directly, and for the first time I understood that the thing in his eyes was not hostility.

It was fear.

"Because on the night your mother died," he said, "the light went out too."

---

The wind dropped.

I could hear the sea, and the kettle ticking as it cooled, and the sound of my own pulse.

"You were not here then," I said carefully. "Mrs. Crowe says you came six years ago."

"I have said very little to anyone."

"Then how do you know the light went out that night?"

He did not answer.

I leaned forward. My hands were trembling now, and I flattened them against the table so he would not see.

"Mr. Thorne. I was eighteen. I was at the window. I saw a woman walking toward this lighthouse in the rain, and I saw the beam die, and I have spent twelve years pretending that I invented it. Now a stranger writes me notes in the dark and puts your name at the bottom, and you sit there and tell me what happened the night my mother died as though you had watched it yourself."

"I did not watch it."

"Then *how*—"

"There is a record," he said. "In the logbook. Every keeper writes the same entry on the same night, every year. *Light extinguished at 11:52 p.m. by order of the Magistrate. Restored 1:14 a.m.* The first keeper recorded it. Pennick copied it. I copied it."

"Why would a magistrate order a lighthouse darkened?"

"I do not know."

"You are lying."

It was not a guess. I watched the muscle move in his cheek, and I knew.

He rose from the chair, crossed to the shelf, and took down a ledger bound in black cloth. He laid it on the table between us, open at the last page.

The entry was dated the night before.

*Light extinguished at 11:52 p.m. Not by order of the Magistrate. Restored 12:19 a.m.*

Beneath it, in the same hand, he had added a line.

*She has come home.*

I could not breathe.

"I wrote that at one o'clock this morning," Gabriel said. "Before I knew your name had been put on any note. I wrote it because for twelve years the light has gone out on the anniversary of her death and nothing has happened. And last night, for the first time, it was *you* who went out into the dark to find out why."

"I did not go out."

"No," he said. "But you were watching. Weren't you?"

I had been.

God help me, I had been.

---

I left him at half past ten.

He walked me to the door of the cottage and no farther. The air outside had warmed a little, and the gorse smelled of honey and salt, and I stood in the sun with my mother's letter against my ribs and tried to decide whether I had just spoken to an honest man.

"Miss Vale."

I turned.

He was standing in the doorway with the light behind him, and I could not read his face.

"If you are wise," he said, "you will go back to the house and tell Mr. Ashcombe that the lighthouse is of no interest to you."

"And if I am not wise?"

"Then you will come back."

"Is that an invitation, Mr. Thorne?"

"It is a warning."

"They are beginning to sound very much alike."

Something that was almost a smile touched the corner of his mouth, and was gone.

"Yes," he said quietly. "I had noticed that."

I walked down the cliff path without looking back.

I did not see him step out of the doorway, or the way his hand went to his waistcoat pocket the moment I turned my back.

---

*Gabriel*

I watched her until she was a gray shape against the gorse.

Then I opened my hand.

A single pearl lay in my palm. Round, flawless, the size of a pea. I had carried it for twelve years, through two ships, one wreck, and every night of six winters at the top of this tower.

I had seen its sisters only once, strung at a woman's throat, on the last night of her life.

I had not told Eleanor Vale that I knew her mother's face.

I had not told her that I was the last person to hear her mother's voice.

And I had not told her that the letter in her coat was not the only one her mother left behind.
