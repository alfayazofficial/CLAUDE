# Chapter Nine
## The Night of the Shipwreck

*Gabriel*

She came on the fifth day, and she did not knock.

It was dusk. I was in the kitchen of the cottage, scraping the skin off a pot of porridge that had scorched, because I had stopped eating properly and had begun to treat meals as an inconvenience, and I heard the latch lift. I turned with the spoon in my hand.

Eleanor stood in the doorway in the red light of the dying sun.

She had been crying. She had stopped. The traces were still there, faint and pink around the eyes, and her chin was lifted in a way I was beginning to know.

"I have not forgiven you," she said.

"I didn't expect you to."

"I've read my mother's diary."

"All of it?"

"There are pages missing."

I put down the spoon.

"But I know enough," Eleanor said. "I know she had a friend, a young man she called *our G.* I know she paid for his schooling. I know she was frightened of something, and that she trusted him. And I know that you are he, and you never told me." She drew a breath. "I came to say that I will hear it. Everything. From the beginning. And that I will not interrupt."

"Not tonight."

"Gabriel."

"There's a part of it I can't tell you yet." I met her eyes. "But there's another part I can. It's the part you asked me first, on the first morning. Why I abandoned the navy. Why I came to this place. I'll tell you that. If you'll sit."

She looked at me for a long moment.

Then she took off her gloves, and sat down at my table, and folded her hands.

---

"I was surgeon's second on the *Halcyon,*" I said.

I will tell it as I told it then. I find I cannot tell it any other way.

"She was a frigate, ten years old, built at Chatham. A good ship. Four hundred men, give or take. I'd been with her three years; I'd gone out as a junior dresser and been promoted when Dr. Hallam died of fever at Alexandria. I was twenty-six. I had a cabin the size of a coffin and a medicine chest and more responsibility than I knew what to do with. And I had my brother."

"Samuel."

"Samuel was seventeen when I took him aboard, and twenty by the night of the wreck. Our mother was dead; our father had been drowned off Bude. I'd been a poor guardian, always away. I thought I might make him into something. A midshipman, perhaps. He had a quick head. He could read a chart by moonlight." My voice thickened. "He was the best of us."

Eleanor said nothing. She had said she would not interrupt, and she was a woman who kept her word.

"We were homeward bound. Three years on the station, a cargo of invalids and brass and two hundred barrels of Madeira. It was April. The Channel had been wicked for a week. We'd been two days beating up against a south-westerly with the storm jib split, and the captain was a careful man, Captain Wray, and he did not want to anchor in open water with a rising glass."

The window glowed behind her. The light moved on the table between us, red going to rose.

"He knew of a harbor. A refuge, on the Cornish coast, with a lighthouse. A good old light, he said. On the Mare's Teeth. Visible twelve miles. We'd run in under its lee and ride out the night.

"We raised it at nine o'clock. I was in the sick bay, strapping a man's ribs. I heard the lookout call, and I heard a cheer go along the deck, and I thought: *thank God.* I thought it would be over soon."

"And the light—"

"It was burning." I stared at the grain of the table. "It was burning at nine. At ten. At eleven. We made our approach along the line of the beam, as every sailor on earth would have done, and there was a swell running, and a hard flaw out of the southwest, and at five minutes to midnight, the light went out."

I could hear her breathing.

"It did not flicker. It did not fade. It went out like a hand over a candle. One moment there was a lighthouse, and the next there was a hole in the sky where it had been." I swallowed. "And the captain shouted for the helmsman to bring her about, and the helmsman couldn't see to do it, and I felt the whole ship lift under my feet and come down on something that was not water."

"The Teeth."

"The Teeth." I pressed my palm flat on the wood. "She broke her back in eleven minutes. I counted. I counted because I could not do anything else. I was on the orlop deck with forty men in hammocks and the whole ship was screaming. The water came in through the gunports. It came up the companionway like a white tongue. I got as many out as I could. Eleven. I carried a boy up the ladder with a broken leg, and when I came on deck there was nothing on the sea but wreckage and men, and the rain, and the dark."

"And Samuel?"

"I saw him once."

I stopped.

She did not speak. She did not touch me. I will never forget that; she simply waited, her hands very still, and let the silence be as long as it needed to be.

"He was clinging to a spar. Forty yards off. I shouted. He saw me. He raised an arm. I swam to him, or tried to. There was a wave." I shut my eyes. "I remember the wave. I remember it standing up behind him like a wall. And then he wasn't there, and I was in the water, and something hit me in the head."

I touched the old scar above my left ear. I did it without thinking. I had done it a thousand times.

"They found me on the beach at dawn. Two fishermen. I'd washed ashore half a mile from here, with a cracked skull and a lung full of salt water. I lay in a bed at the Reed house for three weeks, and when I woke I learned that two hundred and eleven men had died, and that the lighthouse keeper, old Pennick, had been found drowned at the foot of his own tower the morning after."

Eleanor made a small sound.

"And I knew," I said. "I knew in my body, as you know a thing in a dream. I'd stood at that rail. I'd watched that light. It does not go out by itself. It was *put* out. Someone knew we were coming, and they put it out, and they let two hundred men die."

"Gabriel—"

"I went to the Admiralty. Court of Inquiry. I told them everything. They listened. They thanked me. They said the light had failed through the negligence of a drunken keeper, God rest his soul, and that no further inquiry was necessary." A laugh, ugly and short. "A drunken keeper. Pennick, who'd never taken a drink in his life. They wrote it up and filed it. I was told I had suffered a blow to the head and that my recollections of the sequence of events were unreliable. They offered me a pension and a rest cure."

"And you refused."

"I resigned my commission. I wrote to Trinity House. They had a vacancy. They did not ask why a naval surgeon wanted to keep a lamp on a headland in the middle of nowhere." I looked up at last. "I came to find out who had killed my brother."

---

There was a long silence.

The sun had set. The red had drained out of the room, and the kettle had begun to cool, and in the corner the stove ticked softly as the iron contracted. Eleanor's face was in shadow. I could not see her eyes.

"You did not come to find out who killed Samuel," she said at last. "You came to find out who killed my mother."

I went very still.

"You said it yourself," she went on. "On the first day. The light goes out on the anniversary. You had the logbook. You knew what I would find. You were not simply a survivor. You were *looking.* You'd been looking since you arrived, because the island's secrets were already in you."

"Yes."

"Because you knew her."

"Yes."

"Because you were there that night."

I did not answer.

I did not need to. She had stopped being a woman who needed answers. She was a woman who read faces, and mine was an open book, and I watched her read it. Watched the understanding come into her features, slow and terrible, like a dawn after a disaster.

"Gabriel," she whispered. "What did you do?"

"Not tonight."

"What did you *do?*"

"I will tell you," I said. "I swear on my brother's name. I will tell you when I have something that can prove it. Not a confession, Eleanor. A *fact.* Because I've sworn a thousand oaths in six years and every one of them has turned to water in my mouth, and I will not give you another that I cannot keep."

She stood.

She stood slowly, one hand on the back of the chair, and for a moment I thought she would strike me. Instead she crossed the little room, three paces, and stood before me in the dark.

"Look at me," she said.

I looked.

Her eyes were wet. Her mouth was set. She was the loveliest and most terrifying thing I had ever seen.

"I do not know you," she said. "I do not know whether you are a murderer or a martyr. I know that my mother loved you. I know that you flinch when I touch you. And I know that, God help me, when you told me about your brother, I believed every word."

"Then—"

"So we will do this together," said Eleanor Vale. "You and I. We'll find the proof. The ledger. The names. The witness. Whatever it is. And when we have it, you'll tell me the rest. And I will decide."

"What if you decide against me?"

"Then I will have been wrong about you," she said. "And I will have found out in time."

She held out her hand.

It was the right hand, bare, with the long fingers and the roughened tips. It trembled a little. I looked at it for a long time.

Then I took it.

It was cold, and it was strong, and it did not let go.

Outside the sea breathed in and out against the Teeth, patient as the dead, and the first lamp of the evening spun up in the tower above us, and began to turn.
