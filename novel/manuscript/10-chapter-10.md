# Chapter Ten
## The Diary

*Eleanor*

Mrs. Crowe brought me the missing pages at six in the morning, folded inside a hymnal.

She did not knock. She came into my bedroom with a candle and a cup of tea I had not asked for, and set the tea on the nightstand, and stood by the bed with the hymnal pressed to her chest like a shield. In the guttering light she looked a hundred years old.

"I told you I'd say what I could," she said. "This is the rest of what I can."

"What is it?"

"Your mother tore them out herself. The week before she died. She gave them to me and said, *If anyone comes asking for the diary, Hester, give them the book and not these.*" She held out the hymnal. Her hand shook. "I have kept them in a place no one would look. The Book of Common Prayer is not a thing a man like Mr. Ashcombe would trouble to open."

I sat up. I took it.

Inside the cover, tucked between *Hymns Ancient and Modern* and the flyleaf, four sheets of cream notepaper had been folded in thirds. My mother's hand. The ink had faded to the brown of dried tea.

"Why now?" I whispered.

"Because I watched you come in from the rain on Thursday with your face like a ghost's," said Mrs. Crowe. "Because I am sixty-six years old and I have been afraid for twelve of them. And because that man up on the headland looked at you from his doorway as though you were the last thing in the world he was permitted to want." She turned to go. "Read it, miss. And then decide whether you'd have been better off not."

---

*3rd June, 1879.*

*I have done something I swore I would not do. I went down to the abbey crypt alone.*

*I should say how it came about. The Reverend asked me in April to catalogue the old parish papers, which have lain in tea chests since the roof fell, and I said yes, for it gives me an excuse to leave the house, and J. likes to think I am being charitable. So I have been going three afternoons a week to the vestry. Dust, mice, a sleeping cat. Baptisms and burials and a hundred years of bad handwriting.*

*Today, behind a rotting press, I found a door.*

*It was not locked. It was only stuck, swollen with damp. I forced it with the poker. Behind it, a stair going down. I took a lantern. At the bottom I found a chamber cut in the living rock, perhaps forty feet long, that opens on the seaward side to the water. At low tide you can walk to the edge. At high tide it fills to the shoulder.*

*On the walls, scratched in the stone with some sharp instrument, were names.*

*Hundreds of them.*

*Next to the names, dates. Next to the dates, a ship.*

*I stood there with the lantern shaking in my hand and read until I could not read for tears. They were the drowned. Someone, over the centuries, had been recording them. Marking each as it came. A priest, perhaps. A fisherman's widow. They had come down in the dead of night and scratched the names on the wall where the tide would wash them, so that the sea might keep what the island would not.*

*The last I could read was dated 1871.*

*Below it was a space, a long smooth space of bare stone. As if the hand that wrote had grown too tired to go on.*

---

*14th June.*

*I have found the ledger.*

*It was in the same chamber, in an iron box in a niche above the water line. It is bound in oilcloth and tied with a cord. On the first page, in an old clerk's hand: "A True Account of the Island's Share."*

*I will not set down here what it says. I will say only that I recognized the handwriting of Josiah Vale's father in the third column. And the Ashcombes, father and son, in the fifth. And a figure in the margin, in pounds, per ship.*

*I am married to a man whose family has been paid by the sea for sixty years.*

*I am the mother of a child whose bread, whose silk, whose piano, were bought with the lives of drowned men.*

*I did not weep. I sat on the damp floor for an hour with the ledger in my lap and I found that I was not even surprised.*

---

*29th June.*

*Eleanor cut her hand on the garden gate today and I held her while Dr. Reed dressed it. She cried and then she laughed. She is eighteen, and she is still so frightened of everything and so sure that I will keep her safe.*

*I cannot keep her safe. I am only learning this now.*

*Reed's new apprentice came with him to the house. A tall dark boy, the sort who can't decide where to put his hands. He took the dressing from Reed without being asked and finished the bandage so neatly that Eleanor admired it. She asked his name.*

*"Thorne, miss. Gabriel Thorne."*

*"Do you mean to be a surgeon?"*

*"If I can afford the college."*

*I looked at the quiet worn face and thought: here is someone who is not afraid of blood.*

---

*9th July.*

*I have helped G. with his fees. I told him it was from a fund of Papa's, for deserving boys. He is clever enough to know I lie. He took the money anyway, with such a look of shamed gratitude that I had to turn away to the window.*

*He has told me about his brother. The little one, Samuel. Fourteen, apprenticed to a fisherman at the north harbor, and (I gather) dreadfully light-fingered. G. says the boy takes after their mother. I said, laughing, that all the best people do.*

*He did not laugh back.*

---

*22nd July.*

*I have told G. about the ledger.*

*I had not meant to. I was alone in the vestry with him, helping carry tea chests, and he said something kind, and the whole dreadful week came up in me like sickness. I told him everything. He listened in silence. When I had done he said, "Mrs. Vale, you must tell the magistrate."*

*"The magistrate is Lord Ashcombe."*

*Silence.*

*"Then you must tell someone who isn't," G. said. "The Bishop. The Board of Trade. Anyone."*

*I have written to them. I do not expect a reply.*

---

*14th August.*

*I cannot write what I saw tonight. I will write only that I was in my room, at the window, and I saw a light go out on the headland. That is nothing. It goes out sometimes when the wind catches the shutters. But it went out at the very moment J. came into the room behind me, and he looked at the window, and then at me, and there was something in his face I had never seen in twenty years of marriage.*

*Calculation.*

*"You've been in the crypt," he said.*

*"I have not."*

*"Margaret."*

*"I have been cataloguing the parish papers."*

*"You have been going places you should not."*

*His voice was perfectly gentle. That was the horror of it.*

*I have been married to a stranger.*

*I have given G. a sealed letter and told him to keep it in his breast. Not to open it. To give it to Eleanor on the day she is a woman grown, if anything should happen. He asked, "What should happen?" and I said, "I don't know." He took it. He put it inside his coat. He said, "I'll never let it out of my sight."*

*He is twenty. God keep him.*

---

I read the four pages three times.

Then I folded them along the creases my mother had made, and lay back on the pillow, and looked at the plaster ceiling until the dawn turned it from black to gray.

The crypt. The names. The ledger. The gentle voice of a man who had held my hand at my mother's funeral and bought me a pony for my sixteenth birthday.

A boy of twenty with a letter inside his coat.

*I'll never let it out of my sight.*

I thought of a velvet bag, nine pearls, a breaking string. I thought of a dark head bent over a medical textbook, a pressed sea-pink marking his page, and a pearl wrapped in lint against a man's thigh.

*I will tell you when I have a fact.*

He had it. He had always had it. It had been against his heart for twelve years, sealed with my mother's wax.

I rose. I dressed in the dark, without ringing for the maid.

I did not go to the lighthouse.

I went to the music room, and sat at the Broadwood, and played the Schubert from the beginning, softly, with the pedal down. I played it again. I played until the sun came into the room and lay along the keys like a hand.

I had been wrong about one thing.

I had thought the worst would be learning that my mother had been betrayed.

It was not.

It was learning that she had been *brave.*

---

*Reading the diary was meant to give me a name. Instead it gave me a debt.*

*That was the thing nobody tells you about the dead. They leave you what they could not finish. And they do it so lovingly that you cannot refuse.*

I wrote these lines in the margin of my own copy of Schubert, in pencil, that morning, and I do not know why. Perhaps it was to see them. Perhaps it was only because I had to put the grief somewhere other than my body.

I am glad I did.

Years later, when the island had forgotten almost everything else, someone found that page, still pencilled and faintly gray, tucked in the lid of the Broadwood.

By then it was the only part of me left that spoke plainly.
