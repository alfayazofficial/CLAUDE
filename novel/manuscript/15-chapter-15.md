# Chapter Fifteen
## The Price of Devotion

*Adrian*

I called on her at noon, with a card, like a gentleman.

It was her rebuke, delivered through Corbett, and it was just. I had sent my steward to do what I had not the stomach to do myself. I should have gone in person. I should have come to the surgery in the dark, and stood in the street, and watched her face as she told me what she had seen.

Instead I had sat at my desk and let a man I despised bring me her words in pieces.

*Tell Mr. Ashcombe that I'd be obliged if he'd call on me in the proper way.*

I had read it aloud, twice, to the empty room. And then I had put on my best coat.

She received me in the morning room. The sun came in low through the windows, laying long gold bars on the Turkey carpet, and she sat in the window seat with a book open in her lap and did not rise. She was in gray wool, her hair coiled low on her neck. She looked tired and very young. Her hands lay quiet on the page.

I stood in the doorway with my hat and could not speak.

"Adrian."

"Eleanor."

"You will want to sit."

"I would rather stand."

"As you wish." She turned a page she was not reading. "Have you found Dr. Reed?"

I could have lied. It was the easier road, and I took it for a heartbeat. *Gone to Falmouth. A patient. Back within the week.* The words were there on my tongue, round and smooth as pebbles.

Instead I said, "He is in my cellar."

---

I will describe what happened to her face. I find I want to describe it exactly, because it is the last time I saw it unguarded.

She did not move. Her lips parted, very slightly. The color went out of her cheeks, not all at once but in a slow tide, and her eyes, those dark honest eyes, widened and then narrowed. A kind of wild calculation passed through them. I watched her weigh fear against anger and decide, with cold and terrible clarity, to feel neither.

"Alive?"

"Alive. Drunk, and extremely frightened, and very much in need of a bath. Corbett's men found him at the quay. He was trying to board a boat to Penzance with a bag of papers and no spectacles."

"Why?"

"Because he is a coward, my dear. Because he had a letter from a man on the mainland saying that I was about to hang him." I permitted myself a dry smile. "I said no such thing. But a man who has done what he has done begins to see gallows in every shadow."

"What has he done?"

"He signed a certificate."

"I know that. You *took* him."

"I took him," I said, "because if I had not, someone else would have. And that someone would not have been so careful of his life."

She laid the book aside.

"You'll let me see him."

"No."

"Adrian—"

"Not yet." I came into the room at last and sat opposite her. I put my hat on my knee. "I will tell you why. And then I'll tell you everything I can, which is more than you think. And at the end of it you may do as you wish."

"I'm listening."

I looked at my hands.

"My father," I said, "was a monster."

---

I told it plainly. I had practiced the speech on the road from Marlowe House, and it all went out of my head, and I found that the truth did not need rehearsing.

I told her that the Ashcombes had been wreckers for four generations. That my grandfather had bought the magistracy with salvage money. That the lantern key, one of two, had been passed from father to son the way another family hands down a watch.

I told her that I had learned it at fifteen.

I told her that I had been taken up the cliff path in a gale and shown the three turns of the key, and that the lesson had been delivered with a firm hand on my shoulder and a gleam in my father's eye, as a farmer shows his son the slaughter of a lamb.

I told her that when I was twenty-two I had stood beside him on the night of the *Sparrowhawk,* and that I had been sick in the grass.

"And your father pushed my mother," Eleanor said.

My heart stopped.

"Gabriel told you."

"He said the rail was broken. He said Lord Ashcombe was standing over it. He said he looked down and saw only water." She regarded me steadily. "I'm not stupid, Adrian. I've known since the first night. I only wanted to hear you say it."

I could not.

I sat with the words behind my teeth, and felt them press and shift like something alive. I thought: *If I tell her, I will lose her.* I thought: *If I do not, she will find it. And then I will lose her anyway.*

It is the great cruelty of the thing. There was no road that led back.

"I cannot say it," I said at last.

"Why?"

"Because I do not know it."

"You were *there*."

"I was on the stair." My own voice sounded strange, thin, like something heard through a wall. "It was dark. It was storming. I heard a cry, a man's voice, and then a crash of timber. I came up the last steps and the gallery was empty. The rail hung over the sea. My father stood at the edge with his back to me."

"And Gabriel?"

"I did not see Thorne."

It was almost true.

I had not seen him *arrive.* I had seen him *after.* A tall boy, soaked, on his knees at the rail, staring at his own hand as though it belonged to someone else.

I did not say that.

I said, "I ran down the rocks. I got to her. She was in the water, face up. I thought—" My voice cracked. "I thought she might be alive."

"And?"

"She was warm."

There was a long silence.

"I carried her up the cliff," I said. "I laid her in the grass. I wrapped her in my coat. And I looked up and saw your window, and you in it, a white shape against the dark."

"I remember."

"I have wondered for twelve years what you saw."

"A man with blood on his hands," said Eleanor, "carrying my mother."

"Yes."

"I thought it was you who had killed her."

I closed my eyes.

"I know," I said.

---

I did not weep. I want that understood. I am not a man who weeps. But my chest ached as though I had swallowed a stone, and for a moment the room swam, and I heard the clock on the mantel and the beat of my own pulse and a sound from very far away that might have been the sea.

When I opened my eyes, she was standing before me.

She laid her hand on my cheek.

"Adrian."

"Don't."

"You were twenty-two."

"I was a coward."

"You were a boy." Her thumb moved. "You carried her. You did not run. You stayed with her in the dark."

"I kept silent for twelve years."

"Yes. You did." She drew her hand away. "And that is what I cannot forgive."

I looked up.

"I could forgive the other," she said. "A boy, frightened, in a storm. I think I could forgive almost anything of that boy. But the man, Adrian. The man who sat in my father's dining room and ate my mother's blue china. The man who told me I'd come to the wrong island."

"I was protecting you."

"From what?"

"From *this.*"

"I don't want to be protected," said Eleanor. "I want to be told."

I stood. I could not remain in the chair. I crossed to the window and stood with my back to her, looking down the garden to the cliffs.

"You do not understand what I have given up."

"Tell me."

"I have paid your father's debts. Forty thousand pounds. The estate was mortgaged to the last acre; the bank was on the point of foreclosing; I bought the notes at a discount and held them in my own name so that no one would know. I had the roof of Vale House mended. I paid Mrs. Crowe's wages out of my own purse when the trust ran dry. I have kept your father's name clean on this island for eight years, and the Assembly Room would have burned him in effigy if I had spoken a word."

"I did not ask—"

"No. You did not. That is the point." I turned. "I did it because I love you. Because I watched your mother die and I could not bear that you should lose the roof over your head as well. I have spent my fortune, and my father's, on keeping this family from the gallows. And I would do it again."

Silence.

"I am not asking for gratitude," I said. "I am asking you to understand that every lie I told was a lie for you."

She looked at me for a long time.

"I believe that," she said.

My heart leapt.

"But it doesn't make them true."

---

She did not ask me to leave. She did not ask me to stay. She stood by the window seat with her arms at her sides, a small straight figure, and I saw in her face something I had never seen before. It took me a moment to name it.

It was pity.

God help me, it was pity.

"There is one more thing," I said. I had not meant to say it. It came up out of me like water through a crack. "You've been to the lighthouse."

"Yes."

"You know what Thorne is."

"I know exactly what he is."

"Do you?" I drew a breath. "Then ask him what he did on the gallery. Not what he saw. What he *did.* Ask him what he did with his hands."

Her face did not change.

"I saw it, Eleanor. I was on the stair. I saw the whole of it, and I have never told a living soul. If you want the truth, ask him. Make him say it. And then ask yourself why a man who held your mother's wrist for nine seconds in a storm would keep a letter in his coat for twelve years."

"Adrian."

"Ask him."

I took my hat.

I walked out of the morning room, down the hall, past my own portrait's rival, my father's heavy painted eyes, and out the front door into the afternoon light. The air tasted of salt and cut grass. A blackbird sang in the orchard.

I stood on the gravel for a moment.

Then I put my hand over my mouth, and for the first time in twelve years, I wept.
