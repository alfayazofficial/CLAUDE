# Chapter Twenty-One
## The Truth Between Us

*Gabriel*

We carried Samuel out of the crypt at the first gray of dawn.

He had refused, at first. He had stood swaying against a pillar, gripping the ledger to his chest, insisting that he would walk. Then his knees had given way, and I had caught him, and for the first time in six years I had held my brother's weight in my arms and felt how little of it there was. He weighed no more than a boy.

We laid him on the cot in the lighthouse cottage. I built up the fire. I put a poultice to his chest, and brewed willowbark in a pan, and watched his breathing for an hour. When at last he slept, fitfully, with his mouth open and his fingers curled on the blanket, I stood over him a long time.

"He'll live," I said.

"Will he?"

Eleanor stood in the doorway. She had taken off her wet oilskin and wrapped herself in my gray blanket. Her hair hung in a rope over her shoulder. Her face was smudged with crypt-dirt, and her eyes were huge and tired and soft.

"I think so. If he rests. If the fever breaks." I turned. "He's strong. He's stronger than I am."

"Is he?"

"He survived."

"So did you."

"I let go."

"Gabriel."

"I need to say it." I did not look at her. I looked at my hands. "I told you in the crypt, because he demanded it. But I have not *said* it. Not to you. Not as I mean to."

She came into the room. She sat down on the edge of the table, near the fire, and drew the blanket over her knees.

"Then say it."

---

I sat opposite her on the stool.

I am not a man who speaks well. I have tried, over the years, to put the thing in words, and every time it has come out wrong, too soft, too hard, a shape of self-pity or self-defense. So I took a breath and told it as it was, flatly, in order, the way a surgeon records a case.

"Your mother came up the stair at about eleven. I was behind her by two minutes. I heard Ashcombe's voice before I saw them. He was shouting over the wind. She was shouting back.

"I came up through the hatch. It was black. The lamp was out. There was rain driving across the gallery like needles. Lord Ashcombe had hold of your mother's arm. He was dragging her toward the rail, and she was fighting him, and she had the lantern key in her hand; she'd taken it off the hook. She said, *I will light it. You will not stop me.*

"He said, *Margaret, be reasonable.*

"She said, *Fourteen men.*

"He hit her.

"It was an open hand across the face, the sort a man gives a servant. She staggered. The rail was rotten. I had known it for weeks; there was a section by the northeast corner that the Board had marked for replacing. She went against it. It gave."

I stopped.

Eleanor made no sound.

"I got there. I don't remember crossing the gallery. I was just there, on my stomach, with my arm over the edge. And I had her. I had her left wrist. It was the one I'd stitched. I felt the sutures under my thumb."

"Gabriel—"

"She looked up at me." My voice was a thread. "She was hanging by one arm over a hundred feet of air, with the sea breaking on the Teeth under her. And her face was *calm.* That's what I cannot forget. She was perfectly calm. She looked at me as though I were a boy who had dropped his books.

"And Ashcombe leaned over my shoulder and said, *Let go, boy. Or your brother hangs.*

"And I thought of Sam."

I stopped again. The fire cracked.

"I thought of him in a cell. I thought of the rope. I thought: *I can hold her. I'm strong enough. I can pull her up.* And I thought: *And then he will kill us both, and Sam will hang anyway.*

"And your mother said—"

I could not.

"What did she say?"

"She said, *Gabriel. Let go.*"

Silence.

"Just that. *Let go.* In a perfectly ordinary voice. And then she smiled."

I heard her breathe in.

"And I opened my hand."

---

I had expected her to weep. I had expected a hundred things: anger, horror, the cold withdrawal I had imagined for twelve years in the long white hours before dawn. But Eleanor did not weep. She sat very still, with her eyes on my face, and when I finished she was silent for a long time.

"Thank you for telling me," she said.

"That's all?"

"What else would you have me say?"

"That I'm a murderer."

"You are not a murderer."

"I killed her."

"Ashcombe killed her." Her voice was steady. "He struck her and dragged her to a rotten rail. You could not have held her. You said it yourself. You'd have been dragged over, or he'd have cut your hand off. She knew it. She knew what was coming. She *chose.*"

"She was told to by me."

"She was told to by *nobody.* She had written it that afternoon." She leaned forward. "Gabriel. She wrote *Do not.* She wrote it before the fact. She said she would not allow you to be the man who dies on a gallery for a woman he barely knew."

"I knew her."

"Then she didn't."

I stared.

"She thought of you as a boy," said Eleanor. "She thought she was protecting you. And you, you thought you'd failed her. And you've been two people on either side of a wall for twelve years, each one convinced the other was lost." Her eyes were bright with tears she did not shed. "Don't you see? It was *mercy.* Hers. And you've been treating it as a sin."

"I took the money."

"Yes."

"I took it, and I left."

"Yes. You did. That's the part I find hard." She did not look away. "You ran, Gabriel. You were twenty and you ran. I understand it. I ran, too. I went to London at eighteen and I didn't come home for twelve years. We're two cowards in a boat, you and I. But a man who builds a tombstone for his brother and keeps a lamp for six years is not a coward. He's only someone who's been waiting for permission."

"Permission?"

"To stop."

I put my face in my hands.

She slid down from the table. I heard the soft rustle of the blanket. She knelt before me and drew my hands from my face, gently, one at a time, and held them in her own.

"Look at me."

I looked.

"I forgive you," Eleanor said.

I could not breathe.

"I do not think there is anything to forgive. But if there is, if you need the words, then I forgive it. All of it. The nine seconds, and the money, and the six years." She pressed my hands. "And I love you."

The world stopped.

It did not go quiet. It went *still,* the way a held note goes still just before it breaks. I heard the fire. I heard Samuel's slow breath. I heard the sea, and the lamp turning in its cage overhead, and my own heart, doing something unexpected and enormous in my chest.

"Say it again," I whispered.

"I love you."

"Eleanor."

"Don't tell me what it means. Don't say it's impossible. I know." Her mouth trembled. "I know what I'm promised to. I know what it will cost. I only wanted you to hear it, once, plainly. In a room. Before the rest."

I took her face in my hands.

I did not kiss her. I only looked, for a long time, the way a man looks at the shore after years at sea.

"I love you," I said.

"I know."

"I loved you from the first morning. From the moment you stood in my doorway in that gray coat and said *you wrote to me.* I have loved you every day since. I will love you after I am dead."

"Don't say that."

"It's true."

"Then say it later." She smiled through her tears. "When there's time."

---

We sat by the fire until the light came up.

She told me about Adrian. About the confession, and the pistol he'd cleaned, and the look on his face when he left. She told me about the plan. Samuel had outlined it, between fits of coughing, in the dark of the crypt. A boat, the Reverend's, a stout little cutter called the *Faith.* A crew of two: Blackwood himself, who could handle a sheet, and Trevennick's son. A course for the mainland, a landing at Mousehole, a cart to Bodmin, where the winter Assizes would sit.

The ledger. The diary pages. The confession. The witness.

"Reed," I said.

"If we can free him."

"And the sea?"

"It's the seventh," Eleanor said. "The day after tomorrow. At dawn, on the flood."

"The glass is falling."

She looked up.

I had been watching it for three days. The great brass barometer on my cottage wall had been creeping down, tenth by tenth, since the evening of the spring tide. A bad sign, in a month like this. A *very* bad sign.

"How bad?"

"I don't know yet. A blow. Maybe worse."

"Can the boat sail?"

"If we leave early enough." I swallowed. "If the light holds."

"The light?"

"There's only one way through the Teeth at night, at that tide," I said. "A narrow channel, three cables wide, lined with rocks. A boat can only find it by the beam. If the lamp should fail—"

I did not finish.

She understood.

"Then you'll keep it burning," Eleanor said.

"Yes."

"No matter what."

"No matter what."

She took my hand and laid it against her cheek. It was warm and damp. The dawn was coming through the window in a long gold bar, and it fell across her face and made a halo of the loose hair at her temple.

"Gabriel."

"Mm."

"When this is over."

"Yes?"

"What shall we do?"

I thought about it.

I thought about a house with a garden. I thought about a piano in a room with open windows, and a woman's hands moving on the keys, and the sea in the distance. I thought about a child. About a morning with nothing in it but light.

It was a good dream.

I let myself have it for exactly as long as it takes a wave to reach the shore.

"Live," I said.

She smiled.

"I'd like that."

Outside, the barometer on the wall gave a small, dry tick. The needle dropped another tenth. And far out to the west, beyond the Teeth, beyond the last pale line of the sea, a bank of copper cloud began to climb the sky.
