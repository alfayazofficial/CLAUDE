# Chapter Seven
## A Photograph in the Rain

*Eleanor*

The dinner at Marlowe House was exactly as dull as I had feared, and exactly as dangerous.

Adrian had opened the good wine. He had lit every candle in the dining room. He had even, with a lover's absurd precision, ordered the table laid with my mother's favorite blue china, which he had somehow acquired from the sale of the Vale effects. I sat there in my black silk gown and ate his chicken and let him tell me about London, and I watched his hands.

They were steady, elegant, beautifully kept. They were the hands of a man who had never been afraid.

*I saw those hands covered in blood.*

"You are very quiet, my dear."

"I'm sorry. I was thinking of the abbey."

"The abbey?"

"I thought I might see it. Before the wedding." I sipped the wine. "I never went inside as a girl. My mother wouldn't let me."

His glass paused an inch from his lips.

"There is nothing to see," Adrian said. "It's a ruin. The roof fell in during the great storm of '74. The Reverend holds services in the parish church now."

"Mr. Blackwood keeps the records there?"

"Who told you that?"

"No one. I assumed."

"You assume a good deal." He smiled, and it was a good smile, a smile that had won over bishops. "I'll take you up myself in the spring, if you like. It's too dangerous to wander alone."

"Of course."

"Promise me you won't go on your own."

"I promise," I said, and added the lie to the pile.

---

It rained for two days.

Not the soft gray rain of the mainland, but the hard salt kind, driven sideways off the Atlantic, that stripped the last leaves from the orchard and turned the cliff path to a river of red mud. I waited until Thursday afternoon, until Mrs. Crowe had gone down to the village, and I put on my oldest boots and my mother's oilskin and went out the kitchen door like a thief.

I told myself I was going to read the diary.

I had been reading it in stolen quarters of an hour for three nights, in the window seat, by a stub of candle, and it had broken my heart in small clean pieces. Not for the grand reasons I'd expected. For the small ones. *Eleanor has a cough again. The ham was dry. J. has gone to Truro and I confess I am glad.* She had written the way women write who know no one is listening, with an honesty so plain that I felt I'd been given a key to a locked room.

But there was a hole in it.

Four pages, torn out. The entries between 3 June and 14 August of that last year. And after the gap, the tone had changed. The shopping lists were gone. The little domestic jokes. In their place, a hard dry vigilance, like a woman on a ship in fog.

*I have told no one what I saw in the crypt. Not Eleanor. Not Hester. Certainly not J.*

*I have found a friend. I did not expect it. He is young and he is frightened, and he is the only person on this island who has looked at me in a month and seen a woman instead of a wife.*

Who?

The diary didn't say. Only *he*, and *the boy,* and once, *our G.*

I stood at the top of the cliff path in the rain, and my boots sank to the ankle, and I said the letter aloud. *G.*

Then I walked on.

---

The cottage door was closed.

I knocked, and no one answered, and I knocked again. The rain came harder. The sky above the lantern had gone the color of slate, and the beam, which should not have been lit before four, was already turning, a slow white arm in the murk.

I pushed the door open.

"Gabriel?"

The room was empty. The stove was lit. A kettle steamed gently on the hob and the table was laid for one: a mug, a heel of bread, an open book face down beside them. I stood dripping on the stone floor and called again, and heard only the hiss of the fire and the roar of the rain on the slates.

He must be at the top of the tower.

I should have waited. I know that. A woman of sense would have sat in the chair by the stove and let him come down. But I had walked a mile and a quarter through a storm to hear something true, and the room was warm and silent, and on the table beside the open book lay a second book, thick and black with a cracked spine.

I picked it up.

I told myself I was only moving it to dry my gloves.

It was a medical textbook. *Gray's Anatomy, 1877.* The paper was soft with handling, and a pressed flower marked a page in the middle, a sea-pink gone brown. I turned the cover over, and a photograph slid out into my hand.

---

It was a small thing. Three inches by four, on stiff cardboard, brown with age. A carte de visite, the kind that were all the rage in the seventies, with a photographer's stamp on the back: *Fenwick & Son, Penzance.*

Three people stood in front of a painted backdrop of a balustrade and a cloudy sky. A stout man in a black coat, with a grave tired face: Dr. Silas Reed, who had treated me for scarlet fever when I was six. A boy of nineteen or twenty with dark hair and gray eyes, standing a half step too far from the others, as though he were not sure he was allowed.

And between them, in a pale gown, with a pearl necklace at her throat and her hand resting lightly on the boy's sleeve, my mother.

She was laughing.

She was laughing as I had never seen her laugh in life. Her head tipped, her eyes narrowed with it. The photographer had caught her mid-breath. She looked about twenty-five years old, and happy, and unguarded, and she had her hand on Gabriel Thorne's arm like a woman touching someone she loved.

I read the date on the back.

*Aug. 1879.*

Six weeks before she died.

The photograph did not fall. I do not know why I thought it might. I stood with it in my hand, and the world went very quiet and far away, and I thought, in a clear cold voice that did not seem my own: *He knew her.*

*He knew her. He lied to me. He sat on that gallery with the sea at his back and said "I am sorry for your loss" and let me weep on his sleeve, and he knew her.*

"Eleanor."

I turned.

---

He stood in the doorway with the rain running off his oilskin and an oil can in his hand. His hair was plastered to his forehead. His face was white.

He looked at the photograph.

He looked at me.

"You went through my things," he said.

"Yes."

"You had no right."

"No." I held up the card. "Neither did you. Tell me this isn't what it looks like."

"It's exactly what it looks like."

"Then you *lied.*"

"I never said I didn't know her."

"You said you had never been on this island before six years ago!"

"I said I came as keeper six years ago. Mrs. Crowe told you the rest."

"Don't." My voice broke. "Don't use that voice on me. Don't do the small true pieces. I asked you on the first day, I *asked* you, and you sat across from me and drank your tea and let me think—"

"Eleanor—"

"Who were you to her?"

He set the oil can down on the table. He did it with great care, as though it might go off.

"I was her friend," he said.

"A *friend.* She is touching you."

"She was kind to me."

"Is that all?"

"What do you want me to say?"

"That it wasn't what I think!"

"What do you think?"

I could not answer. Even to myself, it was only a shape. A chill, a shadow, a drowned thing turning over in the deep. I had seen the way she touched his sleeve, and I had read the word *friend* in her diary, and I had wanted, with a vehemence that frightened me, for it to be a lie. A lie I could hate him for. A clean lie, with edges.

"Tell me," I said. "Tell me now, or I will walk out into that rain and not come back."

He took a breath. I saw the muscle jump in his jaw.

"I cannot," he said.

"Cannot?"

"Not today. Not this way. Not with you standing there looking at me like that." His voice cracked. "I promise you, I will tell you every word. I've told no one. I've carried it so long I don't know how to set it down. But you'll hear it from me, and in order, and I will not have you hear it in anger."

"That is not good enough."

"It is the only thing I have."

"Then it is nothing."

The words were cruel. I knew it as I said them. I watched them land, and watched his face go quiet and shut, like a house in which every light has been put out at once.

I pushed past him, into the storm.

He did not follow.

---

I got as far as the gorse.

The rain was blinding. My mother's oilskin hung off my shoulders like a sack of wet sand. The path was a ditch of red water, and I slipped, and caught myself, and slipped again, and found that I was weeping, which I had not planned.

"Damn him," I said aloud. "Damn him, damn him."

Behind me, I heard the cottage door.

Not footsteps. Only the door, opening, and then, over the wind, a voice that carried across the headland in a cracked thin shout:

"Eleanor! The path is breaking! Stay on the left!"

I stood still.

"The left! The cliff's undercut past the second stile!"

I looked at the ground. The edge, three feet to my right, was not an edge at all but a lip of crumbling turf with nothing under it. In the dim light I had been walking along it for a hundred yards.

I moved left.

When I looked back, he had gone in. The door was shut. The lantern burned above the cottage like a white star, steady and watchful, as it had burned for sixty years.

I walked home with the photograph still clenched in my fist, and I did not realize until I reached the kitchen door that I had taken it.

I stood on the flagstones, dripping, and looked at my mother laughing.

*Tell me you didn't love him,* I thought.

*Tell me what he was to you.*

*Tell me what he is to me.*
