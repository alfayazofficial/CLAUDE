# Chapter One
## The House That Remembered

*Eleanor*

The first thing I noticed about Vale House was that it smelled exactly as I remembered.

Salt. Dust. Old roses.

And something faintly metallic beneath it all, like blood hidden beneath floorboards.

The carriage stopped before the iron gates at half past four. Rain slipped down the windows in long, trembling lines, blurring the house into a shape that seemed less built than grown from the earth itself.

I had imagined this moment a thousand times during the twelve years I spent away.

In some versions, I stepped from the carriage and felt nothing.

In others, I wept.

In the worst of them, I saw my mother standing in the upstairs window, waiting for me.

I saw no one.

Only the curtains moving in a room that should have been empty.

"Miss Vale?"

The coachman opened the door.

I gathered my skirts and stepped into the rain.

The house stood three stories high, its gray stone walls darkened by weather. Ivy climbed the eastern wing. The windows reflected the dying afternoon light like blind eyes.

I had inherited the house, the estate, and a fortune I had never wanted.

I had also inherited its ghosts.

"Welcome home, miss."

Mrs. Crowe stood beneath the entrance arch, her hands folded over her black dress. She had grown smaller since I last saw her, though her eyes remained sharp enough to cut.

"Mrs. Crowe."

She took my gloved hand.

For a moment, neither of us spoke.

Then she said, "You have your mother's eyes."

I looked away.

"Everyone says that."

"Your mother used to hate hearing it."

"Why?"

Mrs. Crowe's expression changed.

Only slightly.

Enough.

"She never believed herself beautiful."

I had no answer.

The house swallowed me as I crossed the threshold.

The entrance hall was unchanged. The marble staircase curved upward beneath the enormous chandelier. My father's portrait hung above the fireplace, his painted eyes fixed upon the door as though he had been waiting for me to return and disappoint him.

Beside it hung my mother's portrait.

She wore a white gown and a string of pearls. Her dark hair was pinned loosely at the nape of her neck. The painter had captured her smile, but not her sadness.

I stopped beneath the portrait.

"Has anything been moved?" I asked.

"Nothing of importance."

"That wasn't what I asked."

Mrs. Crowe lowered her eyes.

I looked at her.

"Has anyone entered my mother's rooms?"

"No, miss."

"Not even to clean?"

"The rooms have been kept as she left them."

I should have felt grateful.

Instead, I felt afraid.

Because somewhere in the back of my mind, beneath twelve years of distance and carefully cultivated forgetting, I remembered a door closing.

A woman's voice.

And the sound of something heavy falling into the sea.

---

That evening, I dressed for dinner in a black silk gown.

Adrian Ashcombe arrived precisely at seven.

He kissed my hand and smiled as though the years between us had been nothing more than a brief inconvenience.

"Eleanor," he said. "You look beautiful."

"You look unchanged."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It depends on what you have been doing with your life."

He laughed.

Adrian had always possessed the sort of charm that made people forgive him before he had done anything wrong. His dark hair was perfectly arranged. His suit was immaculate. Even the rain seemed reluctant to touch him.

He brought me a bouquet of white roses.

I accepted them.

"Thank you."

"I thought they might remind you of happier times."

I glanced at the flowers.

My mother had hated white roses.

She said they reminded her of funerals.

"How thoughtful," I said.

Dinner passed beneath the watchful eyes of my father's portrait.

Adrian spoke of London, of Parliament, of the future of the estate. He spoke of our wedding as though it had already happened.

I listened.

I smiled.

And all the while, I watched his hands.

When I was twelve, I had seen those hands covered in blood.

Or perhaps I had dreamed it.

After dinner, Adrian walked me to the drawing room.

"There's something I wanted to discuss."

"Of course."

"Our engagement."

I felt my stomach tighten.

"We have been engaged for nearly a year," he continued. "I believe it is time we announced the date."

I looked toward the window.

Beyond the glass, the lighthouse beam moved across the sea.

"I have only just returned."

"Precisely. The island needs something to celebrate."

"And what do I need?"

His smile softened.

"You need someone who will never leave you."

For a moment, I almost believed him.

Then I remembered the letter beneath my bedroom door.

The handwriting.

The warning.

And the lighthouse going dark.

"I need time," I said.

Adrian's expression remained gentle.

But his fingers tightened around mine.

"Of course, darling."

He kissed my knuckles.

"Take all the time you need."

---

That night, I woke to the sound of footsteps outside my bedroom.

Slow.

Deliberate.

They stopped at my door.

I held my breath.

Then something slid beneath it.

A folded piece of paper.

I waited until the footsteps disappeared before I rose from bed.

The paper was damp.

Inside, written in unfamiliar handwriting, were six words.

*Your mother did not die alone.*

Beneath them was a single name.

*Gabriel Thorne.*
