# Chapter Eleven
## The Unforgiven

*Gabriel*

I knew she was coming before she crossed the stile.

Six years of solitude had given me the senses of an animal. I could tell the weight of a footstep, the rhythm of a walk, the particular rustle of wool against gorse. I was at the foot of the tower, oiling the door hinges, when the gray coat topped the rise, and I stood and watched her come down the path with her chin high and her hands knotted at her sides.

She did not smile.

"You have a letter," Eleanor said.

I set the oil can on the step.

"You have it in your breast, or you did," she went on, reaching me, "and you have had it for twelve years, and she told you not to let it out of your sight. She said to give it to me on the day I was a woman grown. I am thirty, Gabriel. I have been a woman grown for a very long time."

I said, "Who told you?"

"She did."

I closed my eyes.

"She kept a diary. You must have known. She wrote everything."

"She wrote it for no one."

"She wrote it for me." Her voice trembled. "Whether she knew it or not."

---

I took her into the cottage.

It was a gray morning, with a wind that slapped the shutters, and I built up the fire because my hands needed something to do. She sat at the table with her coat on. She did not take off her gloves. I knelt on the hearth and fed the flames one stick at a time, and felt her watching my back, and when I could not put it off any longer I stood and turned.

"I have the letter," I said. "It's in a place I can't reach just now."

"You can't reach it?"

"I can reach it. I can't open it. There's a difference."

"That's an excuse."

"It is a condition," I said. "She made me swear on my life that I would not give it to you until it was safe. Not safe for me. For you. She knew what was in it. I don't."

"And you think it is not safe."

"I think there are people who would kill to have it. And that one of them is in this parish."

She was silent a long time.

"Tell me what you can," she said finally. "I'll take the rest when it comes."

---

So I told her the first half of the night.

I told it as plainly as I could, because the truth does not need ornament and because I had told it so many times to the ceiling that I knew every beat.

"I had been at Dr. Reed's all evening, keeping the fire in, because the storm was already building and he expected calls. At nine, Mrs. Crowe came to the door. She was soaking and white as a plate. She said, *Mrs. Vale has hurt herself and won't have the doctor. She asked for the boy.*

"I went.

"She was in the kitchen of Vale House, sitting at the big table with her hands in a basin. They were cut, Eleanor. Both palms, across the heel, and the fingers torn to the bone. As though she'd been clawing at a door."

Eleanor's breath caught.

"I asked what she'd done. She said, *I forced the crypt.* I didn't understand. She said, *They've sealed it. I went down with a crowbar to bring up the ledger, and the door was bolted from the outside, and I—* She stopped. She was shaking, not from cold.

"I cleaned the wounds. I stitched what I could. Eleven stitches in the left hand, nine in the right. I used silk from my own kit. She never made a sound. She only talked."

"What did she say?"

"That she'd been betrayed. That someone had told Lord Ashcombe she had the ledger. That she'd seen lights on the headland and knew a ship was coming and knew what they meant to do." My throat was raw. "She said, *There's a brig out there with fourteen men aboard, Gabriel. I can hear her bell.*"

Eleanor's gloved hand went to her mouth.

"I begged her not to go. I told her I'd go myself. She said, *No. They won't harm you if they think you're a boy. They'll harm me, but they cannot let it be known. They would not dare.*" I breathed. "She was wrong."

"Who betrayed her?"

I did not answer. I had not told her that part of the diary, and I would not tell her now. I had no right.

"She put on her cloak," I said. "She gave me nothing, said nothing; she only touched my face. Like this." I lifted my hand to my own cheek. "And she went out the kitchen door. And I stood there for a count of ten like a fool, and then I ran after her."

"You followed her."

"I followed her to the lighthouse."

"And?"

I looked at the fire.

"And by the time I reached the base of the tower," I said, "the light was already dark. I climbed. I took the stair three steps at a time. I heard voices on the gallery. I came up through the hatch and there was a man standing at the rail, looking down. A big man with a lantern. He turned, and I saw his face."

"Lord Ashcombe."

"Yes."

"Where was my mother?"

"The rail. The rail was broken."

The fire hissed. A log shifted. Somewhere above us, in the gallery, a loose shutter beat against its frame like a heart.

"He told me she had fallen," I said. "He said it was the storm. That she must have lost her footing. He said there was nothing to be done." I lifted my eyes. "And I did what any boy would have done. I went to the rail and looked."

"You saw her."

"I saw the water."

"But did you see her?"

"I—"

I stopped.

It was the first time in twelve years that anyone had asked me a straight question about that moment. And I discovered, in the pause, that I could not answer it.

Eleanor leaned forward.

Her voice was very quiet, very gentle, and it went through me like a probe through a wound.

"What aren't you telling me?"

---

I should say that I could have told her.

I should say it so that you understand. There was a moment, in that small hot room, in the silence after her question, when I could have opened my mouth and let it all out. *I had her wrist. I held it. I held it for nine seconds. Ashcombe said a name, and I loosened my hand.*

I could have said it.

I would have lost her.

Not "lost." That implies I'd had her. I had had nothing. Only a hand on a gallery, and a glance across a table, and the memory of a woman who wanted to be left in peace.

But I had had her *regard.* I had had her anger and her trust and her gloved fingers on my sleeve, and I had known with a miserable clarity that if I told her now, in this kitchen, with her mother's pearls in a velvet bag in her pocket, she would stand up from that chair and walk out into the wind and never speak to me again.

I am a coward.

It is the simplest word, and the only accurate one.

"I did not see her," I said. "I looked, and I couldn't. And then Ashcombe took my arm, and he said, *You'll say nothing, boy, or your brother hangs.* And I ran."

She was silent.

"I should have fought him. I should have gone down the rocks. I should have—" I could not finish. "I did not do any of it. I went back to Reed's. I sat at his table with my coat soaked through and I said nothing. And in the morning they found her body on the Teeth, and Reed signed the certificate. *Accidental drowning.* And I held my tongue, and was paid for it."

She went rigid.

"Paid."

"A letter came from London. A bursary. For a medical college. It was signed by a solicitor, but I knew whose money." I made myself look at her. "I took it, Eleanor. I took Ashcombe's money for my silence, and I went to sea."

Silence.

It was longer than any I have known. The fire popped. The kettle began to sing.

"Thank you," Eleanor said at last.

"For what?"

"For telling me that. It must have cost you a great deal."

"It cost me twelve years."

"Yes." She rose from the chair, slowly. She stood with her hands pressed flat to the table. "I don't forgive you. I want to be honest. I don't think I can, today. You had a hand on a gallery rail and a woman in the water and you took money. And I loved her, and she loved you, and I do not know what that means."

"I know."

"But you've given me the first true piece. And I will give you the same in return."

She went to the door. She turned.

"My mother's letter to the Reverend. The one in the study. It's unopened. I'd like you to read it with me."

"Why?"

"Because you are the only person alive who knew her as I did," said Eleanor, "and I can't bear to read it alone."

I stood very still.

Outside, the wind was rising, and the sea had begun to pile up gray on the Teeth, and the first spots of rain struck the window.

"Come tonight," she said. "After supper. Through the garden door. Mrs. Crowe will let you in."

She looked at me one last time, steadily, with those grave dark eyes.

"I forgive nothing yet. But I know the difference between a coward and a liar. You are only the first."

She went out.

I stood in the cottage and listened to her footsteps die away, and I thought: *I have told her half. Only half. And the half I kept is the half that matters.*

And I thought, with a bleak, rising certainty:

*She will find out. And when she does, it will not be me she hates. It will be herself, for having believed.*
