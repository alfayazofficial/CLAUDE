# Chapter Four
## The First Letter

*Eleanor*

"It was me," said Mrs. Crowe.

She said it to the window, not to me. We were in the morning room, and the autumn light lay across the carpet in a long pale bar. She had come in with the tray and not put it down. Her knuckles had gone white on the handles.

"The letter," I said.

"Yes."

"You put it beneath my door."

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because I made a promise." She set the tray on the little table at last and folded her hands in front of her. "She gave it to me the afternoon before. She said, *Hester, if anything happens to me, you are to keep this until the sea gives back what it took. Then you are to give it to Eleanor.*"

I stared at her. "Until the sea gives back what it took."

"That's what she said."

"Twelve years, Mrs. Crowe."

"Twelve years I have carried it in my apron, miss. In the pocket of every dress I owned." Her voice cracked on the last word and she mastered it, as she mastered everything. "I wasn't to give it until the sea gave something back. Not a body, not a boat. I did not know what. I thought I'd know it when I saw it."

"And on Tuesday you saw it."

"On Tuesday," she said, "you came home."

I sat down.

The room tilted gently, as rooms do in dreams. Outside, a gull cried and wheeled off over the garden wall. I thought: *She means me. I am the thing the sea has returned.*

"Did you write the second note?" I asked.

"What second note?"

I showed it to her. She read the six words. The color went out of her face so completely that for a moment I feared she would fall.

"No," she whispered. "No, miss. That is not mine. I would never—"

"Do you know the handwriting?"

"I do not." But her eyes had gone to the name at the bottom, and I watched her swallow. "You've been to see him."

"Yes."

"I told you not to."

"You did."

"And you went anyway, because you are your mother's daughter." She took a long breath. "Miss Eleanor. I will tell you what I can, and then I will tell you what I can't. And you must not press me on the second part. Not yet."

"That isn't fair."

"No. But it is all I have."

---

What she could tell me was this.

On the night my mother died, Mrs. Crowe had been sent to bed at nine. She had heard voices below: my mother's, my father's, and a third she would not name. At half past nine she had heard glass breaking. At ten, my mother had come up the back stairs with her hands bleeding and her face like paper, and had asked for a pen.

"She wrote for ten minutes," Mrs. Crowe said. "Then she folded it and gave it to me. She put on her cloak. She said, *Do not let Eleanor leave her room.* And she went out into the storm."

"And you did not stop her."

"I tried."

"You did not *stop* her."

"Miss," she said, very low. "Nobody ever stopped your mother from doing anything."

It was true. I closed my eyes.

"And who was the third voice?"

"That is the part I cannot say."

"Cannot, or will not?"

"Both."

"Was it Adrian's father?"

She flinched. It was the tiniest motion, a shiver of the shoulders, but I saw it. I had been watching people lie for twelve years in London drawing rooms, and Mrs. Crowe was a poor liar for a woman who had kept a secret so long.

"I'll say nothing against the dead," she said.

"Then say something about the living. Did Adrian know?"

She did not answer.

"Mrs. Crowe."

"I think," she said carefully, "that Mr. Ashcombe has always loved you very much."

"That isn't what I asked."

"It's what I'm able to say."

I stood. I went to the window and put my forehead against the cold glass. My own breath fogged it. Below me, the garden stepped down to the cliff in terraces of wet lavender and box, and past the wall the lighthouse rose from its headland, small and white and impossibly innocent in the morning.

"I am going to find out," I said. "All of it. Whatever it costs."

"I know, miss."

"Will you help me?"

"I will do what I promised your mother."

"Which was?"

"To keep you alive."

She went out and closed the door very softly behind her.

---

I played the piano that afternoon.

I had not meant to. I had passed the music room twice with my hand on the knob, and twice I had walked on. But the afternoon dragged, and the letters lay folded inside my corset like two hot coals, and the house pressed around me with all its held breath.

The instrument was a Broadwood grand, rosewood, with ivory keys yellowed to the color of old teeth. My mother had played on it every evening of my childhood. Chopin, mostly. Schubert when she was sad. Bach when she was angry, hard and precise, like a woman scrubbing a floor.

I sat down and put my hands on the keys.

They remembered.

It was the strangest thing. I had not touched this piano in twelve years. But my fingers found the opening of the Schubert impromptu as though no time had passed, and the room filled with it, that rippling, aching sound, a stream running under ice.

I played for an hour. I played until the light turned gold, and then amber, and then the color of old brandy. I played until I forgot to be afraid.

When I reached the end of the piece, I lifted my hands and let the last chord fade.

And in the silence, I heard the sea.

It was not the sea beyond the cliff. It was nearer. It seemed to come from inside the piano, a faint, wet, rushing sound, like a shell held to the ear.

I frowned. I bent and put my ear to the lid.

Nothing.

I struck the low A.

The note hummed. And beneath it, from somewhere under my feet, came an answering hollowness, a faint echo, as though the floor itself were an instrument.

I knelt and pressed my palm to the boards.

They were cold.

Colder than the rest of the room.

And when I rapped them with my knuckles, they gave back a sound that no solid floor should make.

---

The piano bench had a lid.

I had sat on it a thousand times and never lifted it. Inside lay sheet music, yellowed, familiar: Chopin, Schumann, the Bach preludes with my mother's pencil marks in the margins. I took them out in stacks and laid them on the carpet, and beneath the last of them, taped flat to the wood with a strip of linen gone brittle with age, I found a key.

Small. Black iron. A single notch cut in the bit like a bird's beak.

On the linen strip, in my mother's handwriting, were five words.

*For when you are ready.*

I held the key in the palm of my hand and felt it grow warm.

All my life I had thought of my mother as a woman who left things behind. Gloves. Bookmarks. A smell of orris and lamp oil in an empty room. I had never once thought of her as a woman who had *planned* it.

Someone knocked at the door of the music room.

I shut the bench so quickly it banged.

"Eleanor?"

Adrian.

He stood in the doorway with his hat in his hand, smiling, a little flushed from the wind. "Mrs. Crowe said you were playing. I did not like to interrupt."

"You didn't," I said.

"You've left your music on the floor."

"I was looking for something."

"Did you find it?"

"No," I said, and closed my fingers around the key.

He crossed the room and bent and gathered the sheets, one by one, and set them in order on the piano. He did it carefully. He did it the way one might touch a dead thing, with great tenderness and no hope of waking it.

"She played beautifully," he said.

"Yes."

"You play better."

"That is not true."

"I heard you from the garden, Eleanor." He did not look up. "I stood there for twenty minutes. I could not make myself come in."

I was silent.

"Come to dinner tomorrow," he said at last. "Marlowe House. I will have the good wine opened. We shall talk about nothing."

"I would like that."

"Would you?"

I made myself smile. It cost something.

"Yes."

He looked at me, then, steadily, and for the briefest instant I had the terrible feeling that he knew about the key. That he could see it through the glove of my fist. That he had always known exactly where I was.

Then he lifted my other hand to his lips and went away.

I waited until the front door shut. Then I walked, very quietly, to the entrance hall.

Beneath the great staircase, where the paneling met the floor, there was a door I had never in my life seen opened. A shallow door, painted the same dull green as the wall. A door for brooms. A door you stopped seeing.

I knelt before it.

The lock was small. The notch in the bit was like a bird's beak.

And the key turned as if it had been waiting twelve years to be asked.
