# Chapter Nineteen
## Beneath the Abbey

*Eleanor*

I told him in the dark, on the headland, in the wind.

I had meant to do it gently. I had rehearsed it in the carriage on the way back from the vestry, a speech of three careful sentences that began *Gabriel, I have something to tell you and you must sit down.* But when I reached the cottage the lamp was already turning above and the door stood open and he was kneeling on the hearth, trimming a wick, and he looked up and smiled at me with such joy that every sentence fled.

"Samuel is alive," I said.

The wick fell from his fingers.

He did not speak. He did not move. He knelt on the stone with the scissors in one hand and his face gone blank as paper, and the fire made a red flicker in his eyes.

"I saw his name," I said. "In the vestry. In the Reverend's accounts. He has been signing for bread. He wrote the note, Gabriel. He put out the lamp. He's in the abbey. He has been there six years."

"No."

"Mr. Blackwood told me—"

"No."

"He thinks you were bought. He thinks you betrayed him. He's been waiting—"

"*No.*"

It was not a shout. It was a sound I had never heard from a human throat, a long low ragged exhalation, the sound of a man struck in the breast with a pike. He rose. He stood with his hands open. Then he went past me and out of the door and across the grass, quite slowly, like a sleepwalker, and I followed.

He stopped at the edge of the headland.

Below us the sea was black and rising. The wind whipped his shirt and tore at his hair. He stood with his hands at his sides, and his shoulders shook, and I stood a yard behind him and did not touch him.

"Six years," he said.

"Yes."

"I put up a stone."

"I know."

"I carried flowers." His voice cracked and split. "I stood there in the dark and told him I was sorry."

"Gabriel—"

"I was *sorry!*" He turned. His face was wet and wild. "I gave up everything. I came to this place. I lived like a monk. I thought, if I could only find the man who did it, if I could only bring the truth to light, then he would not have died for nothing. And all the while he was in a *hole.*"

"He's ill."

"He is a fool!"

"He's *twenty-six,*" I said. "And he's frightened. And he thinks his brother sold him."

Gabriel's mouth worked.

"Where?" he whispered.

"The crypt."

"I'll go."

"Not alone. Not now. The tide's making." I took his sleeve. "Tomorrow, at the ebb. The spring low is at four in the morning. We'll go together. You and I and a lantern. We'll find the third niche, and the ledger, and we'll find him."

"I can't wait that long."

"You can."

"Eleanor—"

"You can," I said, "because if you go now, in this state, you will frighten him. He will bolt. He will go down the sea passage and we shall never find him." I held his sleeve tighter. "He has been hiding for six years. Do you think a man like that comes out for shouting?"

He was silent.

Then, slowly, as though every joint in his body ached, he bent his head and rested his forehead against my shoulder.

I put my arms around him.

We stood there, on the edge of the world, until the first stars came out, and the beam went round above us, and a long swell broke against the Teeth like a distant cannon.

---

We went down at ebb tide, a little after four.

The abbey stood a quarter mile north of the church, a roofless ruin of tawny stone, its nave open to the sky. The great east window gaped like a missing eye. In the moonlight, the broken arches seemed to float, and the grass between the pillars was silver and soft as a fleece. Owls cried in the dark.

"This way," Gabriel said.

He had a dark lantern in his left hand, shuttered to a slit. In his right was a crowbar from the tool store. He wore a rough jersey and a leather coat, and he moved with the unhurried alertness of a man who had once been a soldier of sorts.

I followed, in my mother's old oilskin, with the iron key in my fist.

We crossed the nave. We passed beneath the chancel arch. In the south transept stood a ruined vestry, its roof gone, its walls hung with ivy. In the corner a massive oak press leaned drunkenly against the stones, its doors hanging.

"Behind it," I said.

Gabriel shifted the press. It moved with a groan. Behind it, a low door of black wood, bound with iron, hung half open on a single hinge.

Cold air breathed out of it.

He held the lantern higher. A stair descended into the dark. Twenty-three steps. I counted. The walls were slick and green with weed, and the air smelled of brine and rot and something sharper, the cold clean smell of deep water.

At the bottom, the crypt opened out.

It was a vast cave, hewn from the rock, with a vaulted roof and a floor of wet black stone. Pillars stood in rows, rough-hewn, the carved tops long since broken. At the far end, perhaps fifty feet off, the cave simply stopped. There was no wall. There was only the sea, a wide dark mouth of it, rising and falling in a long slow breath, and the moon shone down across the water and laid a white road on the floor.

And on the walls, in the silver light, I saw the names.

---

I had read of them.

I had read my mother's account, three nights ago, in the quiet of my bed. But words are small things, and the crypt was vast, and for a long moment I could only stand with my hand at my throat and look.

They covered every surface. The walls, the pillars, the vaulting, the stairs. Thousands of tiny strokes. Tens of thousands. Name upon name upon name, scratched in the soft stone with nails, with knives, with fragments of iron. Some were deep and clean, the work of a patient hand; some wandered and wavered like writing in a dream. Beside each, a ship. A date. A trade.

*Tom Hocking, cook, Anne Marie.*

*Unknown boy, aged about 9, Constant.*

*Jean Le Goff, Breton, Marie-Josèphe.*

*Mary Teague, passenger, Aurora. With her child.*

I put out my hand. I laid my palm on the cold wet surface of the wall and felt the grooves under my fingers like the veins of a body.

"It's a ledger," I whispered. "It's the *other* one."

"Yes."

"They kept them. All of them."

"The island kept what it could." Gabriel's voice was rough. "Priests, mostly. Widows. The ones who survived the wreckers. They came down in secret and scratched the names, so the sea would remember."

"The tide," I said.

He looked at me.

"It comes in over them every night, doesn't it? It fills the whole place. It washes over every name." I turned slowly, taking in the vault. "And at the ebb it goes out again, and they're still there."

"The tide keeps our names," said Gabriel.

I closed my eyes.

When I opened them, I saw what I had missed.

At the very back of the cave, near the open sea, a smaller space was cut into the wall. A kind of bay, shoulder-high, with a flat stone ledge. On the ledge lay a bundle of blankets. A tin cup. A candle stub. A coat. A heap of orange peel. A small, neat row of pebbles arranged along the edge in the form of a ship.

A camp.

It was empty.

---

Gabriel went to it like a man in a dream.

He knelt by the blankets. He lifted the coat, a long dark coat of rough serge, patched at the elbow, much too thin for the season, and held it to his face. I saw his shoulders heave. He did not make a sound.

"It's still warm," he whispered.

"Then he's near."

"Samuel!"

His voice went out into the vault, huge and broken, and the echoes ran away down the arches. *Samuel. Samuel. Samuel.* The names on the walls gave it back. The sea sighed. A drop of water fell somewhere, *tink,* and was silent.

"Sam! It's Gabe!"

Nothing.

"I never took the money for you! I took it *after!* I didn't know about the *Halcyon!*" He was weeping openly now, on his knees on the stone. "Sam, I thought you were dead!"

A cough.

It came from the dark beyond the lantern light. Wet, tearing, hollow. The cough of a man with half a lung. And a voice, hoarse and cracked and young, that said:

"You were right."

Gabriel went rigid.

"I'm dead," said the voice. "I've been dead six years. Don't call me back."

"Sam—"

"Stay where you are."

"Sam, please. Let me see you."

"Don't." A click: the sound of a hammer drawn back on a pistol. "I will shoot the lady."

I stood perfectly still.

"I don't want to," said the voice. "I've come to like her. She's got her mother's way of standing. But I can't have you rushing me, Gabe. I've waited a long time for this, and I mean to do it right."

"Do what?"

"Tell me why you took his money."

A pause.

Then, very low, Gabriel said:

"Because I let her go."

My heart stopped.

I heard it. I heard each word as though it were being struck on a bell. *I let her go.* The simple words dropped into the vault and sank, and the names on the walls seemed to lean in to listen.

"Eleanor," Gabriel said. He did not turn. "I'm sorry. I meant to tell you properly."

I could not speak.

"I had her wrist," he said. "On the gallery. For nine seconds. Lord Ashcombe said, *Let go, boy, or your brother hangs.* And I—" His voice failed. "I let go."

The sea breathed in.

The sea breathed out.

And in the dark at the back of the crypt, a pale figure rose from the shadow, thin as a rake, with a pistol in his hand and a face like a drowned man's, and said:

"Hello, brother."
