# Chapter Twenty-Four
## The Burning Letter

*Adrian*

The storm broke over Marlowe House at six in the evening, and by seven I had made up my mind to ruin everything.

I did not know it then. I thought I was only restless. I paced the study with my hands behind me while the windows rattled in their frames and the chimney roared, and I told myself that I was a man about to be married. That in less than a day I would stand at the altar of St. Piran's with Eleanor Vale on my arm. That I had done, at last, the one thing that mattered. I had *won.*

I had won in the way that a man wins who has paid a thousand pounds for a dying horse.

She had come to me with her chin high and her eyes dry. She had said *I accept.* She had looked at me across my father's desk, as straight and cold as a blade, and I had known with a terrible clearness that she hated me.

Not hated. That would have been a kind of tribute. She had *looked through* me, the way one looks through a window at a view.

I had said to myself: *It will come. In time. When she sees what I have done. When she sees that I kept my word.*

But I knew, even then, what I had bought.

I had bought a woman's body in a church, and the loan of her name, and the sight of her face across a breakfast table for the rest of my short life. I had not bought one moment of that look she had given the keeper, the look I had seen through the glass on the cliff, a look like a lamp lit behind a face.

I would never see it turned on me.

---

At eight, Corbett came in, dripping, and told me the sea was running over the harbor wall.

"Is the cutter still at her moorings?"

"No, sir." He wiped his face. "She went out at dusk. The Reverend and young Trevennick took her round to the abbey cove."

"Without waiting for Saturday."

"They'll sail tonight, sir. On the flood. There's talk on the quay."

"In this?"

"They say the Thorne boy is aboard."

I stood very still.

I had given her my word. *I will not stop the boat.* I had said it in front of the candle, with the pistol on the blotter. And I had meant it. God knows I had meant it. I had wanted, with a violence that frightened me, to be a man whose word was good.

"They will drown," I said.

"Very likely, sir."

"The channel is three cables wide."

"Yes, sir."

"They'll have to pass the Teeth. In the dark. In a gale." I swallowed. "The only light is the lamp."

Corbett said nothing.

He stood with the rain running off his hat brim and his small pale eyes fixed on the middle distance, and I understood, with a slow cold lurch in the stomach, why he had come to tell me.

"No," I said.

"Sir?"

"Whatever you are about to suggest. No."

"I haven't suggested anything, sir."

"You have been standing in my father's study for forty years suggesting things," I said. "Get out."

He bowed. His face did not change.

"The boat's key is in your desk, sir," he said. "The second one. If you should have need."

He went out and closed the door.

I sat down.

I put my head in my hands.

Somewhere on the headland, in the dark, the lamp turned. I could not see it. I could feel it. A faint white pulse behind my eyelids, steady, relentless, like the beat of a heart.

---

I went to Vale House at nine.

I told myself I wished to see her. I told myself I wished to be certain that she was well, that the storm had not frightened her, that Mrs. Crowe had kept up the fires. I put on my oilskin and walked the half mile through a wind that nearly took the hat from my head, and when I reached the front door and rang, there was no answer.

I rang again.

I tried the latch. It gave.

The hall was dark. A single candle burned on the table at the foot of the stair. The portraits stared down from the walls, Josiah and Margaret, side by side, and the great chandelier swayed above me on its chain and threw long shadows across the marble.

"Eleanor?"

My voice rang. No one answered.

I went up.

The man I had posted on the landing sat slumped against the banister, his hat over his eyes. I touched his shoulder and he fell sideways, quite limp. A neat red mark stood out on his temple. He breathed, thickly, evenly.

Struck. Not killed.

I pushed open Eleanor's door.

The room was empty. The bed was made. The window stood wide open and the curtains streamed into the room like banners, soaked, flapping, dragging rain across the floorboards. A rope of knotted sheets hung from the sill.

For a moment I could not think.

Then, very clearly, I thought: *She never meant it.*

I crossed the room in three steps and looked out. The garden lay below, black and tossing, the lavender thrashing flat. The rope of sheets ended four feet short of the ground. At the bottom, in the mud, I could see a long smear where someone had dropped and rolled.

She had gone.

She had gone to him.

She had *always* been going to him.

I sat down on her bed.

The mattress sighed under me. On the pillow lay a little book bound in black cloth. I picked it up and turned it over, and my thumb found the worn place on the spine where a hand had held it. It fell open.

*This is the private diary of Margaret Vale.*

I read the first line.

I read the second.

Then I stopped, because the page was blurring, and I found that I was crying again, silently, shamefully, without any sound at all.

---

She had left it for me.

I do not mean that she had meant to. But she had put it on the pillow, in plain sight, where a man who knew her would find it; she had known I would come. And I knew what she was asking. She was not asking me to read it.

She was asking me to *understand.*

I turned the pages. I saw my father's name, written in a hand that was steady and unforgiving. *Lord A. has the key.* I saw my own, in an entry dated the summer I was twenty-two. *The Ashcombe boy was at the harbor this morning, helping a lame sailor with his nets. He has his mother's eyes and none of his father's. I wonder what the island will make of him.*

I turned a page.

*28th September. They have taken Eleanor's keys. She is locked in her room for her own safety. I am to go to the lighthouse.*

*Adrian was on the stair when I passed him. He was white. I touched his hand. I said, "Whatever you see tonight, you are not to blame for it." He didn't understand.*

*He will.*

*I hope he will forgive himself before I'm cold.*

I closed the book.

I sat there for a very long time with it in my lap.

Margaret Vale had known. She had stood on the cliff stair in the rain with a murderer waiting above her, and she had stopped to touch the hand of a frightened boy and absolve him.

*You are not to blame.*

For twelve years I had waited for someone to say it. For twelve years I had imagined, in the long hours of the night, the voice of the woman I had failed, telling me it was all right. And she *had* said it. She had said it before it happened. And I had carried her body up the cliff, and put it in the grass, and never once let myself hear.

I stood.

The room swayed. I put out a hand and caught the bedpost.

*Burn it.*

The thought came quite clearly, in my father's voice. *Burn it, boy. Burn it all. There is no proof but paper. Burn the paper and the girl is yours.*

I looked at the diary.

I looked at the fireplace, where Mrs. Crowe had laid a good fire against the cold, and where the coals still glowed in the grate.

I do not know what I would have done, if I had been left alone. I think, in the end, I would have put the book down. I think I would have walked out of that room and down the cliff and knelt in the mud and begged.

But the door opened behind me, and Corbett said:

"She's at the lighthouse, sir."

---

"How do you know?"

"The Penrose boy saw her on the cliff path. In her mother's oilskin. Running." He stood in the doorway with the lantern behind him, a vast dark shape. "She came out of the abbey. By the tunnel. The one beneath the stairs."

"I see."

"The boat sails at one."

"I know."

"The lamp must be dark by half past twelve, sir. If it's to be done." His voice was perfectly even. "I have three men below."

I looked at the book in my hand.

I looked at Corbett.

And I did what I had spent my whole life doing. I did what my father would have done. I did what a hundred Ashcombes had done before me, on a hundred nights like this one, with a key in their pockets and the dark behind them.

I walked to the fire.

I held the diary over the coals.

It was only a small black book. It weighed almost nothing. I felt the heat of the grate on my knuckles and thought: *This is the last thing she wrote. This is the last thing she was.*

*You are not to blame.*

I opened my hand.

It fell onto the coals with a soft sigh, and for a moment nothing happened. Then the cover began to curl. A tongue of blue flame ran along the spine. The pages blackened and lifted, one by one, and the writing showed for an instant in glowing gold, line after line, *Eleanor has a cough again, the ham was dry, J. has gone to Truro and I confess I am glad,* and then crumbled to ash.

I watched all of it.

When it was done, I turned to Corbett. My face was quite dry.

"Get the men," I said.

"Yes, sir."

"I'll go to the lighthouse myself."

"Sir?"

"If it is to be done," I said, "I will do it. I will not have it said that I sent another man to do my father's work."

He bowed.

I took my oilskin from the bed where I had dropped it. I took my father's pistol from the pocket of my coat, and checked the priming, and put it back.

At the door I paused.

The room was cold. The curtains still streamed in the wind. On the pillow lay a dent where her head had been, and beside it, scattered across the white linen, a few long dark hairs.

I bent, and gathered them, and put them in my breast pocket.

Then I went out into the storm.
