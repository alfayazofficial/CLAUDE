# Chapter Twenty
## The Last Page

*Eleanor*

Nobody moved.

The pistol hung in Samuel Thorne's hand, loosely now, its muzzle pointed at the floor. He stood at the edge of the lantern light, a gaunt scarecrow in a ragged shirt, and I saw that he was shaking. Not with fear. With cold, and fever, and a rage so old it had worn through to bone.

He was twenty-six. He looked forty.

His hair was long and matted. A white scar ran from the corner of his mouth to the hinge of his jaw, a seam as clean as a surgeon's. His eyes were Gabriel's eyes, gray and level, sunk in bruised hollows. And across the whole of his face, under the grime, lay the unmistakable architecture of the brother who had knelt weeping on the stone.

"Say it again," Samuel said.

Gabriel got to his feet.

He did it slowly, with his open hands held out from his sides, as a man approaches a horse that has been beaten. He took one step. Another.

"I let go of her wrist," he said. "I had her. I could have held on. Ashcombe said your name, and I—" He stopped. "I was twenty. I thought they'd hang you. I thought, if I do as he says, he'll let the boy go."

"He did let me go."

"Yes."

"With a flogging."

"I know."

"With a *flogging,* Gabe." Samuel's voice cracked. "Forty lashes. In the yard of the courthouse. I was fourteen. Do you know what it was for?"

"For the silver."

"For *stealing* it. From the wreck of the *Sparrowhawk.* The very ship." He gave a wild, ragged laugh. "I was down on the rocks that night with the other boys, picking the bodies, and I put a spoon in my pocket. A spoon. And Corbett saw. And Lord Ashcombe found a use for me."

I made a sound.

"He used me to buy you," Samuel said. "That's what you were never told. I was the bait. He didn't need to threaten me. He only needed you to *think* he would. And you were such a good brother that you took it."

Gabriel's face was ashen.

"I didn't know."

"No."

"I would never have—"

"You would," said Samuel, softly. "That's the worst of it. You'd do it again."

The silence was dreadful.

Then Gabriel said, very low, "Yes. I would."

I held my breath.

"God help me," he said, "I would. Not for her. For you. And I have hated myself for it for twelve years, and I will hate myself for the rest of my life. But I won't lie. You were fourteen years old and you were all I had."

Samuel stared at him.

The gun wavered.

"And the money?" he whispered. "The bursary?"

"I took it because I couldn't stand to be in that house another hour. Because Reed would not look at me. Because you were in a cell and I couldn't help you." His voice broke. "I took it so I could become something. So that someday I could stand in a court and say, *this is what I saw.* I was going to come back. I swore it."

"You took six years."

"I know."

"You took six *years,* Gabe. You were in the navy. You came home in your uniform and sat in your lighthouse and *waited.* While I—" Samuel coughed. It tore through him, long and wet and wrenching, and he doubled over, bracing one hand on the wall. Blood flecked his lips.

Gabriel was across the space in two strides.

He caught his brother as he fell.

---

I will not tell all that followed.

It was not mine to tell. I stood at the edge of the cave, with the sea breathing behind me, and watched two men who had spent six years believing each other dead cling together on a floor of wet stone. I saw the pistol go skittering into the shadow. I saw Gabriel's hand cup the back of his brother's head, large and tender. I heard the broken sounds they made, the half-words and the sobs, and the tide came slowly up the floor of the crypt and wet my boots, and I did not move.

When at last they drew apart, Samuel's face was streaked with tears and dirt. He was laughing.

"You look like a bloody gentleman," he said.

"You look like a corpse."

"I've been practicing."

Gabriel laughed too, a cracked, wondering, almost frightened sound. He wiped his face with the heel of his hand.

"Sam. You're burning up."

"It's nothing."

"It's a lung fever. You've had it for months."

"I've had worse."

"You'll come out of here."

"After."

"Sam—"

"*After,*" said Samuel, with a flash of the old stubbornness. "I haven't waited six years in a hole to be dragged out like a sick sheep. There's work." He turned his head. His gray eyes found me, and his expression changed. "Miss Vale."

"Mr. Thorne."

"I frightened you."

"Yes."

"I'm sorry. I thought—" He coughed again, shorter. "I thought you'd bring Ashcombe. That it was a trap. That he'd sent you."

"He didn't."

"I know that now."

"Then why the pistol?"

A faint, tired smile touched his mouth.

"Because I'm a coward too," said Samuel. "It's a family failing."

---

The third niche was behind a broken column near the seaward end of the vault.

Samuel showed us. He had known it for two years. He had found it by accident, he said, crawling about in the dark for a place to hide a loaf; he had put his hand into a crack and felt the edge of an iron box. He had not opened it. He did not know what it held. "It felt like something that wasn't mine," he said.

The stone was carved with a cross, upside down.

I knelt before it.

It took the two of them together to pry it loose. A slab, a foot square, set into the rock with lime, so old that it crumbled at the edges. Behind it, a cavity. And in the cavity, wrapped in a square of oilcloth gone green with age and bound with a hempen cord, a book.

It was larger than I expected. A folio, bound in tarred canvas, as thick as my wrist. The cord had rotted. When I lifted it, the cover fell open.

*A True Account of the Island's Share.*

I turned the pages, and my hands shook.

I will not set down what it said. I have told myself I would not; there are things that should be left for the Assize. But I will say that it was written in four hands, across a hundred and twelve years, and that the earliest entry was in a clerk's copperplate dated 1779, and that the last was in a heavy square script that I knew from the cheques on my father's desk. *Ashcombe. Per Sparrowhawk. £340. Vale's third: £113 6s. 8d.*

Each name had a column. Each column had a sum.

On the final leaf, pinned with a corroded pin, was a folded sheet of notepaper. Cream-colored. Margin ruled in blue.

My mother's stationery.

I knew it before I touched it. I knew it the way you know a footstep on a stair. I unfolded it with a hand that did not feel like mine, and the paper crackled, and my mother's handwriting rose off the page like a voice.

---

*28th September, 1879. Four in the afternoon.*

*I am in the crypt. The tide is out. I have hidden the ledger in the third niche, under the stone with the cross cut upside down, and I am writing this by the light of a candle, kneeling on the wet floor, because I will not be able to write it at home.*

*I must record what I heard this morning.*

*I was in the garden at nine. Josiah was in his study. The window was open. He had Lord Ashcombe with him, and the door was shut, and they spoke in low voices, and I stood beneath the sill among the lavender and heard every word.*

*Josiah said: "She has the ledger. She has found it. I've seen her go down."*

*Lord Ashcombe said: "Then it must be recovered."*

*"She'll not give it up."*

*"She will be made to."*

*A silence. I heard the clink of a glass.*

*Then Josiah said — and I set it down exactly — "Whatever is needed, Edward. But not Eleanor. Never Eleanor."*

*"Your wife, then."*

*"She's… yes." A pause. "Do it however you must. Only keep her from suffering."*

*I did not move. I could not. I stood among the lavender, with the bees droning and the sun hot on my back, and I heard the man I had married speak of my death as a question of arrangements.*

*It is, I find, a very peculiar sensation.*

*I thought I should feel rage. I did not. I felt only a kind of enormous, infinite tiredness, as though a great weight I had been carrying for twenty years had been lifted off and replaced by one infinitely heavier.*

*He did not say it with malice. That is the thing I cannot bear. He said it as one says, "Pass the salt." He said it as a man says, "I've decided against the horse."*

*I have been loved by a weak man, and that is a worse thing than being hated by a strong one.*

*I am writing this so that someone will know. Not for justice. I do not expect justice. For truth. Because it is a kind of crime to let a lie sit unnamed in the dark.*

*Eleanor. My darling. If you read this, you will know that your father was not a monster. He was only a man who could not say no. He did not wish me dead. He wished that things might be otherwise, and he lacked the courage to make them so. Forgive him if you can. If you cannot, forgive yourself for not being able to.*

*I am going to the lighthouse tonight.*

*I cannot stop what is coming. But I can light the lamp.*

*One ship. Fourteen men. If I am too late, then I shall have tried. And that, I find, is all I have ever wanted to be known for.*

*Margaret.*

*P.S. Gabriel. If you find this. You will have tried to hold me. I know you. I can see you doing it. Do not.*

*I will not allow you to be the man who dies on a gallery for a woman he barely knows.*

*Be kind to my daughter. And be kind to yourself.*

---

I read it to the end.

Then I read the postscript again.

The crypt was very quiet. The sea sighed against the stone. Somewhere in the vault a drop of water fell and fell, a slow soft clock. I knelt on the floor with the paper in my lap and my hands pressed flat against it, and I was aware of Gabriel standing a few feet away, rigid, not breathing.

"Gabriel."

He did not answer.

"She wrote this at four o'clock that afternoon. Before she knew you would be there."

"Eleanor—"

"She said *Do not.*"

"I know."

"She said she would not allow you to die for her."

"I heard."

"Then why—"

I stopped.

Because I could see his face. In the lantern light, it was a mask of agony. I understood suddenly, with a kind of cold clarity, what I should have understood weeks before.

It was not the rail he had been unable to forgive himself for. It was not even the nine seconds.

It was that she had *told* him not to hold on.

And he had known. In some part of himself, in the instant that Ashcombe said his brother's name, he had known that she wanted him to let go. That she was already lost. That to hold would have been to drown with her. And he had opened his hand, and she had fallen, and for twelve years he had asked himself *which was it?*

Which was the coward's reason, and which the kind one.

He had never been able to tell.

"Gabriel," I said, very softly.

He turned away.

I rose. I went to him. I laid my hand on the middle of his back, between the shoulder blades, and felt the great shuddering of his ribs, and he did not turn, and I said:

"I knew."

His head came up.

"I knew," I said. "Since yesterday. Adrian told me. He was on the stair. He saw you."

"And you came anyway."

"I came to the lighthouse."

"And—"

"And I did not ask."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted to know who you were," I said, "before I knew what you'd done."

He turned then. He stood looking down at me in the lantern light, his face naked, his eyes red-rimmed and enormous.

"And now?" he whispered.

"Now I know," I said. "And I'm still here."

For a long moment he did not move.

Then he made a sound that was neither laughter nor weeping, and put his arms around me, and held on as though the floor had dropped away beneath us and I was the only solid thing in the sea.

Behind us, from the edge of the water, Samuel Thorne coughed once, discreetly.

"If you two are quite done," he said hoarsely, "I'd like to propose a plan."
