# Chapter Twelve
## The First Kiss

*Eleanor*

He came at ten, through the garden door, with rain on his shoulders and no hat.

Mrs. Crowe let him in. She did not speak; she only looked at him with an expression I could not read, the look of a woman handing a child to a stranger over a river. Then she took her candle and went up the back stairs and left us in the dark kitchen, two people who had not decided what they were.

"This way," I said.

I led him through the hall. We went on tiptoe past my father's portrait, past my mother's, past the long shadow of the staircase. The green door was open a hand's breadth. I took his sleeve and drew him down the eleven steps, and I felt, through the wet wool, the tension in his arm, like a drawn bow.

At the bottom he stopped.

He stood in the doorway of my mother's study with the candle on the desk throwing his shadow up the wall, and he looked at the room, the chair with its gray shawl, the chart, the shelves of storm books, and I saw his face change.

"She sat here," he said.

"You've been in this room."

"Once." His voice was strange. "I carried her books down. In July. She asked me not to tell." He touched the back of the reading chair with two fingers, very lightly, and took them away. "I always thought she would have made a very fine physician."

"She would have hated it. The smell."

"Yes." He almost smiled. "She would have."

---

The letter lay where I had left it, in the desk drawer, sealed with red wax. The paper had yellowed. The ink of the address was still dark and clear.

*To the Reverend Thomas Blackwood. To be delivered by hand.*

I held it out.

He did not take it. "It isn't mine to open."

"It isn't mine either. He's the addressee."

"Then perhaps we ought to give it to him."

"We'll give it to him," I said. "After we know what's in it. I am done being the last to learn things in this house."

He looked at me. In the candlelight his eyes were the color of rain on slate. Then he nodded, once, and took the letter from my hand, and broke the seal with his thumb.

He read it first. I watched his lips move on the last line. Then he gave it to me.

*Thomas,*

*If you are reading this, I am dead, and you have the proof in your keeping. I know you will do what is right. I know it because you married me to a man I did not love and wept at the altar, and because you have never once pretended that the Church is anything but a weak and weathered thing.*

*I have put the ledger in the third niche of the seaward wall, behind the broken column, under the stone with the cross cut upside down. You will know it when you see it. Do not trust the vestry. Do not trust the key. Go at low tide and go alone.*

*The names are on the wall. I have added the Sparrowhawk's as they came to me, from the harbor books. There will be fourteen. By the time you read this there may be more.*

*I ask three things.*

*First, that you tell no one at Vale House. My husband is not a villain. He is only a weak man who has found a reason to be weak. But he has a fear in him that is stronger than love, and I will not be the cause of its turning on my child.*

*Second, that you take the ledger to the Bishop's court. Not to the magistrate. Not to the Crown officer. I have written to them and had no answer, and I think the answer was a hand closing in the dark.*

*Third, that if my daughter should ever return to the island, you will tell her I loved her. And that I was not brave. I was only too stubborn to be afraid.*

*Margaret.*

I read it twice.

When I finished I laid it on the desk, very carefully, as though it might bruise, and put both my hands flat on either side of it.

"She wrote that she was not brave," I said.

"Yes."

"She was the bravest person I ever knew."

"Yes."

"And she thought—" My voice cracked; I let it. "She thought it was *stubbornness.*"

"It's the same thing," Gabriel said quietly. "From the outside."

I laughed. It came out as a sob. I put my face in my hands, and he did not move, did not reach for me, did not say a word; he stood in the dim light with his arms at his sides and let me weep. And I understood that this was a courtesy, rarer than any comfort, the courtesy of being left alone in a room with someone.

At last I took a breath, and wiped my eyes with the heel of my hand.

"The third niche," I said. "The upside-down cross."

"I know the one."

"You've been in the crypt?"

"Only once. I never found the ledger. I thought it had been taken." His eyes were fixed on the letter. "She said low tide."

"The next spring tide is the fifth."

"Eight days."

"We'll go together," I said. "At night. With Mr. Blackwood's blessing or without it."

"Eleanor—"

"I know." I looked up at him. "It is dangerous. I know. You have been telling me since the first morning. I still want you to come."

His mouth moved. He said nothing.

---

I do not remember who moved first.

I have gone over it a hundred times, in the long weeks after. I would like to say it was he. I would like to say that he stepped forward and took my face in his hands and kissed me with all the long starvation of six silent winters. It would make a better story. It would make *him* a better man in my memory, a villain or a hero, anything but what he was.

But I think it was I.

I think I rose from my mother's chair with her letter still warm in my fingers, and crossed the narrow space between us, and stopped with my forehead nearly touching his collarbone. I could smell the sea on him. Lamp oil, salt, tar soap, and under it the plain clean smell of a man who has worked with his hands.

"Gabriel," I said.

He did not answer.

"Look at me."

He looked.

I have been looked at by men all my life. I have been admired, flattered, assessed. I have been undressed by eyes in London ballrooms, and found it faintly funny. But no one had ever looked at me as though I were a frightening and miraculous thing that had stepped out of a book.

"I am going to marry Adrian Ashcombe," I said. My voice trembled. "That has not changed. I have a duty and a promise. You know the situation."

"Yes."

"I don't want it to be only that."

"Eleanor—"

"I would like one thing," I said, "that I have chosen."

He closed his eyes.

His hands came up, slowly, and cupped my face. They were rough and warm, and they smelled of oil, and they shook. His thumbs moved once across my cheekbones, brushing away the last of the tears. It was the gentlest touch I have ever known. It was the touch of a surgeon for a wound.

"I'm not worthy of this," he said.

"I did not ask if you were."

"I did something terrible."

"I know."

"You'll hate me."

"Probably."

"Then—"

I rose on my toes and kissed him.

---

It is hard to say what it was like.

I have played Chopin for twenty years, and never once captured it. It was not fire. It was not a storm. It was something much quieter and much worse: a kind of recognition, as though two halves of a broken line had found each other in the dark.

His mouth was careful, at first. Asking. Then my hand went to the back of his neck and his fingers slid into my hair and the whole careful world went out like a candle. He made a sound, low in his throat, that was almost pain. He pulled me against him. I felt the hard length of him through my gown and the wild hammer of his heart against my breast, and I thought, dazedly: *so this is what everyone was talking about.*

I tasted salt.

I did not know whose tears they were.

When at last we broke apart, we stood forehead to forehead, breathing as though we had run a mile. His hands had not left my face. My fingers were knotted in his collar. Above us the old house creaked and settled; on the desk, the candle guttered in a draft from the tunnel door.

"That was a mistake," said Gabriel, hoarsely.

"Yes."

"We'll make it again."

"Probably."

"I should go."

"You should."

Neither of us moved.

---

The knock came from above.

Three raps, clean and distinct, on the green door at the top of the stairs.

We froze.

I felt the blood leave my face. Gabriel's hand closed around mine, hard. In the silence, I heard the latch lift.

"Eleanor?"

Adrian's voice, soft, almost amused. It drifted down the narrow stair like smoke.

"I know you're down there, my dear."

I did not breathe.

"Mrs. Crowe left the garden door unbarred. And there are wet footprints in the hall." A pause. A very light, very pleasant laugh. "I thought I'd bring you the Michaelmas pie. You left before dessert."

Gabriel's eyes met mine.

*He knows.*

I put my finger to my lips. I took the letter from the desk and tucked it into my bodice. I turned and, silent as a cat, pushed the iron-hinged door in the stone wall, the one bolted from the inside, and drew Gabriel in after me.

The tunnel closed behind us.

Above, I heard Adrian's boots begin to descend, slow and unhurried, one step, two, three.

In the dark, Gabriel's arm came around my waist and held me against his chest, and I thought, absurdly, *I have never been so frightened in my life, and I have never been so happy.*

The stairs creaked.

Adrian said, quite gently, to the empty room:

"I'll wait."
